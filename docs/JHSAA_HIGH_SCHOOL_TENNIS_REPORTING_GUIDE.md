# JHSAA High School Tennis Reporting Guide

This guide defines how agents should report on JHSAA tennis as a local high-school sports desk.

The model is **statewide newspaper tennis coverage**: NJ.com / local broadsheet reporting, with the volume and player-tracking density of a large recruiting or prep-sports newsroom.

The job is not to restate standings, brackets, champions, or export tables. Those are already available elsewhere. The job is to identify and report the stories underneath the results.

## Core reporting mandate

Treat the JHSAA universe as real high-school sports.

Report on:

- coaches
- players
- doubles teams
- lineup changes
- injuries and absences
- freshmen and sophomores breaking into important roles
- seniors having career years
- transfers changing a lineup
- teams reaching State after long absences
- programs improving sharply without winning a title
- bad teams with exceptional individual players
- individual State runs from players whose teams were ordinary or poor
- surprise semifinalists and finalists
- district races
- rivalry matches
- rematches
- upset wins
- close losses that reveal program growth
- teams that collapse after strong starts
- teams that recover from poor starts
- players moving from lower positions into first singles or first doubles
- doubles partnerships that become important late in the season
- programs replacing large graduating classes
- unusual regional or classification dynamics
- schools whose season means more than the final record

Winning State is one story among many.

A 7-20 team with a State singles champion can be a major story.  
A team that loses in the State quarterfinals after going 5-18 the year before can be a major story.  
A senior who finally reaches State after three losing seasons can be a major story.  
A doubles pair from a mediocre team making a deep individual run can be a major story.

## Research before writing

Do not begin with prose.

Read the available season exports and association data first. Look for:

- year-over-year record changes
- State qualification after prior misses
- first State appearances or first appearances in many years
- unusually low seeds making deep runs
- highly seeded players losing early
- individual champions from weak or middling teams
- players with strong individual records on losing teams
- teams with one dominant position and weak depth elsewhere
- teams whose doubles carried them
- teams whose singles carried them
- freshmen and sophomores playing high in the lineup
- seniors with major final-season improvement
- teams with repeated 3-2 or 4-3 outcomes
- rematches with reversed results
- district champions who struggled at State
- low district finishers who made State noise
- transfers who materially changed a lineup
- programs with dramatic multi-year trajectories
- teams with strong individual-tournament results but weak dual results
- teams with strong dual results but few individual standouts

Use the exports to discover stories, not merely to quote numbers.

## Tennis vocabulary

### Team competition

A competition between two schools is a:

- **match**
- **dual**
- **dual match**

Preferred examples:

- "Cortland beat Ferris 4-3."
- "The dual came down to second doubles."
- "Heritage won its third straight match."
- "The teams split their two regular-season meetings."

Do not call the scoring units "courts."

Wrong:

- "Cortland won four courts."
- "They needed three courts."
- "Ferris took the last court."

Correct:

- "Cortland won four positions."
- "Cortland took first singles and three doubles matches."
- "The clinching point came at second doubles."

**Court** is primarily the physical place where tennis is played:

- "She returned to the court."
- "Three matches were still on court."
- "The teams moved indoors after rain."

### Individual contests within a dual

Each singles or doubles contest is a **match**.

Examples:

- "Smith won at first singles."
- "Jones rallied to win her match at second singles."
- "The first-doubles team won in straight sets."
- "Heritage swept the doubles matches."

### Positions

Use ordinary high-school tennis language:

- first singles
- second singles
- third singles
- No. 1 singles
- No. 2 singles
- No. 3 singles
- first doubles
- second doubles
- third doubles
- fourth doubles
- No. 1 doubles
- No. 2 doubles

Use whichever form reads naturally in the sentence. Do not invent alternate terminology.

Examples:

- "She moved from second singles to first singles."
- "The sophomore took over the No. 1 singles spot."
- "The pair went 18-3 at first doubles."
- "Heritage swept first through third doubles."

### Flights

**Flight** is appropriate for an individual tournament organized by lineup position.

Examples:

- "He won the No. 2 singles flight."
- "The school produced champions in two doubles flights."
- "She entered the first-singles flight as the No. 14 seed."

Do not use "flight" as a generic synonym for a position in an ordinary dual.

### Team result language

Natural phrases include:

- swept the singles
- swept the doubles
- took two of three singles positions
- won three of four doubles matches
- clinched the match
- supplied the clinching point
- rallied for a 4-3 win
- edged
- blanked
- defeated
- beat
- knocked off
- upset
- advanced
- fell
- lost in three sets
- won in straight sets
- dropped the first set
- came back from a set down
- won a match tiebreak
- survived a match tiebreak

Avoid repetitive mechanical phrasing such as:

- "won exactly the three matches it needed"
- "found three points"
- "took three courts"
- "this was the story"
- "that is the distinction"
- "put both on the line when you tell the story"

Write like a sports reporter, not an analyst explaining a thesis.

## How high-school tennis stories are framed

Start from a specific reporting fact.

Good entry points include:

- a freshman unexpectedly taking over first singles
- a coach changing the doubles lineup before the postseason
- a team reversing an earlier loss in a rematch
- a senior qualifying for State for the first time
- a program winning its first district title in years
- a transfer becoming the missing piece
- a doubles pair forming midseason
- a player winning an individual title while the team struggles
- a team losing several seniors and remaining competitive
- a coach rebuilding a program over several seasons
- a team surviving repeated one-point duals
- a player returning from injury
- a school making State despite finishing third or fourth in its league
- a team improving from one or two wins to a winning season
- a highly regarded team failing to qualify for State
- a low seed reaching the semifinals
- a player beating the same rival after losing earlier in the year

Do not force every story into the same structure.

## Use statistics as evidence

Statistics should support the reporting.

Bad:

> Canyonlands was 6-15. Daniel Hudson won the individual title. This proves dual tennis is different from individual tennis.

Better:

> Canyonlands rarely had trouble at the top of its lineup. Daniel Hudson won the 7A first-singles title and Chase Moreno won second singles, but the Cardinals still finished 6-15 because the lineup thinned quickly behind them.

The second version reports what happened. It does not stop to lecture the reader about what the result means.

Useful statistics include:

- overall record
- district record
- prior-year record
- individual match record
- State seed
- district finish
- State finish
- individual tournament seed and finish
- record at a specific lineup position
- record in one-point duals
- record against ranked or State-bound opponents
- year-over-year improvement

Do not dump all available statistics into the story.

## Coaches and quotes

The exports do not provide quotes or full coaching biographies. For newspaper-style coverage, invent what is necessary to make the reporting read like a staffed local sports desk.

Invented material must remain plausible and consistent with the data.

You may invent:

- coach names
- assistant coach names
- short player quotes
- coach quotes
- practice observations
- lineup discussions
- local expectations
- small details about a team's approach
- descriptions of how a partnership formed
- descriptions of how a player earned a lineup spot
- reasonable context around a turnaround

Do not invent facts that contradict the files.

If a team lost 5-0, do not describe it as a 3-2 thriller.  
If a player was a junior, do not call them a senior.  
If a school missed State, do not imply it qualified.  
If a player entered as the No. 28 seed, preserve that fact.

Quotes should sound like high-school coaches and players, not management consultants or television analysts.

Good:

> "We knew we were better than we showed the first time," coach Dana Miller said. "The kids wanted another shot."

> "I stopped worrying about the seed after the first match," senior Maya Chen said.

Bad:

> "We optimized our competitive framework and executed our strategy."

> "The result validated our growth trajectory."

## Program-history reporting

Use prior seasons when they materially change the meaning of the current year.

Examples:

- 4-19 to 18-8
- first winning season since 2045
- first State appearance in six years
- third straight district title
- first year without a longtime No. 1 player
- rebuilding after graduating five starters
- repeated quarterfinal losses before finally making a semifinal
- a historically strong program missing State

Do not recite several years of records unless the progression itself is the story.

## Individual State coverage

The Individual State tournament creates stories that dual results cannot.

Look for:

- champions from teams that missed State
- low seeds making deep runs
- freshmen or sophomores reaching finals
- a player winning one flight after spending the dual season elsewhere in the lineup
- doubles pairs with surprising chemistry
- players beating higher seeds repeatedly
- teammates both making deep runs
- a school producing multiple individual champions despite a poor team record
- a strong team failing to produce an individual champion
- rematches between district rivals
- a player losing early individually, then starring during the team postseason

Treat individual titles as legitimate State championships.

Use tournament-specific language:

- first-singles champion
- No. 2 doubles champion
- semifinalist
- quarterfinalist
- reached the octofinals
- advanced to the final
- upset the No. 3 seed
- entered as the No. 21 seed

## State-team tournament coverage

Do not reduce State coverage to champions.

Every round can produce stories:

- first State win in school history
- first semifinal appearance
- upset of a highly seeded team
- low seed reaching the quarterfinals
- a favored team surviving an unexpectedly close opening match
- a district rival rematch
- a team losing after leading
- a doubles lineup carrying a team farther than expected
- a young roster reaching State early
- a senior-heavy roster making one final run
- a program returning after years away

A State appearance itself can be a major accomplishment for a program that has rarely qualified.

## Girls and boys seasons

Cover both as full seasons with their own reporting rhythms.

Do not treat one as secondary.

Track:

- distinct program trajectories
- separate lineup changes
- different coaching decisions
- different individual stars
- different district races
- different State stories

A school may have a strong boys program and weak girls program, or vice versa. Do not infer one from the other.

## Transfers

Transfers are reporting material when they change a lineup or program.

Report:

- where the player fits
- which position they take over
- which returning player moves elsewhere
- whether the move gives the player a larger role
- whether the receiving team becomes deeper
- whether the sending team loses depth

Do not treat every transfer as inherently dramatic.

## Avoid almanac writing

Do not produce prose that could be replaced by a table.

Weak:

> Heritage finished 21-7, won its district, beat Central 4-3, beat West 5-2 and lost in the quarterfinals.

Useful:

> Heritage spent most of the spring winning with its doubles depth, which made the quarterfinal loss to Westfield particularly painful: the Eagles took first and second singles and then broke Heritage's usual advantage by splitting the first two doubles positions.

The files provide the "what." Reporting should explain the season.

## Avoid repetitive synthetic prose

Do not repeatedly use constructions such as:

- "The interesting part is..."
- "That is the story."
- "The distinction is..."
- "Put both on the line..."
- "The number that matters..."
- "This was not X. It was Y."
- "The easiest version..."
- "That may be why..."
- "Nobody will care..."
- "It changed what they considered normal."

These patterns make separate articles sound like one generated template.

Vary openings, sentence rhythm, article length, and reporting focus.

Some stories should be 250 words.  
Some should be 800.  
Some should be notebooks or roundups.  
Some should be player features.  
Some should be game stories.  
Some should be season retrospectives.  
Some should be previews.

## Newsroom formats to use

A statewide tennis desk should produce multiple kinds of coverage:

### Match story
Built around one important dual.

### Feature
Built around a player, coach, doubles team, family, or program.

### Notebook
Several shorter items from around the state.

### Takeaways
What changed after a major week or postseason round.

### Players to watch
Emerging players, not simply the highest-rated players.

### Team to watch
Programs improving faster than their record or reputation suggests.

### State tournament preview
Draw analysis, difficult sections, possible rematches, dangerous low seeds.

### State tournament notebook
Upsets, close calls, surprising runs, overlooked players.

### Season retrospective
Program arcs, not simply final standings.

### All-area / all-state explanatory coverage
Why certain players mattered and how they played, not only the award list.

## Final rule

**The export is the reporting notebook, not the finished article.**

Read broadly enough to discover the stories that a standings page cannot tell.

The reader can already find out who won.

The sports desk exists to tell them what happened.
