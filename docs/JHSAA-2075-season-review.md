# JHSAA 2075 Season Review

## Executive summary

2075 is the first season in which the JHSAA's postseason architecture clearly separated into multiple classification-specific championship models rather than treating format differences as isolated exceptions.

The major findings:

- **Team State re-consolidated after the more volatile 2074 season.** 13 of 24 champions were No. 1 seeds and 23 of 24 came from the top four. The only champion outside the top four was **B.T. Washington girls in 2A from the No. 8 seed**.
- **State itself was less upset-heavy than 2074.** Lower seeds won 22.6% of State duals, down from 28.1% last season. Twelve of 24 finals were decided by one team point.
- **Only seven programs won their first State championship**, compared with 15 in 2074. Four programs swept boys and girls titles in the same championship group: **Notre Dame (9A), Walter-Kenny (4A), Bay Oregon (3A), and Pacific Friends (Group 2)**.
- **The 8A/9A move to 48 teams passed its first test.** Their four fields selected 64 at-larges; 16 won in Parastate, two reached the Round of 16, and none went farther. Most importantly, **11 TOSS top-32 teams that missed the Road were saved into State**.
- **The smaller 40-team 7A field looks better sized than the 48-team version.** Only 16 at-larges were selected across boys and girls, but six won in Parastate — the same raw number of 7A Parastate wins as 2074 from half as many at-large bids. Seven TOSS top-32 teams were rescued.
- Across all committee fields — 9A, 8A, 7A and Group 1 — **25 TOSS top-32 teams reached State only because of the at-large process**. Only one TOSS top-32 team in those eight fields was left out entirely: **Mercer City girls, No. 31 in 7A**.
- **The first Group 2 3S/3D postseason produced almost exactly the frequency predicted before the season.** Boys had 31 championship-path duals finish 3-3 out of 141 (22.0%); girls had 32 of 144 (22.2%). Every one went to the championship tiebreak procedure.
- At State specifically, **8 of 31 Group 2 duals in each gender finished 3-3 — 25.8%**. Boys had a championship tiebreak in both a quarterfinal and a semifinal. The format immediately created the distinct postseason drama it was intended to create.
- **Pacific Friends swept the inaugural Group 2 3S/3D State championships**, boys 4-2 over Lizarra and girls 5-1 over Cutbank.
- **Group 3 stayed on 1S/4D and gave no evidence that it needed the Group 2 intervention.** State one-point rates were 52.2% boys and 47.8% girls, with only 8.7% sweeps in either gender.
- The large-school 4S/5D effect settled back toward its established range: winner changes relative to the old 1S/4D subset were 14.9% in 9A, 16.0% in 8A, 11.5% in 7A and 10.6% in Group 1.
- **Individual State became slightly chalkier again:** 83 of 144 champions were No. 1 seeds and 132 were top-four seeds. Only one champion came from seed 10 or worse: **Ivan Nguyen, 3A boys No. 1 Singles, from the No. 10 seed**.
- Three freshmen won No. 1 Singles State titles: **Prince Bailey (2A boys), Penelope Gonzales (6A girls), and Leah Moore (1A girls)**.
- **Ethan Argueta won 5A No. 1 Singles again as a junior**, giving him a second S1 title after winning as a freshman in 2073 and missing the title in 2074.
- **Daphne Talbert's career took a different turn after her Group 3 S1 streak ended.** She transferred to Truman, finished No. 2 on the 4A roster, and won 4A No. 2 Singles State while teammate Isabelle Davis won No. 1 Singles. Truman finished 32-5 and State runner-up.
- The final transfer layer contained **2,205 returning-player school changes**. The original 1,852-player opportunity pass and the later stranded-talent repair behaved very differently and should continue to be treated as separate transfer markets.
- The 1,852-player opportunity pass produced a median destination rank of No. 10, 68.3% top-11 placement, 75.1% with 10+ varsity appearances, and only 1.6% with zero varsity appearances.
- Of the secondary repair slate, **353 players actually landed at the intended destination**. Every one finished top 11 and made 10+ varsity appearances; median destination rank was No. 2 and median varsity appearances were 29. The repair class was overwhelmingly source stars from bad programs, not buried reserves.
- That repair pass had visible competitive consequences. **17 of the 24 State champions received at least one repair player.** Walter-Kenny boys and West Oberlin boys each received two source stars who became the top two players on the roster, and both programs won State.
- The match engine remains stationary. Favorite-win rates by OVR gap remain essentially unchanged from 2074.

# IV. Group 2 3S/3D: the experiment worked exactly as forecast

The regular season remained 3S/4D. Group 2 switched to **3S/3D throughout the championship pathway**.

The preseason reconstruction predicted that roughly 22% of six-flight Group 2 duals would finish 3-3 and that the championship tiebreak would become a recurring institutional feature rather than a rare emergency.

That is almost exactly what happened.

| | Boys | Girls |
|---|---:|---:|
| Championship-path duals | 141 | 144 |
| 3-3 duals | **31** | **32** |
| 3-3 rate | **22.0%** | **22.2%** |
| State duals | 31 | 31 |
| State 3-3 duals | **8** | **8** |
| State 3-3 rate | **25.8%** | **25.8%** |

Every 3-3 championship dual was marked `decided_on_tiebreak`.

### State-round distribution

**Boys**
- Round of 32: 6 tiebreak duals
- Round of 16: 0
- Quarterfinals: **1**
- Semifinals: **1**
- Final: 0

**Girls**
- Round of 32: 6
- Round of 16: 2
- Quarterfinals: 0
- Semifinals: 0
- Final: 0

So the format did produce the hoped-for late-round drama: one of the two boys semifinals reached 3-3 and required the championship tiebreak.

The first champions under the format were both Pacific Friends:

- boys def. Lizarra **4-2**
- girls def. Cutbank **5-1**

Neither final needed the tiebreak.

### Why Group 3 was correctly left alone

Group 3 remained 1S/4D.

Its State profile:

- boys: **52.2%** one-point duals, **8.7%** sweeps
- girls: **47.8%** one-point duals, **8.7%** sweeps

That is a healthy five-flight postseason. The 2075 data supports the decision to use Group 2, not Group 3, as the intervention site.

# VIII. Transfer market: two different markets inside one offseason

Comparing returning player IDs between 2074 and 2075 yields **2,205 school changes**.

The important thing is that this was not one homogeneous transfer class.

## A. The original opportunity pass

The first pass contained exactly **1,852 movers**.

These were primarily current sophomores and juniors who were projected outside a useful role and were moved for playing opportunity.

Outcomes:

- median projected source rank: **No. 14**
- median 2075 destination rank: **No. 10**
- **68.3%** finished top 11
- **75.1%** made 10+ varsity appearances
- **1.6%** made zero varsity appearances
- median varsity appearances: **19**

At the player level, those are good opportunity outcomes.

The failure was program flow: too many individually defensible departures came from the same sources before their ladders were recalculated.

## B. The secondary stranded-talent repair pass

The owner then ran a separate repair pass designed around a different player population:

> good players on bad teams that were not going to become good.

The repair slate contained 367 proposed players:

- **288 were source No. 1**
- 70 were source No. 2
- 9 were source No. 3
- **78.5% were source No. 1 players**
- median source winning percentage: **.346**
- **74.7%** came from teams at .400 or worse

Of those 367, **353 appear at their intended destination in the 2075 export**.

Every one of those 353:

- finished in the destination top 11;
- made at least 10 varsity appearances.

Their median:

- destination rank: **No. 2**
- varsity appearances: **29**

This was not an opportunity-clearing class. It was a **stranded-talent market**.

## Combined transfer outcomes

| Metric | Boys | Girls | Combined |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Movers | 1,109 | 1,096 | **2,205** |
| Median projected source rank | 13 | 13 | **13** |

The overall source-rank median moves upward because the repair class deliberately included source stars.

## Grade shape

Among inferred movers:

- 996 were 2074 sophomores;
- 1,135 were 2074 juniors.

The freshman movers are almost entirely the stranded-talent repair population: their median source rank was No. 1, median destination rank No. 3, and every one made 10+ varsity appearances.

# IX. The repair pass materially changed competitive order

The repair pass was not competitively neutral.

**17 of the 24 State champions received at least one repair player.**

That does not mean every title was caused by the repair. It does mean the repair layer was deeply embedded in the championship field.

Three examples are especially clear.

## Walter-Kenny boys — 4A State champion

Repair arrivals:

- Brayden Davis — Rostova Junction, 7-18 source; 91 OVR in 2074 → **96 OVR, No. 1** at Walter-Kenny; 23 varsity appearances
- Landyn Moore — Skypark, 7-18 source; 87 OVR → **89 OVR, No. 2**; 31 varsity appearances

Walter-Kenny won 4A State at 31-2.

Davis was the No. 1 seed in 4A S1 and reached the quarterfinal. Moore was the No. 1 seed in S2 and finished runner-up.

## West Oberlin boys — first Group 1 State championship

Repair arrivals:

- Cole Edwards — Grant, 11-19 source; 91 OVR → **94 OVR, No. 1**; 32 varsity appearances
- Matias Goodson — Crater View, 10-16 source; 89 OVR → **90 OVR, No. 2**; 33 varsity appearances

West Oberlin went 29-5 and won its first Group 1 State title.

Goodson won Group 1 No. 2 Singles State.

## Truman girls — 4A State runner-up

Repair arrivals:

- Isabelle Davis — Tensas, 9-18 source; 96 OVR → **97 OVR, No. 1**
- Daphne Talbert — Rimrock, 17-20 source; 92 OVR → **95 OVR, No. 2**

Truman went 32-5 and reached the 4A final.

Davis won 4A S1; Talbert won 4A S2.

This is exactly the intended distinction between the two transfer markets:

- buried players move because they need a varsity seat;
- stranded stars move because their program cannot give their talent a competitive future.

# X. Source integrity: the lesson remains real

Even after the repair pass, raw transfer flow still shows why future agents must enforce source integrity during construction rather than after the slate is complete.

Among gender-programs:

- maximum outgoing movers from one program: **13**
- **91** programs finished with net mover losses of 4 or more
- **24** finished net -6 or worse
- **2** finished net -10 or worse

Largest final mover-flow losses:

- Walter-Kenny boys: **-11**
- Truman girls: **-10**
- West Oberlin boys: **-9**
- Caldera girls: **-8**
- Port Meridian North boys: **-8**
- Sunset Hills boys: **-8**
- Jersey City boys: **-8**
- Temescal boys: **-8**

The final rosters were not literally emptied — Walter-Kenny boys still carried 22 players, Truman girls 24, West Oberlin boys 24 — but the depth distribution was radically altered.

The season result does not negate the process error. In fact, Walter-Kenny and West Oberlin winning State after receiving elite repair players shows how consequential the repair became.

Future classes should prevent that first-wave depletion rather than rely on a second market to repair it.

# XIII. What 2075 says about the current policy experiments

## 1. 8A/9A should keep the 48-team model

The first season produced:

- 64 at-large teams;
- 16 immediate Parastate wins;
- two Round-of-16 runs;
- 11 TOSS top-32 rescues;
- no deep at-large championship incursion.

That is a good first-year balance.

## 2. 7A looks better at 40

The smaller field did not weaken Parastate.

It made the selected population more competitive:

- same six Parastate wins as 2074;
- half as many at-large bids;
- seven TOSS top-32 rescues;
- no at-large beyond the Round of 32.

The mechanism appears valuable; the 48-team field was what was oversized.

## 3. Group 2 3S/3D passed its first test

The predicted 3-3 rate was essentially exact.

The format created:

- 63 championship tiebreak events;
- 16 at State;
- a quarterfinal tiebreak;
- a semifinal tiebreak.

That is frequent enough to be a recognizable Group 2 convention without overwhelming the tournament.

## 4. Group 3 did not need the intervention

Its retained 1S/4D State tournaments were close and low-sweep.

The evidence supports the decision to leave Group 3 alone.

## 5. The transfer markets must remain separated

2075 demonstrates three distinct lessons at once:

- the ordinary opportunity class substantially increased varsity participation;
- the source-depletion bug was real;
- the stranded-talent repair class was extremely effective for individual players and materially affected championship competition.

Future agents should never collapse those three observations into one generic "transfer good players" algorithm.

# Bottom line

2075 is one of the more institutionally informative seasons in the archive.

The field-size experiments produced clean evidence:

- 48 works for 8A/9A;
- 40 looks better for 7A;
- Parastate continues to rescue genuinely strong teams without becoming a parallel championship route.

The format experiment produced even cleaner evidence:

- Group 2 3S/3D generated the exact tiebreak frequency anticipated before implementation and immediately created a distinct postseason identity;
- Group 3's retained 1S/4D structure remained healthy.

And the transfer layer produced the strongest warning:

- moving buried players for opportunity works;
- moving stranded stars from bad programs works;
- doing either without protecting source-program integrity can still distort the association.

The useful lesson is not to stop intervention. It is that the JHSAA is now mature enough to distinguish **which intervention is solving which problem**.
