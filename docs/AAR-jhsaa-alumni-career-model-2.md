# AAR — the alumni tracker's career model 2

Owner spec, 2026-11. The alumni tracker (`app/jhsaa_college.py`,
`docs/AAR-jhsaa-sim-separation-and-alumni-tracker.md`) gives each graduating
class's leading seniors a finished college career without simulating college. Its
first model produced one kind of career. The owner, looking at a real class:

> it doesn't make sense that all of them had successful careers in D1 … that
> everyone even played at all, and that if they did play that there isn't more
> variety.

This is the second model, `app/jhsaa_college_v2.py`. **It is switched on**
(`jhsaa_college.ACTIVE_MODEL` is 2) — owner, after seeing the measured
distribution: *"turn on the new model, i don't care about past classes."* Every
class with no rows yet is written with it. A class already stored keeps whatever
wrote it; nothing regenerates one. The interface has not been touched.

The full measurements and ten example careers per gender are in
`docs/reports/REPORT-jhsaa-alumni-career-model-2-validation.md`, generated by
`scripts/jhsaa_college_validate.py`.

## Why model 1 produced one career

The cause was specific, and it was in the placement, not in the missing features.
Model 1 ranked a class 1-200, dealt it into fixed division shares, and matched each
senior to the program at the same relative standing. The career then ran on

```
fit = (standing - strength) + noise
```

where `standing` is the senior's place among Jefferson recruits in that division
and `strength` the program's place in the division. Placement set those two equal
on purpose, so `fit` was noise around zero for everybody: nearly every recruit
started mid-lineup and climbed a spot or two a year. The record came mostly from
the program's prestige. Injuries, transfers and honours added on top of that would
have decorated the same career.

Measured on eight classes per gender: under model 1, **100%** played all four
seasons, **0%** transferred, **0%** never started, **99%** of lineups moved up,
**0.1%** moved down, and every class sent exactly 200 seniors in the same
62/44/42/52 division split whatever its strength.

## What model 2 is

The architecture is unchanged: deterministic, generated once from a seed keyed on
the pid, persisted whole, no college season simulated, no JHSAA season or roster
read by the model. What changed is what is inside.

1. **A snapshot at graduation** (`jhsaa_college.snapshot_of`,
   `world_jhsaa_grad_snapshot`). The rung captures, for the class's top 320 seniors
   by exit rating: exit rating, estimated and true ceiling, the senior-year singles
   and doubles records and the flight played most (off `TeamSeason.matches`),
   matches and postseason matches, honours, style and trait, net and serve relative
   to the rest of the player, academic rating, nationality, hometown, the school's
   stable identity (`School.ident`) and its class, area and county. Captured
   whichever model is active, so a class that graduates under model 1 still has its
   full inputs on record.
2. **Level, not rank.** A senior's exit rating is read against the pooled leading
   seniors of the five classes before theirs, and that standing is mapped onto the
   college game's own recruit distribution (`ncaa._talent_mean(0.5, "D2")`,
   `world.RECRUIT_TALENT_SD`, through `development.compress_talent`). A deeper
   class sends more players further.
3. **A destination the player chooses.** A program recruits a senior it reads as
   within reach of its own roster — D1 on the projection, D2 on today's level,
   D3/D4 on both, the college game's radar. Among those, the senior weighs
   prestige, the level of tennis, the role they expect as a junior, academics and
   geography, by one of four aspirations. D4's academic gate
   (`recruit_economy.d4_academic_min`) and the service-academy rule
   (`ncaa.us_only_program`) are hard gates. There is no division quota.
4. **A depth chart per program per season** (`Context.seats`), generated from the
   program's strength and roster cap on a seed that does not depend on the player.
   A seat is (program, entry cohort, index), so a teammate who is a freshman one
   year is a sophomore the next, and two alumni at one college meet the same team.
   Alumni already written are part of the team a later class walks into, and a
   singles position already on the record is theirs.
5. **A career through it, one season at a time.** Development follows one of five
   shapes (steady, early, late, stalled, regress — the last goes backwards). Each
   season the lineup position is where the player's level falls on that chart,
   singles and doubles separately; each match is played against the opposing
   program's player at the same position. Injuries cost matches and ground. After
   each season the player stays, transfers (down for a place to play, or up after a
   big year), stops playing while staying enrolled, leaves college, or — a D1
   All-American — turns professional.
6. **Honours against the field.** All-Conference and All-American, singles and
   doubles, by reaching the level of the Nth best No. 1/No. 2 player (or No. 1
   pair) across the conference's or the division's depth charts that season, with a
   winning record required.

The career type on each row (`arc`: immediate star, steady contributor, late
developer, reserve, doubles specialist, stalled prospect, transfer success, injury
affected, early departure, turned pro) is **read off what happened**, never assigned
in advance.

## What it produces

Eight classes per gender, the same classes through both models. The seniors are
real: one year of real Jefferson rosters, resampled into classes with small
strength shifts.

| | model 1 | model 2 girls | model 2 boys |
|---|---|---|---|
| careers per class | 200, always | 169–260 | 173–225 |
| first college D1 | 31% | 21% | 19% |
| singles starter as a freshman | 91% | 41% | 38% |
| still playing as a senior | 100% | 80% | 78% |
| never a singles starter | 0% | 19% | 19% |
| never in any lineup | 0% | 17% | 17% |
| D1 signees never a singles starter | 0% | 13% | 15% |
| reached No. 1 singles | 23% | 20% | 21% |
| transferred | 0% | 15% | 16% |
| left the team, stayed enrolled | 0% | 12% | 14% |
| left college | 0% | 7% | 8% |
| career singles record under .500 | 33% | 37% | 35% |
| lineup moved DOWN over the career | 0.1% | 9% | 8% |
| signed in Jefferson | 10% | 7% | 7% |
| any honour | 9% | 22% | 22% |
| D1 All-American careers, per class | — | 0.9 | 0.6 |

These are the figures of the model as switched on (after the D1 All-American fix
below). Class strength moves the class: a class 1.2 points stronger than the
reference sent 260 girls on (58 to D1); one 1.2 points weaker sent 169 (38 to D1).

Integrity, both genders: a rebuild is identical; career totals equal the season
sums; every college is a real program; no service academy holds a non-American;
every D4 signee clears that program's academic gate; of ~8,200 singles lineup
seasons, none claims a position another alumnus holds at the same college in the
same season; every All-American season is also an All-Conference season in the
same discipline. Cost: about 1.4 seconds a class.

## Five things the measurements showed that the design did not

These are why the model was measured before it was switched on.

1. **Compare players and programs on one scale.** The college game squashes every
   drawn talent above a knee (`compress_talent`) — recruits and rosters alike. The
   first build compared compressed recruits against RAW program means: no senior
   cleared any D1 program's bar, and each class signed zero or one D1 player.
2. **The high-school gap is a ranking, not a quantity.** The best seniors sit close
   to their high-school ceiling (median 7 points for the top 200, against 17 for the
   whole class). Read literally, staffs projected a median 2.7 points of college
   growth where a teammate gets 4.3, and every Jefferson recruit looked finished.
   College growth is now the college game's own (`_CLASS_MATURITY`), and the
   high-school headroom decides where in that range a senior falls.
3. **Playing time has to saturate.** With "higher on the ladder is always better",
   seniors chose the weakest program that would take them — 150 of 244 went to D3
   and over 100 a class became All-Americans. Being a sure starter is now worth as
   much as being the No. 1, and the level of tennis has its own weight.
4. **A depth chart has to be the size of the roster.** A cohort's size was keyed on
   `cohort + age`, which is the season: every cohort got the same size and a
   ten-man D2 roster came out as eight or twelve. A test on roster size found it.
5. **The home pull was three times too strong.** 28% signed in-state against the
   project's rule that Jefferson's kids mostly leave; it is 7–8% now, about what
   model 1 produced.

## Owner decisions (2026-11) and what they measured at

The four open questions were answered, and one rule was added.

1. **D1 honours — keep the All-American standard, fix All-Conference.** Zero
   All-Conference singles in ~450 D1 careers was too restrictive. D1 All-Conference
   is now deeper (14 singles, 7 doubles teams — a first and second team) and
   reaches the No. 4 singles position and the No. 2 pair (`ALL_CONFERENCE`). The
   cause of the zero was structural: a Jefferson recruit is an average core player
   at the program that signs them and only reached the top two on a weak team,
   where the record was not there. **Measured: 9.0% of girls' and 7.6% of boys' D1
   signees earn All-Conference singles at some point** (8.1% and 6.8% in doubles),
   about one a class for the girls. D1 All-American is unchanged and still did not
   occur in these classes.
2. **The gender gap in D1 signings stays.** The men's and women's talent tables are
   intentionally different; nothing was changed.
3. **Attrition — keep the probabilities, keep the endings apart.** No rate was
   changed. A row now carries `departure` (`kind`, `cause`, the class year it
   followed, the college, the role), and the career type is named for how it ended.
   A transfer is a move and is never one of them.

   | how a career ended | girls | boys | causes recorded |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | left the team, stayed enrolled | 13.1% | 11.4% | no role · doubles only · injury |
   | left college | 7.6% | 7.1% | personal |
   | injury ended it | 1.2% | 0.9% | injury |
   | transferred (a move, not a departure) | 13.9% | 14.6% | opportunity · up |

4. **Lower-division honours — keep All-Conference, more All-Americans.** D2, D3
   and D4 All-Conference are untouched (15–20 singles a class in each of D2 and
   D3). Their All-American lists are twice as deep (40 singles, 20 doubles teams,
   `ALL_AMERICAN`). All-American seasons a class went from about 12.5 to 20.6
   (girls) and 13.2 to 18.9 (boys); 8.1% of girls' and 6.6% of boys' careers with a
   lower-division season include one. **Of D1-level players starting in a lower
   division, 21.9% (girls) and 9.7% (boys) became All-Americans there.** The boys'
   figure is lower because the men's top D2 programs are themselves D1-level in the
   college game's tables, so a D1-level man is not a standout there.
5. **All-Conference comes first, at every level.** A player is only considered for
   All-American in a season they made All-Conference in the same discipline. It is
   a gate in the code, not a coincidence of two thresholds: the conference line and
   the division line are built from different fields, so without it a player in a
   strong conference could clear the national line and miss their own. The
   validation asserts it on every season it generates.

The figures in items 1, 3 and 4 are from before the fix below; the current ones
are in the next section and in the table above.

## D1 All-American was 0%, and why (owner, 2026-11: "0% makes no sense")

It was not a strict standard doing its job. Two things in the model made it
impossible, and neither was the All-American list.

1. **The best seniors were the slowest developers in college.** College growth is
   ranked by high-school headroom (ceiling minus exit rating). The ranking was
   taken across the whole class, and the best seniors are the ones nearest their
   high-school ceiling — so all of them landed at the bottom and got the least
   college growth. Measured: 18 top-two singles seasons in 310 girls' D1 careers,
   the best of them 2.7 points under the national line; the boys' best was 3.9
   under. Headroom is now ranked among the 20 seniors rated either side
   (`HEADROOM_WINDOW`): "more room than players like me". After: 64 top-two
   seasons (girls), the best 2.0 over the line.
2. **The record bar was one the field itself could not clear.** An All-American
   had to win 65% at least, against a par of 68%. Those numbers came from the
   lower divisions, where the best players stand clear of their opposition. In D1
   the top of the division is squashed against the talent cap and plays itself:
   the 20 players who define the All-American line win a measured median **57%
   (women) and 59% (men)** at their own position on this model's schedule. The
   country's 20th-best player could not have been named. D1's national honour is
   now read against D1's own records (`NATIONAL_RECORD`: par .58, at least .55),
   with results weighted 12 rather than 6 — a D1 All-American is a ranking honour,
   decided on results, and at 6 a player one point under the line needed a record
   about 2.2 standard deviations better than their level predicts.

Not changed: the list (20 singles, 10 doubles teams, the top two singles positions
and the No. 1 pair), the All-Conference-first gate, and every lower-division rule
and constant.

**Measured after, 8 classes a gender: 7 girls' careers and 5 boys' include a D1
All-American season — 0.9 and 0.6 a class.** For scale: Jefferson supplies about
4% of the country's D1 freshmen in this model (45 girls and 38 boys a class,
against 383 programs turning over about 1,150), and something like 15 careers in a
national class include an All-American season — an estimate, since the field's
results are not generated. A proportional share is therefore roughly one career
every two classes; the measured rate is somewhat above that for the girls and
close to it for the boys.

Fixing the growth ranking moved other numbers, because it was wrong for every
strong senior and not only for future All-Americans:

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| first college D1, girls / boys | 18% / 8% | 21% / 19% |
| D1 signees earning All-Conference singles | 9.0% / 7.6% | 15.1% / 14.1% |
| D1-level players in a lower division who became All-Americans there | 21.9% / 9.7% | 28.0% / 25.0% |
| careers with a lower-division season that include an All-American one | 8.1% / 6.6% | 8.5% / 8.6% |
| transferred | 13.9% / 14.6% | 14.5% / 16.0% |
| left the team · left college | 13.1% · 7.6% / 11.4% · 7.1% | 12.4% · 6.5% / 13.6% · 7.5% |

**The gender gap in D1 signings mostly closed**, and nothing gender-specific was
touched. D1 staffs recruit on the projection; with the top seniors read as
finished products, the men's higher D1 bar shut almost all of them out. The
talent tables still differ and the rates still follow from them.

Still open, and worth a look once real classes exist:

- **Class size swings with strength** (169 to 260 across ±1.2 rating points). Real
  classes may not differ that much.
- **Nobody turned professional** in these classes: the D1 All-American seasons
  were mostly senior years.

## Opportunity transfers have to buy a bigger role (owner spec, 2026-11)

A player who left for playing time only had to project into the destination's
lineup — its last singles position was enough. The spec: evaluate the projected
change in role before approving the move, require a meaningful improvement, and
let the role come from the player's level against the receiving program.

- **One reading of a depth chart** (`_standing`). The season is played from it
  and a transfer is judged on it (`project_role`: singles position, doubles
  pair, expected starts, expected matches), so a projected role and the role
  then played are the same calculation.
- **Staying is projected too.** The destination is compared with what the
  player's own program projects for next season, both on today's level.
- **What counts as meaningful** (`OPPORTUNITY`, `meaningful`):
  a reserve needs a clear singles place (two or more positions above the last)
  and 12 more matches than staying; a doubles-only player needs that, or the
  No. 1 pair when staying would not give it; a lower-lineup starter needs a
  top-three singles position, at least two higher than staying, and no fewer
  matches.
- **The choice is made among programs that pass**, weighing position (a higher
  place is worth more to someone leaving for a role), level of tennis, academics
  and geography as before. They now look much further down than a recruit does
  (`LOOK_DOWN_TRANSFER`), which is what lets a D1 reserve reach a D3 program.
- **No program passes → no transfer.** The player stays and meets next season's
  decisions like anybody else.
- **Lower-lineup starters can now leave** (`LOW_LINEUP_TRANSFER`, 5% / 5% / 2% by
  class year). They never did before, so this adds transfers.
- **Upward moves stay separate.** A player stepping up a division accepts any
  place in the lineup; inside a division an upward move is still to a top-four
  position. No improvement test applies.
- **Every transfer records what it bought**: the season before (`before`), what
  staying projected (`stay`), what the destination projected (`projected`), the
  season after as played (`played`), the change in matches, starts, singles
  position and doubles pair (`change`), and `improved`.

One run on twelve synthetic classes (2,298 careers, 422 transfers with a season
after them) — synthetic seniors, so the rates are not the real association's:

| who moved | n | played more after | matches before → after | singles position after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| reserve, for opportunity | 343 | 99.1% | 3 → 41 | median No. 2; 246 in the top three |
| doubles only, for opportunity | 9 | 100% | 24 → 45 | all 9 gained a singles place |
| lower-lineup starter, for opportunity | 54 | 72% | 30 → 42 | median No. 1; 49 in the top three |
| starter, moving up | 16 | 25% | 45 → 38 | median No. 3 |

All 73 D1 reserves who went to D2 played singles the next season, as did all 8
lower-lineup D1 starters who went to D2 (positions 1 and 2). The sample had one
D1-reserve-to-D3 move and one D2-to-D1 move (a No. 5).

Not done: the four regression tests the spec asked for were not written, and
nothing else was run, at the owner's instruction to stop testing. The run above
is the only execution of this code.

## The pages (owner, 2026-11)

> fix the page issues with alumni who have multiple schools, year by year lists
> and pagination instead of a 200 sort list

The page had been one table of a whole class reading a new-model career through
the first model's keys: one college, singles only.

How real trackers organise this was looked up first. They do it three ways: a
commitment list per class year (TennisRecruiting), a page per college with its
commitments by year (the same site), and a list per high school (the AJC's lists
of Georgia players on college rosters, grouped under their high schools). The
page has those three as views of one body of careers, plus every class at once.

- **`/jhsaa/alumni`** — four views: the class of the season in the scope bar, all
  classes, by college, by high school. Every list is 25 a page. A search box
  (player, high school or college), a division filter and a career filter
  (played four seasons, transferred, left the team, left college, injury ended
  it, turned pro, any honour, All-American). Every column heading sorts.
- **A transfer is one career that names every college**, in order, and is on each
  of those colleges' lists. The division filter means "played in it at any
  point".
- **Season by season, one click down.** Each career opens to its year-by-year
  lines: which college, singles position and record, doubles position and
  record, honours, and whether the season was as a reserve, doubles only, or
  lost to injury. One button opens every career on the page.
- **A program's College tab** lists its own graduates, newest class first.
- **The player page's College panel** shows every college, both records, how the
  career ended, and the same season lines.

How it is built:

- **Lists run in SQL, never over parsed careers.** A career is a JSON blob of a
  few kilobytes. The first page parsed a class; an all-classes view built the
  same way would parse every career the save has ever written on each click. The
  career table now carries summary columns (`_SUMMARY_COLS`: status, seasons,
  peaks, records, honours, whether they moved), and
  `world_jhsaa_college_stint` holds one row per college a career passed through.
  Filters, sorts and counts read those; only the 25 careers on screen are parsed.
  Measured on four classes (812 careers): about 50 ms a page.
- **Both are derived from the stored career** (`summarise_pending`), versioned
  (`SUMMARY_VERSION`), and rebuilt from it on the next read when missing or
  stale. The career itself is never touched. Classes written before this are
  summarised the first time a list is opened.
- **Only the Class view fills in an old class.** The all-classes, college and
  high-school views show what is written; opening them never builds a class.

Checked by rendering all four views, the program tab and the player panel
against four real classes (one written by the first model) and looking at
screenshots; no page scrolls sideways at desktop or phone width.

### Reviewed against a UI/UX rule set

The owner pointed at a public UI/UX checklist (accessibility, touch targets,
responsive layout, data tables). The pages were measured against it in a real
browser, and five things failed:

| rule | measured | now |
|---|---|---|
| body text contrast 4.5:1 | division, conference and season notes at 3.7–3.9:1 (`--text-faint`) | 5.2:1 on white, 10.5:1 on the tinted season rows |
| target size 24×24 (44×44 for touch) | expand button 21×24, the "#" sort link 20×18 | 28×28 and 27×24; 44×44 on a touch screen |
| a sortable table states its sort | no `aria-sort` | on the sorted heading |
| controls have names | expand button and search box had none | `aria-label` on both |
| visible keyboard focus | none on the page's own controls | a 2px ring on every one |

And one layout rule: no sideways scroll on a phone. The table carries a 980px
minimum, so on a phone it scrolled inside its wrapper with the name on one screen
and the record on the next. Below 720px each career is now a block — name,
high school, colleges, then the numbers each with its own label — and the sort
is offered as a menu, since there are no column headings left to press.

One trap from that: the season lines are a table INSIDE the career table, so
phone rules written as `table.jh-alum tbody tr` restyled them too and the
seasons came out as unlabelled fragments. The outer rules use direct-child
selectors and the season lines have their own.

The checklist's own scripts were not installed or run; it was used as a list of
things to measure.

## Not done

- **Classes are tracked from when they are written.** The all-classes, college
  and high-school views cover the classes that have careers; an older class
  joins them when its own Class view is first opened. There is no button that
  fills every past class at once.
- **No historical regeneration.** Existing careers stay as written. Rewriting them
  is a separate, explicitly authorised migration and no code here does it.
- **The validation plays no season**, so the senior-year record inputs are covered
  by unit tests, not by the report.
- **Program-year context is roster-level only.** A program's strength does not move
  from year to year, and its record is not generated.
- The research export carries the new columns.
- **No test was run after the D1 All-American fix or the switch** (owner: no more
  test runs). `tests/test_jhsaa_college.py` and one test in
  `tests/test_jhsaa_college_v2.py` were edited to pin model 1 where they cover
  it; those edits are unexecuted. The validation script ran clean, including its
  integrity assertions.
