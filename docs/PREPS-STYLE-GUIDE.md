# JHSAA Preps Style Guide: Angles, Leads, Headlines and Structure

Read this with `docs/JHSAA_HIGH_SCHOOL_TENNIS_REPORTING_GUIDE.md` and `docs/DESIGN-story-forms-from-sports-journalism.md`. Those two decide what a story may claim and what vocabulary it uses. This document decides how each story is approached, opened, headlined, built and ended, so that a season review reads like a staffed prep desk and not like one template run twelve times.

Every rule here was written because agents kept doing the opposite. Treat the rules as requirements.

## 1. Hard rules that override everything below

1. **Never call a lineup a "card."** Write lineup, lineup order, or the positions by name. This also applies to headlines and quotes.
2. **Never call a scoring unit a "court."** A team wins positions or matches: "took first singles," "swept the doubles." Court is the physical place: "three matches were still on court."
3. **"Flight" belongs to the individual tournament only.** In a dual it is a position.
4. **Do not contradict the record.** Scores, seeds, grades, finishes, dates and names come from the files and are fixed. Invent coaches' words, scenes, weather, crowds and reasons freely, as the reporting guide allows.
5. **No framework talk in the body.** No form names, table names, column names, "the export," or "the data does not say." Sourcing is one line at the end of the whole document.
6. **Do not reuse a previous season's review.** Its stories, headlines, openings and quotes are spent. Each season gets fresh reporting from its own export.

### Corrections to the draft rulebook this guide replaces

The owner's first draft of these rules had examples that break the repo. Do not copy them:
* "Lineup card" and "court five" break rules 1 and 2.
* "Court friction" and "ladder order" are not tennis language; use lineup order.
* Headline examples with judgment adjectives ("bitter") break the desk rule against adjectives in a headline.
* One example gave a third singles score of 1-6 where the record says 2-6. Every example score must match the files.

## 2. Choosing the angle

Numbers are the easiest thing in an export to grab, so agents lead with seeds, rankings and records. Beat writers almost never do. They lead with a tactical choice, a lineup problem, a person, or a moment, and use the numbers as evidence underneath.

Pick each story's angle from this order. Take the first one the material genuinely supports.

1. **The tactical paradox.** The result contradicts itself: a team swept singles and lost every doubles match; a champion lost at first singles; a team won the dual while its best player lost.
2. **The lineup decision.** A coach moved a player, split a pair, promoted a freshman, or sat a senior, and the result can be traced to it.
3. **The depth player.** The fourth doubles pair or third singles player who supplied the clinching point while the headliners lost.
4. **Roster movement.** Transfers arriving or leaving and what they did to both lineups: who moved down, who lost a spot, which program got thinner.
5. **The close finish.** Third sets, match tiebreaks, a dual decided by one position, a team that lost three matches after winning the first set.
6. **Seeds and bracket position, last.** Use a seed as the angle only when the gap is historic, such as a 28 seed beating a 1 seed. Otherwise a seed is one clause of background, placed after the lead paragraphs.

Before any of this, apply the consequence gate from the reporting guide. The event has to have changed a title, a berth, a seed, a road, a streak, or how a program or player is judged. A good angle on a low stakes event does not earn a section.

### History: only three triggers

By default, write only about the current season: the matches, the tactics and the roster movement. Bring in school history only when one of these is true:

1. **The fallen power.** A traditionally strong program rebounds from a recent low. For example, a program that went 5-21 last year returns to its usual standard.
2. **The drought breaker.** A team wins a title or reaches a final for the first time in ten or more years.
3. **The dynasty.** A program extends a multiyear streak or defends a title.

When a trigger is met, frame the history in one sentence. When none is met, leave history out entirely.

## 3. Openings: match the lead to the story

A season review needs several lead types, and no two consecutive sections may open the same way. These are the standard prep and feature leads, with what each one is for:

| Lead type | What it does | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Straight news | Who won, how, and the key performer, in one sentence | A clean title win where the result itself is the news |
| Delayed | Opens on a person or moment, reaches the result in the second or third paragraph | The meaning sits in one decision or one match |
| Scene | Places the reader somewhere: the bench, the parking lot, the changeover | A final or a clinching match with a clear physical moment |
| Quote | Opens on a coach or player's words | The words carry the conflict better than a description can |
| Contrast | Two facts that pull against each other | The tactical paradox angle |
| Single item | One number or one detail that stands for the whole story | A streak, a drought, one decisive set score |
| Aftermath | Opens after the event, on how it is still being discussed | A loss that ended a season or changed a program's view of itself |

Lead rules, taken from how sports writing is taught:
* **Put the score in the first few paragraphs, not necessarily the first sentence.** A reader should never reach paragraph four without knowing the result.
* **Do not narrate from the start of the match.** Begin at the point that decided it, usually late, and go back only as far as needed.
* **No generalizations.** Nothing about how close matches are decided by small margins or how seasons have ups and downs. If the sentence could open any story, cut it.
* **No long introductory clauses.** The main action goes at the front of the sentence.
* **Do not lead with background, place or date.** "Heritage played at Westfield on Tuesday" tells the reader nothing.
* **Do not lead with records.** A record goes in parentheses or a later sentence.
* **No "hope" or "look to" in previews.** Say how the team plans to win.

### The nut graf

After a delayed, scene, quote or aftermath lead, the second or third paragraph states plainly what the story is about and why it matters now: the berth, the title, the streak, the reassessment. A nut graf that could be swapped into another story is too vague. Rewrite it with this season's facts.

## 4. Headlines

A headline tells the reader what happened and makes them want the story. Newspaper conventions apply:

* Present tense for past events ("Westfield takes 5A title").
* Active voice, with a subject and a verb.
* No period at the end.
* Short, specific words. Use names, positions and numbers over abstractions.
* No puns by default. A pun is allowed only when it is exact and could not be read as mocking a teenager.
* No judgment adjectives ("stunning," "bitter," "dominant"). Let the verb and the facts do the work.
* News heads play it straight. Feature heads may tease the angle, but they must not misstate it.

### Headline archetypes

Every section in one review uses a different archetype, and consecutive reviews must not open with the same one.

| Archetype | Shape | Example (invented schools) |
|---|---|---|
| Result | Subject, verb, object, maybe the score | Heritage edges Westfield 4-3 for first 6A title |
| Paradox | Two facts set against each other | Four singles wins, no doubles points, no State trip |
| Decision | Names the choice | Coach moves No. 1 singles to doubles for the final |
| Voice | A short quote | "I'd make the same call again," Larsen says |
| Person | A player and what they did | Freshman at third singles supplies the clinching point |
| Place or time | A moment that anchors the story | The third set that ended at sundown in Cortland |
| Streak or drought | A number tied to history, used only under a history trigger | Ferris wins its first title since 2071 |

A headline must not repeat the lead sentence word for word. Choose key words from the story, not from its first line.

## 5. Formats, and how the approach changes with each

| Format | Length | Structure | Default lead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Match story | 400 to 700 words | Decisive moment, score, what it decided, key positions, quotes placed with the moments they explain, what comes next | Straight news or scene |
| Feature | 600 to 1,000 words | Delayed lead, nut graf, scenes and quotes alternating with context, kicker | Delayed or scene |
| Notebook | 3 to 8 items, deliberately unequal lengths (60 to 300 words each) | Each item has its own bolded name lead, and none depends on another | Varies per item |
| Top stories countdown | 5 to 10 entries, counting down to the most consequential | Each entry is a short story with its own angle | Varies per entry |
| Player of the year | 500 to 800 words | Why this player mattered and how they played, not a list of wins | Scene or quote |
| Takeaways | 4 to 6 numbered points | One claim each, one piece of evidence each | Straight |
| Season retrospective | Several sections of unequal length | Program arcs, chosen for consequence, with an angle rotation across sections | Varies per section |

The format solves a selection problem. When the season has several unrelated stories that matter, use a notebook or a countdown instead of forcing them under one thesis.

### Angle rotation in multisection pieces

No two consecutive sections may use the same angle from section 2 or the same lead type from section 3. One way to plan a five section review:

1. Tactical paradox, contrast lead
2. Lineup decision, scene lead
3. Close finish, delayed lead
4. Roster movement, quote lead
5. Notebook of individual items, each with a different opening

Write the plan down before drafting, and check the finished piece against it.

## 6. Paragraph to paragraph

Facts dumped one per sentence read as a log. Each paragraph must connect to the one before it through one of these links:

* **Cause:** the previous paragraph explains why this one happened.
* **Contrast:** this paragraph pulls against the previous one.
* **Consequence:** this paragraph is what the previous one led to.

Quotes:
* Put a quote right after the moment it explains. Never stack quotes at the end.
* Attribute after the first sentence of a quote, not after the last.
* Do not introduce every quote with a setup sentence that says what the quote will say.
* The best quote goes early. The second best can end the story.
* Quotes come from both sides of a result when the losing side has something to say.

Commentary:
* Cut words that grade the facts for the reader ("an abysmal 1-6," "a remarkable run"). The numbers already say it.
* Every number attaches to a cause, a consequence or a comparison, as the design doc requires.

Vocabulary: use what coaches and beat writers use. That means lineup, lineup order, changeover, split sets, third set, match tiebreak, dual, clinching point, first singles, No. 2 doubles. It never means "card," and never "courts" as a count of points won.

Banned phrases, on top of the lists in the reporting guide:
* testament to
* beacon of hope
* proved their mettle
* stark contrast
* navigated the waters
* defied the odds
* from the get go
* passed the test
* gutsy
* avenged
* when all was said and done

## 7. Endings

The last paragraph must tell the reader something new. It should be one of:
* a concrete scene,
* a quote,
* the next match or the next season's fixed fact, such as who returns and who graduates.

Never end on a summary, a lesson, or a sentence about what the season meant. If the closing line could have been written before the season, cut it and end on the last concrete fact.

## 8. Checklist before a review ships

* Does every section pass the consequence gate?
* Is any section led by a seed, a ranking or a record without a historic reason?
* Do two consecutive sections share an angle or a lead type?
* Do two headlines share an archetype?
* Does any headline carry a judgment adjective or a pun?
* Is the score within the first few paragraphs of every match story?
* Is history used without one of the three triggers, or for more than one sentence?
* Do "card," "courts" as a count, or "flight" in a dual appear anywhere?
* Is there a quote, a scene or a counterfactual in every section?
* Are quotes placed with their moments, attributed after the first sentence?
* Does any invented detail contradict the record?
* Does any section reuse a story, opening or quote from a previous season's review?
* Does any closing sentence summarize or zoom out?
