# JHSAA 2078–2080 Longitudinal Review

**Status:** reference report for postseason architecture, transfer-market evolution, program trajectories, and talent-distribution analysis across the 2078, 2079, and 2080 research exports.

This report exists so future agents do not reason from one season in isolation. The three-season window is especially useful because it spans:

- the 2078 936-player transfer intervention;
- the 2079 952-player transfer intervention;
- the 2080 900-player transfer intervention;
- the maturation of role-liberation, feeder, reload, and weak-program rebuild mechanisms;
- the Group 2 expansion from 32 to 40 teams;
- the Group 3 expansion from 28 to 32 teams;
- continued differentiated postseason formats across classifications.

The central finding is that the JHSAA has reached a relatively mature competitive equilibrium. State is getting closer and somewhat more upset-prone without becoming random. Upper classifications have compressed materially in roster strength. The transfer system has also revealed which interventions produce durable value and which are showing diminishing returns.

---

## 1. State volatility increased without destroying hierarchy

| Metric | 2078 | 2079 | 2080 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Lower-seed State wins | 31.9% | 32.8% | **33.4%** |
| One-point State duals | 39.5% | 44.6% | **45.4%** |
| No. 1 seeds winning State | 12/24 | 7/24 | 7/24 |
| Top-four seeds winning State | **18/24** | **18/24** | **18/24** |
| First-time champions | 6 | 7 | **8** |
| Deepest champion seed | 15 | 12 | **29** |

The most important stability signal is that exactly **18 of 24 champions came from the top four in all three seasons**.

What changed was not the size of the elite tier. What changed was dominance within it. No. 1 seeds became much less automatic, while the top four remained strongly predictive.

2080's most extreme championship run was Hagerstown boys winning Group 1 from the No. 29 seed. That is a tail event, not evidence that seeding has lost meaning: median champion seed remained 3.

The correct interpretation is:

> **The top four matter enormously. No. 1 does not own the tournament.**

Repeat champions remained rare enough to prevent ossification, while durable dynasties still exist. Pacific Friends girls won Group 2 in all three seasons and reached 23 total State titles.

## 2. Upper-classification roster strength compressed

Top-11 OVR dispersion declined in the classifications where transfer/rebuild policy has been most active.

| Class | 2078 | 2079 | 2080 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 9A | 5.48 | 5.13 | **4.97** |
| 8A | 5.33 | 4.58 | **4.43** |
| 7A | 6.35 | 5.16 | **5.05** |
| 6A | 4.67 | 4.54 | 4.85 |
| Group 1 | 4.81 | 4.47 | 4.73 |

Winning-percentage dispersion moved similarly:

- 8A: .190 → .187 → **.174**
- 7A: .196 → .195 → **.183**
- 9A: remained roughly stable near .185

This is not league-wide artificial parity. Several lower classifications became slightly more dispersed in 2080.

The more defensible reading is that the transfer/rebuild system has thickened the middle of the deepest classifications while preserving distinct classification-level talent bands.

## 3. The differentiated postseason formats now have recognizable statistical identities

### 1S/4D middle classifications

The 1S/4D classes continue to produce knife-edge championship tennis.

2080 one-point State rates:

- 6A: 56%
- 5A: 50%
- 4A: 54%
- 3A: 56%
- 2A: 50%

These classifications remain the natural environment for deep-seed runs because five-point duals let concentrated, well-shaped rosters defeat teams with stronger aggregate depth.

### Wide-card classifications

One-point State rates:

| | 2078 | 2079 | 2080 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 9A | 34% | 35% | **44%** |
| 8A | 34% | 39% | **50%** |
| 7A | 30% | 38% | 33% |
| Group 1 | 39% | 45% | **50%** |

The wide cards have not stopped rewarding depth. Rather, the top of those classifications has compressed enough that deep-roster tennis itself is becoming closer.

### Group 2

Special 3–3 State deciders:

- 2078: 14.5%
- 2079: 17.7%
- 2080: **20.5%**

The 3S/3D decider is now a stable characteristic of Group 2 rather than an experimental curiosity.

## 4. Group 2 field expansion is validated

The old 32-team Group 2 field was excluding teams whose computer-consensus quality clearly belonged inside State.

Best omitted consensus ranks before expansion included:

- 2078 boys: approximately No. 19
- 2079 girls: approximately No. 22
- 2079 boys: approximately No. 28

The move to **40 teams in 2080** pushed the first omitted teams to approximately:

- boys: No. 39
- girls: No. 38

That is a major improvement.

The added teams were competitive rather than ornamental:

- 10 of 16 Group 2 at-larges won at least one State match
- validation rate: **62.5%**

The champions remained No. 1 seeds, so the expansion corrected selection without obviously distorting the title layer.

**Recommendation:** keep Group 2 at 40.

---

## 5. Group 3 expansion remains unresolved

The old 28-team Group 3 field had a legitimate omission problem. In 2079 a girls team around consensus No. 26 missed the tournament.

The move to 32 solved the cut-line problem:

- 2080 best omitted boys team: about No. 33.8
- 2080 best omitted girls team: about No. 33.1

But the new entrants were not competitive.

| Season | Group 3 bids | Won ≥1 | Rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2078 | 8 | 5 | 62.5% |
| 2079 | 8 | 6 | **75.0%** |
| 2080 | 16 | **2** | **12.5%** |

In 2080:

- seeds 29–32 went 0–8;
- the 16 at-large teams produced only two total wins;
- lower-seed State win rate fell from 44.4% → 42.6% → **17.7%**.

This creates a real policy tension:

- 32 teams improves selection coverage;
- 28 teams may preserve competitive density better.

A useful next experiment is a **28-team / 8-committee-bid** counterfactual, e.g. 20 Road + 8 committee, instead of assuming field expansion is the only way to solve Road omissions.

**Recommendation:** treat Group 3 at 32 as under evaluation, not settled.

## 6. Parastate overall remains highly stable

| | 2078 | 2079 | 2080 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|

| Won ≥1 State match | 96 | 100 | 106 |

The committee system is therefore broadly stable. The anomaly is Group 3 specifically, not committees as a whole.

# Transfer-market evolution

## 7. Actual offseason volumes

| Market | 2078 | 2079 | 2080 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Deep clearing | 768 | 760 | 717 |
| Role liberation | 71 | 92 | **110** |
| Feeder / down-flow | 85* | 88 | 61 |
| Reload / up-flow | 12* | 12 | 12 |
| Total | **936** | **952** | **900** |

\*2078's final 97-player deliberate second pass supplied the feeder/reload layer.

The critical finding is not movement volume. It is conversion quality by mechanism.

---

## 8. Generic deep clearing is showing clear diminishing returns

| Metric | 2078 | 2079 | 2080 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Median destination rank | 10 | 12 | **13** |
| Top 11 | **68%** | 49% | **40%** |
| 10+ varsity appearances | 72% | 51% | **41%** |
| Median varsity appearances | 20 | 10 | **6** |
| Median candidate OVR | 41 | 38 | **31** |

The declining median candidate OVR is the key diagnosis.

The market has worked through many of its most obvious cases. By 2080 the median deep-clearing candidate was only 31 OVR, which means the mechanism increasingly moved ordinary deep-roster players rather than plainly varsity-caliber talent trapped in JV/reserve roles.

A move from No. 17 to No. 13 is technically upward mobility, but increasingly fails the doctrine's actual objective.

**Conclusion:** generic deep clearing should move from the center of transfer policy to a residual mechanism reserved for obvious mismatches.

---

## 9. Role liberation is strengthening dramatically

| Metric | 2078 | 2079 | 2080 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Movers | 71 | 92 | **110** |
| Top 11 | 98.6% | 94.6% | **100%** |
| 10+ varsity appearances | 100% | 97.8% | **100%** |
| Median destination rank | 3 | 5 | **3** |
| Median varsity appearances | 28 | 29 | **29** |
| Doubles → singles | 21 | 10 | **78** |
| Material role improvement | 79% | 61% | **93%** |

The 2080 policy became more explicit about finding actual S1/S2/S3 roles for experienced lower-doubles players, and the outcome improved sharply.

This is the cleanest longitudinal validation in the transfer experiment.

### Persistence

These moves also last.

Among 2078 role movers young enough to remain in 2079:

- all 29 stayed at their new school;
- median 26 varsity appearances the next season;
- median 10 singles appearances.

Among the equivalent 2079 cohort in 2080:

- all 43 stayed;
- median 27 varsity appearances;
- median 13 singles appearances.

These are durable changes to player careers, not one-season rentals.

---

## 10. Reloads and rebuilds solve different problems

### Reloads

The four 2079 reload targets produced immediate high-end success:

- Waldo: rank 50 → **1**, State champion
- Los Feliz: 52 → **2**
- Puerto de los Reyes: 55 → **3**
- Jefferson Science: 56 → **5**, State champion

One year later:

- Waldo: rank 5, still State-caliber
- Los Feliz: rank 46, missed State
- Puerto de los Reyes: rank 64, missed State
- Jefferson Science: rank 49, missed State

Reloads therefore behave like **high-leverage veteran rentals**. They can instantly create a championship roster but usually do not rebuild the institution by themselves.

### Weak-program cohort rebuilds

The 30 programs rebuilt in 2078 began with a median class rank of **79**.

Immediately after intervention:

- median rank: **37.5**

By 2080:

- median rank: **41**
- 15 of 30 made State
- median winning percentage improved from about .193 pre-rebuild to **.446** two years later

This indicates significantly more persistence than a reload.

A concentrated rebuild can leave structural residue even after the original imported class ages.

---

## 11. Rebuild-host targeting improved over time

Median one-year class-rank improvement:

- 2078 weak hosts: **+33.5**
- 2079 weak hosts: **+46**
- 2080 weak hosts: **+51.5**

Median winning-percentage gains:

- +.304
- +.426
- +.355

Host selection is becoming better at finding programs with genuinely replaceable varsity capacity rather than nominal roster openings.

---

## 12. Feeder transfers remain useful, but need role-aware evaluation

Feeder success should not be judged primarily by whether the receiving program climbs dozens of places.

The proper question is:

> **Did the blocked upper-class player acquire a meaningful role without damaging a functioning recipient program?**

The mechanism remains useful, but future versions should require a more explicit destination-role fit rather than relying mainly on classification/rank matching.

---

## 13. Transfers compressed the deep classes without flattening the association

Mean top-11 talent remained largely stable:

- 9A: 61.2 → 60.9 → 60.2
- 8A: 59.6 → 59.5 → 59.4
- 6A: 55.8 → 55.4 → 55.6

The classification talent bands did not collapse into one another.

What changed was the **distribution inside the classes**: the gap between strongest and weakest programs narrowed in the largest classifications.

This is consistent with allocation improvement rather than parity engineering.

# Player and historical layer

## 14. Individual State remains more orderly than Team State

| | 2078 | 2079 | 2080 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| No. 1 individual champions | 80/144 | 65 | **82** |
| Top-four champions | 130 | 126 | 128 |
| Mean champion seed | 2.11 | 2.43 | 2.39 |
| Deepest champion | No. 12 | No. 12 | **No. 21** |

Team championships are therefore substantially more volatile than individual championships.

That reinforces the conclusion that lineup construction and dual format—not merely random player-level upset variance—drive much of State's uncertainty.

## 15. Historic careers now require active retention protection

Examples of sustained historical arcs:

- **Pacific Friends girls:** Group 2 champion all three seasons; 23 total State titles.
- **Nora Caudill, Grace Christian:** 1A girls POY in 2078 (freshman), 2079 (sophomore), 2080 (junior); positioned to chase a fourth straight POY.

These careers validate the retention doctrine: accumulated institutional history should prevent the transfer system from optimizing away potentially legendary four-year careers.

## 16. Exchange students remain appropriately consequential but not dominant

Using the one-season Grade 11 arrival signature:

- 2080: 87 arrivals

2079 produced one Individual State champion from that population.

2080 produced several All-State players but no Individual State champions.

The exchange mechanism remains appropriately noisy: occasionally significant, usually just roster texture, and not an annual championship lottery.

# Governing conclusions after 2078–2080

## Postseason architecture

1. **Keep Group 2 at 40.** The expansion fixed a real selection defect and added competitive teams.
2. **Keep all other established field sizes unchanged.** Their cutoff behavior is generally well calibrated.
3. **Treat Group 3 at 32 as unresolved.** Selection improved, but competitive density deteriorated sharply in the first expanded season.
4. Test a **28-team / 8-committee-bid Group 3** counterfactual before making another permanent change.
5. Preserve differentiated match formats. The three-year data increasingly supports classification-specific competitive identities rather than a single universal card.

## Transfer architecture

The transfer market should now be ordered as follows:

### Primary mechanisms

- varsity role liberation;
- weak-program concentrated rebuild cohorts;
- selective reloads.

### Secondary mechanism

- deliberate feeder placements with explicit role fit.

### Residual mechanism

- deep clearing only for clearly varsity-caliber players whose current role is an obvious mismatch.

The key longitudinal evidence is:

> Deep-clearing median OVR: **41 → 38 → 31**  
> Deep-clearing top-11 conversion: **68% → 49% → 40%**

versus:

> 2080 role transfers: **100% top 11, 100% meaningful varsity use, 93% material role improvement.**

The easy JV-clearing problem has been substantially worked through.

The next transfer-policy frontier is no longer:

> **Who can we move?**

It is:

> **Whose career is structurally constrained by the role their current program can offer, and where is there a specific program-level need that gives them something meaningfully different?**

That should be the governing transfer question going forward.

## Why this report matters for future talent-model work

Any future adjustment to player talent distributions, development curves, classification talent bands, ceiling compression, roster depth, or generation rules should be evaluated against this three-season baseline.

Changing talent levels can easily alter:

- deep-roster congestion;
- role-transfer candidate volume;
- classification-specific parity;
- field-size adequacy;
- upset rates by dual format;
- rebuild-host capacity;
- exchange-student impact;
- historical career persistence.

Future agents should therefore compare talent-model changes against the 2078–2080 baseline rather than judging them only from raw OVR distributions or a single simulated season.
