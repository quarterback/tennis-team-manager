# BRIEF: JHSAA Reserve-Cohort Mobility

**Status:** Offseason roster-management philosophy  
**Initial data basis:** 2039 boys and girls JHSAA season exports  
**Primary experiment:** 2040 offseason and season

## Summary

JV creates a new class of evidence in the JHSAA simulation: some programs do not merely have a few blocked players; they have an entire reserve group strong enough to function as a varsity team somewhere else.

The clearest 2039 example is Rockridge boys. Rockridge went 27-5 at varsity and 19-0 at JV while carrying multiple returning reserve players in the high-50s and low-60s OVR. That group is not ordinary low-end transfer inventory. It is effectively a second varsity team without a varsity schedule.

The broader philosophy is **reserve-cohort mobility**. A strong JV / “Varsity 2” cohort can move as a unit to another program for a season so its players can compete at varsity level. The move may be temporary for most of the cohort and permanent for some. It does not require a recurring affiliate relationship.

This is distinct from the narrower affiliate-loan model. An affiliate relationship is one possible institutional form of reserve-cohort mobility.

## Why JV changes the problem

Before JV existed, a player buried at roster position 14 or 18 was mostly visible through ratings and ladder position. JV now tells us whether that player is simply depth or is already outperforming reserve competition.

The same can be true at the group level. A school can reveal that its entire second group is varsity-caliber somewhere else.

That creates a new offseason question:

> What should happen when a school is carrying a second varsity-caliber team that has no varsity schedule of its own?

Reserve-cohort mobility is the answer.

## Four different transfer markets

| Market | Player problem | Default movement | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opportunity clearing | Weak, marginal, or lower-middle players lack a useful role | Same class first, then down the pyramid | Individual |
| Blocked talent | A good player is trapped behind exceptional depth | Targeted move to a meaningful varsity role | Individual |
| **Reserve-cohort mobility** | A source has an entire second varsity-caliber group | Move a cohort to one host for a season | Cohort |
| Top-end portal | Established varsity players have competitive or personal goals | Strategic | Individual or cluster |

These mechanisms should remain separate.

A weak No. 18 belongs in opportunity clearing.

A 59/77 player going 18-1 in JV may belong in blocked talent.

Eight players of that quality sitting together below one varsity lineup create a reserve-cohort problem.

## Cohort, not collection

The point is to move these players together.

Moving eight reserve players independently recreates the ordinary transfer market. Moving them as a unit lets the simulation test the reserve team itself:

- Can the group function as a varsity team?
- What classification does it actually belong in?
- Which players remain useful when JV success becomes varsity competition?
- Which players become worth bringing back afterward?
- Can a weak host become respectable, a contender, or a champion?

A normal cohort might contain 7-10 players. The source need not empty its entire JV roster.

## Host philosophy

The host should already be bad enough that replacing its weakest varsity positions does little competitive harm.

Two shapes are especially useful.

### Core + void

The host has 2-4 legitimate varsity players and then a severe drop through the rest of the lineup. The cohort fills the weak portion while the host's best players remain important.

### Near-total rebuild

The host is poor throughout the lineup. The cohort can become most of the varsity roster for one year.

This is the cleanest way to answer questions such as:

> What would Rockridge B do if it played a 5A varsity schedule?

## Host-player protection

The mechanism should not solve one opportunity problem by creating another for a host senior.

Before placement:

1. Build the host's returning ladder.
2. Add the proposed reserve cohort.
3. Identify host seniors who would be knocked out of varsity.
4. Move those seniors through the ordinary opportunity-clearing market first.
5. Displaced host underclassmen play JV for the season.

JV makes temporary underclass displacement acceptable. Seniors get first priority to play.

## Geography is optional

Local placements can create legible feeder-school stories, but geography is not a governing constraint.

The stronger mental model is a tiered sports system. Reserve talent can be placed where competitive opportunity exists.

A same-city relationship may feel like a feeder arrangement. A distant placement may simply be the best competitive experiment. Both are valid.

## One-season default

Reserve-cohort moves are presumed to last one season, but return is not automatic.

The lifecycle is:

1. Source identifies an excess reserve cohort.
2. Cohort moves to a weak host.
3. Players compete at varsity for one season.
4. After graduation changes the source roster, the source gets first call.
5. Players the source needs for meaningful varsity roles may return.
6. Players the source still does not need remain at the host.
7. Retained players are not moved somewhere else again merely to optimize placement.

The source has **first call, not permanent ownership**.

A plausible result:

> 9 players move → 5 return → 1 graduates → 3 remain permanently.

This gives the host durable value from the arrangement.

## Relationship to development

Match participation does not drive player development in the JHSAA simulation.

Development rolls remain independent.

Reserve-cohort mobility changes **opportunity and evidence**, not intrinsic development. A player who would have spent another year in JV instead produces a varsity record against a different level of competition.

The next development roll would have occurred either way.

## What the source learns

A varsity season elsewhere can reveal whether a reserve player's JV performance translated.

A player may:

- dominate and clearly deserve recall;
- become a useful but ordinary varsity player;
- struggle despite a strong JV record;
- prove more valuable in doubles than singles;
- remain blocked at the source even after a good host season;
- become more valuable to the host than to the source.

That makes recall a real roster decision rather than an automatic transaction.

## What the host gets

The host receives more than a temporary record boost.

During the cohort year:

- varsity becomes more competitive;
- weak host underclassmen move into JV rather than disappearing;
- JV depth increases;
- the host's best incumbents play in a stronger team context.

Afterward:

- some reserve players may remain permanently;
- displaced host underclassmen can return to varsity;
- the program may retain a larger roster and a stronger baseline.

## Classification fit

The destination should be chosen by the competitive level of the **cohort plus host core**, not by a rigid source-to-destination rule.

A 9A reserve group might belong in 8A, 6A, 5A, or even 2A depending on how strong that cohort actually is and what experiment is being run.

Perfect competitive balance is not always the objective.

Some pilots should test whether an elite JV side can actually win a lower classification. Others should test whether a reserve cohort can merely make a terrible program average.

## Identifying reserve cohorts

A future search should look for source programs with a group of returning players who:

- played substantial JV lines;
- played little varsity;
- sit behind a genuinely strong varsity roster;
- are collectively strong enough to be useful varsity players elsewhere;
- number enough to operate as a coherent block.

The mechanism is for an **excess team**, not merely one or two blocked players.

## No permanent affiliate is required

Reserve-cohort mobility can be one-off.

Rockridge can place a cohort at one host in 2040 and somewhere completely different in 2041. A host that keeps several players does not automatically become a permanent Rockridge affiliate.

Recurring relationships can emerge if they prove useful, but they are optional.

This is the key distinction:

**Reserve-cohort mobility** is the general rule allowing excess reserve teams to move.

**Affiliate loans** are a recurring or explicitly paired version of that rule.

## Relationship to affiliate loans

`BRIEF-jhsaa-affiliate-loan-cohorts.md` describes the narrower affiliate model.

An affiliate relationship adds:

- a recognizable source-host pairing;
- a feeder-club identity;
- the possibility of recurring placements;
- a more legible long-term relationship between programs.

Those features are not required for reserve-cohort mobility.

The broad principle is simply:

> A varsity-caliber reserve cohort should be allowed to spend a season playing varsity elsewhere rather than remain trapped as an overqualified JV team.

## Relationship to the low-end clearing cascade

The ordinary low-end transfer market remains separate.

Weak and lower-middle players should:

1. search within their own classification first;
2. recalculate that class after placements;
3. spill to the next lower tier only if still unmatched;
4. repeat down the pyramid;
5. use 2A and 1A as final clearing markets.

That process improves competitive density inside each tier before pushing excess downward.

Reserve-cohort players are not low-end surplus. Their source school is simply unusually deep.

## What to measure

### Player outcomes

- varsity lines at host;
- singles/doubles role;
- varsity record;
- comparison with prior JV performance;
- recall, graduation, or host retention;
- role after returning to source.

### Source outcomes

- remaining JV depth;
- whether recalled players actually make varsity;
- how many players are permanently ceded to hosts.

### Host outcomes

- varsity record and classification standing;
- host incumbents retained in varsity;
- host underclassmen shifted to JV;
- JV size and results;
- permanent players retained afterward.

### System outcomes

- whether reserve mobility increases competitive density in 4A-7A;
- which classifications best absorb elite reserve groups;
- how often reserve cohorts become title contenders;
- whether host programs retain lasting gains.

## Initial experiment

The first season should use only a small number of cohorts with deliberately different shapes:

- an elite reserve group dropped aggressively into a lower classification;
- a cohort placed only one tier down;
- a core-plus-void host;
- a near-total rebuild;
- a local placement that resembles a feeder relationship;
- a non-local placement chosen solely for competitive fit.

The affiliate-loan brief contains concrete 2039 source and host candidates for these pilots.

## Design principle

The simulation should not assume that the competitive level of a player's school is also the competitive level at which every player at that school belongs.

Deep programs can contain multiple teams worth of varsity-caliber talent.

JV makes those teams visible.

Reserve-cohort mobility lets the rest of the association use them.
