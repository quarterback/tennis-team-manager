# JHSAA State Format Study: 5/2, 3/4, and 1/4

## 2027 Boys and Girls Preliminary Analysis

### Executive summary

The JHSAA State championship uses a **1 singles / 4 doubles** dual format after teams spend most of the season playing **5 singles / 2 doubles**. This has long generated an internal philosophical disagreement.

The conventional argument favors 5/2: the championship should use the same competitive test as the regular season.

A second faction favors 3/4: retain substantial singles competition, increase roster participation, and make doubles more important without allowing it to dominate the championship.

The JHSAA position has historically been different. High-school tennis is treated explicitly as a **team sport**. Junior players already have extensive opportunities to distinguish themselves individually through USTA, UTR, and other competitive pathways. Scholastic tennis should therefore reward roster construction, partnership, depth, and the ability to compete collectively. The 1/4 State format is an intentional expression of that philosophy rather than an attempt to reproduce conventional tournament tennis.

Preliminary analysis of the 2027 boys and girls seasons suggests something important:

> **The 1/4 format is not merely quirky. It materially changes which kinds of teams are strongest.**

Across 1,638 boys and girls programs, changing the weighting of observed regular-season singles and doubles performance from 5/2 to 1/4 moves a team's expected line share by approximately **11.8 percentage points on average**. The median movement is approximately 11.5 points. Among the most format-sensitive 5% of teams, the shift is roughly **24 percentage points or greater**.

A hypothetical 3/4 format produces substantially less disruption: approximately **6.6 percentage points on average**.

This does not establish that 1/4 is unfair. It establishes that JHSAA is making a consequential choice about what its championship measures.

The early evidence suggests the format is doing exactly what its advocates intend.

---

## The three competing ideas of team tennis

The disagreement is easier to understand if the formats are treated as competing definitions of scholastic tennis rather than merely different lineup cards.

### 5 singles / 2 doubles

Nine players participate.

Singles represents five of seven courts:

* Singles: 71.4%
* Doubles: 28.6%

This is the regular-season JHSAA format and the strongest continuity argument for State.

A team that proves itself over an entire season under 5/2 would face essentially the same competitive problem in the championship.

The philosophical premise is straightforward:

> State should determine the best version of the same kind of team that competed during the regular season.

### 3 singles / 4 doubles

Eleven players participate.

* Singles: 42.9%
* Doubles: 57.1%

This is the natural compromise format.

Doubles becomes slightly more important than singles, roster depth becomes more important, and more athletes participate. But a program with three excellent singles players retains a substantial portion of that advantage.

The cost is roster size. Moving from nine starters to eleven represents a 22% increase in the number of players necessary to fill a complete lineup.

That may matter very differently to a large 9A program than to a small 1A or 2A school.

### 1 singles / 4 doubles

Nine players participate.

* Singles: 20%
* Doubles: 80%

This is the JHSAA State format.

It preserves the nine-player roster footprint while radically changing how those nine players are deployed.

S1 remains important and gives the association an individual marquee matchup. But the championship overwhelmingly rewards partnership tennis.

The philosophical premise is:

> The high-school championship should be the fullest expression of tennis as a team sport.

That distinction is important when evaluating whether the format is "fair." JHSAA did not choose 1/4 because it believed it was competitively equivalent to 5/2.

It chose it because it was not.

# Measuring the format effect

The 2027 research exports allow a simple first approximation of how much each format changes the value of a team's demonstrated strengths.

Let:

* `S` = regular-season singles line win percentage
* `D` = regular-season doubles line win percentage

Then:

```text
5/2 Expected Line Share
= (5S + 2D) / 7

3/4 Expected Line Share
= (3S + 4D) / 7

1/4 Expected Line Share
= (S + 4D) / 5
```

These are descriptive indices rather than match predictions. They simply ask:

> If the team's demonstrated singles and doubles performance were reweighted according to a different lineup card, what would its expected share of courts look like?

This produces a useful statistic:

## Format Lift (Fmt)

```text
Fmt = 1/4 Expected Line Share - 5/2 Expected Line Share
```

A positive Fmt identifies a team whose demonstrated strengths become more valuable at State.

A negative Fmt identifies a team whose regular-season strengths become less valuable.

Because of the weights involved:

```text
1/4 lift over 5/2
= 0.514 × (D - S)
```

For 3/4:

```text
3/4 lift over 5/2
= 0.286 × (D - S)
```

This means 3/4 creates only about **5/9 as much format movement** as 1/4.

The compromise is therefore visible mathematically as well as philosophically.

# How large is the effect?

Across the combined 2027 boys and girls datasets, containing 1,638 programs:

* average absolute 5/2 → 1/4 movement: **~11.8 percentage points**
* median absolute movement: **~11.5 points**
* most format-sensitive 5%: approximately **24 points or greater**
* average absolute 5/2 → 3/4 movement: **~6.6 points**

Those are substantial changes.

A championship played under 1/4 is not simply 5/2 tennis with a little more emphasis on doubles.

It can materially reorder the competitive hierarchy.

A preliminary comparison of team rankings generated from the raw format indices reinforces this. Across the boys and girls classifications, the average rank correlation between the 5/2 and 1/4 profiles is approximately **0.51**.

For 5/2 and 3/4, it is approximately **0.77**.

Similarly, only about 32% of teams appearing in the top eight under the raw 5/2 profile remain top eight under 1/4. Under 3/4, approximately 54% remain.

These figures should not yet be treated as predictive ratings. They use raw observed singles and doubles results and do not fully account for opponent quality, lineup availability, or other contextual factors.

They nevertheless establish the scale of the question.

**1/4 materially reorders team strength.**

# Case study: Vespertine girls

The 2027 7A girls championship provides perhaps the clearest defense of the existing JHSAA philosophy.

Vespertine finished:

```text
25-10
#22 7A TOSS
State Champion
```

Its regular-season profile:

```text
Singles Win%       47.5%
Doubles Win%       89.6%

5/2 Index          59.5%
3/4 Index          71.6%
1/4 Index          81.2%

Format Lift       +21.6
```

Under conventional 5/2 weighting, Vespertine looks like a good but hardly overwhelming team.

Under the State format, it looks elite.

This was not merely theoretical. Vespertine beat #18 Romero-Finniski 4-1, #5 St. Vincent 4-1, and eventually #7 Morne Rouge 3-2 for the State championship.

Vespertine therefore presents the philosophical argument in its strongest possible form.

Critics can reasonably say:

> The #22 team became championship-caliber because JHSAA changed the competitive test.

Supporters can respond:

> Correct. Vespertine developed the best doubles program in the classification. That is precisely the kind of team the JHSAA championship is designed to reward.

Vespertine was not necessarily a Cinderella.

It may have been a **JHSAA team hiding inside a conventional tennis résumé**.

# Case study: Port Meridian North girls

The 8A tournament produced an even more extreme example.

Port Meridian North entered with:

```text
19-15
#29 8A TOSS
State Finalist
```

Its profile:

```text
Singles Win%       36.8%
Doubles Win%       84.0%

5/2 Index          50.3%
3/4 Index          63.8%
1/4 Index          74.6%

Format Lift       +24.3
```

Under 5/2, this is approximately an average underlying profile.

Under 1/4, it becomes an extremely dangerous one.

Port Meridian North subsequently beat #7 Longfellow 5-0, defeated #4 Valderra 3-2, beat #17 Cortland Memorial 3-2, and reached the State final before #1 Westover stopped the run.

Again, 3/4 produces the intuitive compromise.

```text
5/2:  50.3
3/4:  63.8
1/4:  74.6
```

The 3/4 interpretation is essentially:

> Port Meridian North is substantially better suited to team-oriented championship tennis than its record suggests, but it should not be transformed from average to elite merely because of that specialization.

Whether that is preferable depends on what State is intended to measure.

# Case study: High Prairie girls

The strongest argument from the other side may be 1A finalist High Prairie.

High Prairie finished 29-4 with an elite regular-season profile:

```text
Singles Win%       86.4%
Doubles Win%       48.0%

5/2 Index          75.4%
3/4 Index          64.5%
1/4 Index          55.7%

Format Lift       -19.7
```

This is not a mediocre team exposed by better competition.

It is an excellent team whose greatest strength becomes dramatically less valuable at State.

High Prairie's singles advantage represents five of seven regular-season courts.

At State, it represents one of five.

The championship result illustrated the problem perfectly. High Prairie won S1 against Marlow County but lost three of four doubles courts and the championship, 3-2.

The 5/2 faction can make a serious argument from this case:

> High Prairie spent an entire season demonstrating extraordinary team strength. The championship then removed four of the five positions in which that strength was expressed.

The 3/4 faction has perhaps an even stronger response:

> Doubles should matter more at State, but High Prairie should still receive meaningful credit for building multiple excellent singles players.

Under 3/4, High Prairie falls from 75.4 to 64.5 rather than all the way to 55.7.

Its weakness matters.

Its strength still matters.

# Case study: Camden Flat girls

Camden Flat illustrates why the format question should not be reduced to championship winners.

The 1A program entered State at #32 TOSS and reached the semifinal.

```text
Singles Win%       49.5%
Doubles Win%       78.9%

5/2 Index          57.9%
3/4 Index          66.3%
1/4 Index          73.1%

Format Lift       +15.2
```

Camden Flat also went 6-3 in one-point regular-season duals.

Before the State tournament, those two facts would have provided independent reasons to regard the team as dangerous:

1. its roster becomes considerably stronger under State weighting;
2. it had already demonstrated substantial experience surviving narrow duals.

A #32 TOSS team reaching the State semifinal therefore does not automatically indicate bracket randomness.

The regular-season ranking may simply be measuring a different competitive environment.

# The boys evidence

The boys export strengthens the case that this is a systematic feature rather than an isolated girls-season curiosity.

Among the clearest examples is 7A champion Arrieta Treasure Valley:

```text
Singles Win%       55.8%
Doubles Win%      100.0%

5/2 Index          68.4%
3/4 Index          81.1%
1/4 Index          91.2%

Format Lift       +22.7
```

Its championship opponent, Morne Rouge:

```text
Singles Win%       47.5%
Doubles Win%       95.8%

5/2 Index          61.3%
3/4 Index          75.1%
1/4 Index          86.2%

Format Lift       +24.9
```

The presence of two enormously doubles-heavy teams in the same State final is notable.

Combined with Vespertine, Port Meridian North, Camden Flat, and other examples, the early evidence suggests the 1/4 championship environment is selecting for a recognizable roster architecture.

This deserves continued longitudinal study.

# The control case: Cortland girls

Not every championship is explained by format.

That is analytically important.

9A champion Cortland finished 35-1 and ranked #1 in TOSS.

Its regular-season profile:

```text
Singles Win%       92.0%
Doubles Win%       96.0%

5/2 Index          93.1%
1/4 Index          95.2%

Format Lift        +2.1
```

Cortland is effectively **format-proof**.

A 5/2 championship would favor Cortland.

A 3/4 championship would favor Cortland.

A 1/4 championship favors Cortland.

This provides an important distinction for future analysis.

Some teams succeed at State because the format unlocks a roster whose strengths were muted during the regular season.

Other teams succeed because they are simply better at everything.

A useful analytics system should distinguish those cases.

# The strongest case for 5/2

The case for 5/2 is competitive continuity.

If the purpose of a State tournament is to determine the best team from the competitive population established during the regular season, the postseason should ideally preserve the same test.

5/2 does this automatically.

There is no Format Lift because there is no format change.

It also avoids the conceptual oddity of asking coaches to construct one type of lineup for most of the season and another for the championship.

The 2027 evidence suggests that this objection has empirical substance. The 1/4 change is large enough to substantially reorder team profiles.

A coach objecting to 1/4 is therefore not merely complaining about an unfamiliar format.

The coach can accurately say:

> State measures something materially different from the regular season.

The question is whether that is a defect.

---

# The strongest case for 3/4

The evidence makes 3/4 an unusually credible compromise.

It accomplishes several things simultaneously:

* doubles becomes the majority of the dual;
* singles retains substantial competitive importance;
* roster depth matters more;
* more athletes participate;
* format disruption is substantially smaller than under 1/4.

Empirically, it produces only about 5/9 as much reweighting as 1/4.

The examples are intuitive.

Port Meridian North:

```text
5/2    50.3
3/4    63.8
1/4    74.6
```

High Prairie:

```text
5/2    75.4
3/4    64.5
1/4    55.7
```

3/4 tells both programs:

> Your specialization matters, but neither specialization defines the entire championship.

That is a compelling competitive argument.

Its primary structural disadvantage is roster size.

A complete 3/4 lineup requires eleven players rather than nine.

That should not be dismissed as an administrative detail. A format that works comfortably for large classifications may create participation and forfeiture problems among smaller schools.

Before serious consideration of 3/4, JHSAA would need to study:

* roster sizes by classification;
* incomplete-lineup frequency;
* lower-flight forfeitures;
* number of eligible players available to 1A/2A programs;
* whether girls and boys programs face different roster constraints;
* whether requiring eleven starters would reduce the number of schools capable of sustaining varsity tennis.

A competitively elegant format could still be institutionally worse.

---

# The strongest case for 1/4

The strongest defense of 1/4 is not statistical.

It is philosophical.

JHSAA treats scholastic tennis as a distinct form of the sport.

Junior tennis already supplies substantial infrastructure for individual competition. Strong singles players have USTA, UTR, tournaments, rankings, recruiting showcases, and other mechanisms through which individual excellence can be recognized.

High-school tennis has a different opportunity.

It can make tennis genuinely collective.

The 1/4 format forces programs to develop:

* partnerships;
* doubles tactics;
* roster depth;
* complementary player combinations;
* athletes whose value may not be captured by individual-star hierarchies;
* coaching systems capable of turning individual players into functioning units.

It also preserves the nine-player roster size used by 5/2.

The format therefore changes the *deployment* of the roster without requiring additional athletes.

Most importantly, the preliminary evidence indicates that this philosophy has competitive consequences.

Vespertine matters here.

A program with mediocre singles results and extraordinary doubles performance can become a State champion.

That is not necessarily evidence against the system.

If JHSAA believes doubles development and collective play should define its championship, Vespertine is evidence that the policy works.

---

# Fairness depends on the definition

The 2027 evidence does not answer whether 1/4 is fair because "fairness" contains several different questions.

### Continuity fairness

Does State reward approximately the same form of excellence demonstrated during the regular season?

**5/2 clearly performs best.**

### Balance fairness

Do both singles and doubles remain consequential while doubles and roster depth receive greater championship importance?

**3/4 performs best.**

### Participation fairness

Can schools across classifications reliably field the required number of athletes?

The current evidence is insufficient. The nine-player requirement gives 5/2 and 1/4 a likely structural advantage over 3/4, but roster data should be studied.

### Institutional fairness

Does the championship reflect what JHSAA believes high-school tennis ought to value?

If the stated values are partnership, team construction, participation beyond individual stars, and a distinct scholastic form of tennis:

**1/4 is highly coherent.**

These definitions should not be collapsed into one question.
