# REPORT — JHSAA 2069 Epiregional Year-One Review

**Season:** 2069  
**Scope:** JHSAA boys and girls, all classifications  
**Policy:** Epiregional seeding reform, first season  
**Owner rule:** 2026-09  
**Relevant implementation:** `jhsaa.run_epiregional`, `jhsaa.state_seed_order`, `seed_atr`

## Executive summary

The first season of the Epiregional produced a strong validation of the reform.

The event did enough competitive work to justify existing without becoming a second elimination layer. Across 96 Epiregional duals, the higher-seeded Zonal champion won roughly two-thirds of the time, while nearly half of all duals finished 3–2. The round therefore neither rubber-stamped the seeding formula nor reduced to a coin flip.

Its most important effect was on State seed placement. Under the old rule, all 192 Zonal champions across the 24 boys/girls classification fields would have occupied the top eight State seed lines automatically. Under the new rule, 44 of those top-eight positions were released to non-Zonal champions that rated higher on the State seeding measure.

That means **22.9% of the top-eight State seed lines changed hands because of the reform**.

The downstream State results strongly suggest that the Epiregional was separating stronger Zonal champions from weaker ones rather than merely adding another result to the schedule:

- 70.8% of Epiregional winners reached at least the State quarterfinals.
- 44.8% reached at least the State semifinals.
- 30.2% reached the State final.
- 18 of the 24 State champions had won their Epiregional.
- Only three State champions had lost their Epiregional.

The reform also preserved the intended distinction between **qualification** and **seed protection**. Every Zonal champion remained in State. Losing the Epiregional could cost a protected seed, but it did not cost the berth. Cottonrock Point's girls 4A run is the clearest example: it lost its Epiregional 5–0, entered State as the No. 15 seed, and then won the championship.

Year one therefore supports the policy premise: **a Zonal title earns State, but it does not by itself establish that a team belongs on one of the top eight seed lines.**

---

## 1. Event profile

Each of the 24 classification-gender fields sent its eight Zonal champions into four Epiregional duals.

That produced:

| Metric | 2069 |
|---|---:|
| Epiregional duals | 96 |
| Higher-seeded winner | 65 |
| Lower-seeded winner | 31 |
| Lower-seeded win rate | **32.3%** |
| 3–2 duals | **47 (49.0%)** |
| 4–1 duals | 29 (30.2%) |
| 5–0 duals | 20 (20.8%) |

The result profile is close to ideal for the event's purpose.

A 32.3% lower-seeded win rate means the seeding input was informative but far from deterministic. The higher-seeded team won a clear majority, but roughly one dual in three overturned the initial order on the flights.

At the same time, 49.0% of Epiregionals finished 3–2. The round therefore generated a large number of consequential, close duals without making the initial seeding meaningless.

The Epiregional behaved as a competitive sorting mechanism rather than a ceremonial confirmation of the formula.

---

## 2. The seeding reform moved 44 top-eight positions

There were 192 Zonal champions across the 24 fields.

Under the previous rule, those 192 teams would have filled all 192 top-eight State seed lines automatically.

Under the 2069 rule:

- the 96 Epiregional winners received guaranteed top-eight placement;
- 52 of the 96 Epiregional losers still retained a top-eight seed on merit;
- 44 Epiregional losers fell to seed No. 9 or lower.

Therefore:

**44 of 192 top-eight State seed lines changed hands.**

That is **22.9%** of all protected seed lines.

This is materially lower than the five-season historical backtest, which found 356 changes across 960 bye lines, or 37.1%. That difference is useful. It shows that the new rule does not manufacture turnover simply because it exists. In 2069, the Zonal-champion cohort was strong enough that more than half of the Epiregional losers still earned top-eight placement through the seeding measure.

The mechanism is therefore responsive to the season rather than quota-driven.

---

## 3. The teams retained and displaced by the rule were meaningfully different

The distinction between Epiregional losers who retained a protected seed and those who fell outside the top eight was substantial.

Among the 52 losing teams that still received a top-eight State seed, the median class rank was approximately **5.5**.

Among the 44 losing teams that fell to seed No. 9 or below, the median class rank was approximately **12**.

That is the separation the reform was designed to expose.

The old rule treated those two groups identically because both had won Zonals. The new rule preserved top-eight protection for the losing Zonal champions that still looked like elite teams over the full season while removing it from teams whose Zonal title had outrun their larger body of work.

The distinction was not absolute. Eleven of the 44 displaced teams still reached the State quarterfinals, seven reached the semifinals, and Cottonrock Point won State. That is a feature rather than a contradiction.

The rule is not intended to declare the ninth seed incapable of winning. It is intended to stop a Zonal title from forcing the association to rank that team above stronger State entrants.

---

## 4. Epiregional winners performed much better at State

The clearest year-one validation is the State performance split between Epiregional winners and losers.

| State outcome | Epiregional winners | Epiregional losers |
|---|---:|---:|
| Quarterfinal or better | **68 / 96 (70.8%)** | 41 / 96 (42.7%) |
| Semifinal or better | **43 / 96 (44.8%)** | 18 / 96 (18.8%) |
| Final or better | **29 / 96 (30.2%)** | 8 / 96 (8.3%) |
| State champions | **18** | 3 |

The round was therefore strongly associated with later State performance.

Epiregional winners were:

- 1.66 times as likely to reach the State quarterfinals;
- 2.38 times as likely to reach the semifinals;
- 3.64 times as likely to reach the final;
- six times as likely to win State.

One season is not enough to treat those ratios as stable causal estimates. But the separation is large enough to establish that the Epiregional was not merely decorative.

The teams that won it subsequently behaved like stronger championship teams.

---

## 5. Eighteen of 24 State champions won their Epiregional

Eighteen State champions came from the Epiregional-winning side.

Three champions came from the losing side:

- **Girls Group 3 — Latgaway.** Lost 4–1 to Virginia City, then won State from the No. 6 seed.
- **Girls 4A — Cottonrock Point.** Lost 5–0 to Orlova, entered State as the No. 15 seed, then won State.
- **Girls 5A — Kingsley.** Lost 3–2 to Sea Cliff, retained the No. 4 State seed, then won State.

The remaining State champions came from championship paths outside the relevant Epiregional-winner/loser comparison.

The three losing-side champions are important because they demonstrate that the Epiregional did not become a disguised elimination round.

A team can lose the event, lose its seed protection, and still win State.

That is exactly the architecture the reform intended.

---

## 6. Cottonrock Point is the canonical year-one case

Cottonrock Point's girls 4A season is the most useful single example of how the new system is supposed to behave.

The team lost its Epiregional to Orlova **5–0**.

Under the old rule, its Zonal title would still have guaranteed one of the top eight State seeds.

Under the new rule, that result and the larger seeding résumé placed Cottonrock Point at **No. 15**.

It then won State.

This is not evidence that the Epiregional failed. It is evidence that the distinction between seed placement and tournament possibility is functioning correctly.

The association did not have to make either of two bad claims:

1. that a Zonal title proved Cottonrock Point belonged in the top eight; or
2. that losing the Epiregional proved Cottonrock Point did not belong in State.

Instead, the system made the narrower claim the evidence supported:

**Cottonrock Point had earned its State berth but had not earned a protected seed.**

The team then settled the rest in the State draw.

---

## 7. The Epiregional did not simply reinforce the formula

Thirty-one of the 96 lower-seeded teams won their Epiregional.

Examples included:

- Boys Group 3: Covenant Christian def. Natchez Mercy, 4–1.
- Boys 1A: Pellford def. Wishram, 3–2.
- Girls Group 2: Lodestone County def. Pacific Friends, 3–2.
- Girls 6A: Carden Pass def. Valley Christian, 3–2.

These results matter because the event would have little value if it merely restated `seed_atr`.

Instead, roughly one-third of the lower-seeded Zonal champions took the guaranteed seed away from the team the formula initially preferred.

The reform therefore combines two forms of evidence:

- the season-long seeding measure establishes the matchup order;
- the dual itself decides which side receives guaranteed top-eight protection.

That is materially different from both the old automatic-Zonal rule and a purely formulaic merit-seeding system.

---

## 8. Boys and girls diverged

All three State champions that lost their Epiregional came from the girls side.

The boys side was substantially more orderly: every boys State champion that emerged from an Epiregional pairing had won that Epiregional.

That fits the larger 2069 postseason pattern, in which the girls State tournaments produced more survivor champions and greater bracket volatility in several classifications.

The distinction is worth tracking over multiple seasons before treating it as a structural gender effect. The first-year result may reflect the particular competitive distributions in 2069 rather than anything inherent to the event.

But it is a useful monitoring signal for future AARs.

---

## 9. Comparison with the historical backtest

The historical five-season backtest projected a much larger amount of seed-line turnover:

| Measure | Historical backtest | 2069 actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Top-eight lines reassigned | 356 / 960 | 44 / 192 |
| Share reassigned | **37.1%** | **22.9%** |

The lower actual rate is not a weakness.

The historical exercise established that the old automatic-Zonal rule could materially distort State seeding over time. The first live season then showed that the new system does not force that historical average onto every year.

When Zonal champions are strong, they retain more of the top eight.

When they are weaker, the mechanism has room to move more of them down.

That season-to-season elasticity is preferable to a fixed reform target.

---

## 10. Year-one judgment

The Epiregional passed its first-season test on all four of the questions that matter.

### Did the event itself matter?

Yes.

Thirty-one of 96 lower-seeded teams won, and nearly half of all duals finished 3–2.

### Did it materially change State seed placement?

Yes.

Forty-four top-eight seed lines moved from Zonal champions to higher-rated non-Zonal champions.

### Did it distinguish teams that subsequently performed differently?

Yes.

Epiregional winners substantially outperformed losers at every major State finish threshold.

### Did it preserve the principle that a Zonal champion had already qualified for State?

Yes.

Every Zonal champion remained in the State field, and three Epiregional losers went on to win State.

---

## Conclusion

The year-one evidence supports keeping the Epiregional.

The old system conflated two separate achievements:

1. earning a State berth by winning a Zonal;
2. earning one of the best positions in the State draw.

The Epiregional separates them.

A Zonal champion is still a State qualifier. The Epiregional determines whether that champion also earns guaranteed top-eight placement, while the remaining protected seeds are awarded on season-long merit.

In 2069 that distinction affected 44 of 192 top-eight positions, produced a competitive round in which lower-seeded teams won nearly one-third of the time, and generated a winner cohort that substantially outperformed the losing cohort at State.

The strongest year-one finding is therefore not that the Epiregional reduced upsets or made State more predictable.

It is that **the association stopped confusing qualification with seeding, and the first season's results behaved as though that distinction carried real competitive information.**
