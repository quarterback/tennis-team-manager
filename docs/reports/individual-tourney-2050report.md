# Evaluating the JHSAA Individual State Championships

### Early findings on access, player development, school retention and the growth of tennis in Jefferson

**Prepared for the Jefferson High School Activities Association**
For distribution to the Jefferson Athletic Directors Association, the Jefferson tennis coaches community, school administrators and media partners

## Executive summary

The JHSAA created the Individual State Championships in response to a problem that team tennis could not solve by itself.

Jefferson’s team championship remains the association’s premier competition. The concern was that the importance of team success was also creating incentives that worked against school-based tennis. Players at strong programs could spend years deep in a lineup. Players at ordinary programs could be excellent without ever experiencing State competition. Families increasingly understood that transferring to a deeper or more successful tennis program could affect visibility, postseason opportunity and the perceived value of a high-school career.

The initial objection to an individual championship was reasonable: Jefferson already had a substantial calendar of junior, club and offseason tournaments. Coaches, athletic directors and principals ultimately made the more persuasive case. Access to those tournaments is uneven. Not every student plays year-round. Not every family can support regular travel, entry fees, private coaching or a tournament schedule outside the school season. A school-based state championship provides an opportunity that depends first on being a member of a school team.

The first two years of results strongly support that decision.

In 2050, the boys’ and girls’ main individual championships provided **16,767 students** with a place in a State draw. The mixed doubles championship brought in another **1,812 students from below the nine main-draw positions**. In total, **18,579 students participated in an individual State championship event**.

More important than the volume is who benefited.

Nearly **60 percent of the students in the 2050 main draws attended schools whose team did not qualify for team State**. Players from non-State teams won **11 of the 144 boys’ and girls’ individual championships**. Players from teams with losing dual-match records won **13 titles**.

The mixed doubles results provide an even stronger indication that the new structure is creating a pathway rather than merely another tournament. Of the **1,366 underclass students who entered mixed doubles in 2049**, **1,009 — 74 percent — advanced into a main Individual State singles or doubles position one year later**. Of those 1,009 students, **1,003 did so at the same school**.

Fifteen members of that 2049 mixed cohort became **2050 main-draw State champions**.

The event is also serving a second purpose that was not central to the original access argument: because the main championships occur before the team season and their results count toward the school ladder, they have become a statewide preseason challenge tournament. Coaches receive meaningful competitive evidence before the first dual match. Players can win their way upward before a lineup settles. In 2050, **2,643 of 11,034 main-draw matches produced a seeding upset**, nearly one in four. Twenty-one of the 144 champions entered their draw seeded outside the top eight.

The recommendation of this review is straightforward: **retain the current structure and protect the features that distinguish it from the existing junior-tournament ecosystem.**

The JHSAA should regard the championships as a successful extension of school tennis, not as a competitor to team State and not as a replacement for junior tennis.

## Why JHSAA added the event

The policy problem was larger than identifying the best singles player in Jefferson.

Before the championships were established, JHSAA participation data consistently showed how much of a school roster could pass through a season with limited varsity opportunity. Across the 2045 through 2048 baseline:

* **41.6 percent** of players recorded five or fewer varsity matches.
* **8.9 percent** recorded no varsity match at all.
* The pattern was nearly identical between boys and girls.
* Across those four seasons, almost **60,000 player-seasons** ended with five or fewer varsity appearances.

That problem has several causes. High-school tennis necessarily concentrates meaningful varsity roles. A regular JHSAA dual uses eleven players. State uses an even smaller championship lineup. Strong programs may carry substantially more players than they can use in consequential matches.

JHSAA has addressed the deeper participation problem separately through JV competition. The Individual State Championships address a different question:

**Must a student attend one of Jefferson’s best tennis schools to have a meaningful State-level individual opportunity?**

The answer now is clearly no.

That matters in a transfer environment.

The association’s goal was never to prevent every transfer. There are legitimate reasons students change schools, and some players genuinely need a different competitive environment. The concern was making transfer the obvious answer to a structural problem created by the championship system itself.

An individual championship gives a strong player a reason to remain at an ordinary school. It gives a player at a deep program something meaningful to earn before deciding that leaving is the only path to opportunity. It also creates public recognition for excellence that team standings cannot always capture.

The 2050 results provide unusually clear examples.

Canyonlands finished **6-15** and did not make team State. Daniel Hudson won the 7A No. 1 singles championship. Chase Moreno won No. 2 singles.

Bridger County Christian finished **8-16** and missed State. Edward Martinez won Group 2 No. 1 singles. Blake Smith won No. 2 singles.

Sotavento’s girls finished **5-18**. Pyper Dudek won the 8A No. 1 singles championship.

Mt. Jacqueline went **8-16**. Kylie Ackerman won the 2A No. 1 singles championship.

Foothills Christian went **10-17**. Isabel Knight won the 1A No. 3 singles title.

Before the individual championship existed, those seasons ended with the team record defining the public account of those players’ years.

Now it does not.

That is a significant change in what school tennis can offer.

## State competition is reaching students whose teams never reach State

The open-entry structure has turned out to be one of the event’s most important decisions.

Every school sends the holders of the six main positions:

* No. 1 singles
* No. 2 singles
* No. 3 singles
* No. 1 doubles
* No. 2 doubles
* No. 3 doubles

Nine students per program therefore enter the main event.

In 2050, **530 boys programs and 581 girls programs did not qualify for team State**.

Those schools nevertheless accounted for:

* **4,770 boys** in the main individual championship
* **5,229 girls**
* **9,999 students total**

That means **59.6 percent of all main-draw participants came from teams that were not part of team State**.

The opportunity was not ceremonial.

In 2050, players or doubles teams from non-State programs recorded:

* **198 quarterfinal-or-better entries**
* representing **177 different school-gender programs**
* **74 semifinal-or-better entries**
* **26 finalist entries**
* **11 State championships**

The same pattern was visible in 2049.

This answers one of the original questions about whether a statewide individual tournament would simply produce another stage for the same elite programs. It has not.

The strongest schools still win a large share of championships. They should. They have strong players and deep programs.

But they do not own access to the tournament.

## Mixed doubles may be the most important developmental result

Mixed doubles was deliberately designed differently.

The six main championship positions use a school’s top nine players. Mixed doubles is reserved for players **below those nine positions**. It is a single summer championship rather than another flighted event.

That made mixed doubles easy to dismiss as a consolation tournament.

The first longitudinal data suggests that description substantially understates its value.

The 2049 mixed championship included **1,766 students**. Four hundred were seniors. The remaining **1,366 were eligible to return in 2050**.

One year later:

**1,009 of those 1,366 students were in the main Individual State Championship.**

That is **73.9 percent**.

Their progression was:

| 2050 position | 2049 mixed entrants |
| ------------- | ------------------: |
| No. 1 singles |                   6 |
| No. 2 singles |                  14 |
| No. 3 singles |                  37 |
| No. 1 doubles |                 184 |
| No. 2 doubles |                 393 |
| No. 3 doubles |                 375 |

The obvious route is mixed doubles into the main doubles lineup, but the progression reaches all the way to first singles.

The competitive results are even more notable.

Among the returning 2049 mixed entrants, in 2050:

* **191** reached the octofinals or better in a main draw.
* **88** reached a quarterfinal.
* **44** reached a semifinal.
* **27** reached a championship match.
* **15 became main-draw State champions.**

This included future champions at Jesuit, Cherry Hill North, Fletcher-Garrison Hall, Pacific Friends, Paul Robeson, Norfolk, Silver Lake and other programs.

Among the 20 underclass students who won a 2049 mixed championship, **16 reached the main draw the next year**.

Mixed doubles is therefore doing something unusually useful for the association: it gives JHSAA a statewide event in which tomorrow’s lineup can be seen before tomorrow arrives.

A sophomore who is tenth at her school is not simply “the tenth player.” She is potentially next year’s No. 2 doubles champion. A freshman mixed entrant may be a future first-singles player.

The data now demonstrates that those are not hypothetical pathways.

## The retention finding deserves continued attention

The original policy discussion included transfer pressure, so the mixed progression data should be examined in that context.

Of the 1,366 returning 2049 mixed entrants, 1,287 could be located on a 2050 JHSAA roster.

**1,280 remained at the same school.**

Among the 1,009 who progressed into the main individual championship, **1,003 made that progression at the same school**.

That is 99.4 percent of the mixed-to-main cohort.

One year of observational data cannot establish that the tournament caused those students to remain. We would not recommend making that claim publicly.

It does establish something more useful: **the pathway JHSAA hoped to create is actually occurring.**

Students are entering the lower-lineup championship, remaining with their schools and appearing one year later in the main championship.

The association should continue following this cohort over multiple seasons.

## The preseason date has become one of the event’s best features

The individual championship could have been placed after the team postseason.

That would have produced champions.

It would have produced much less value for the school season.

Holding the event in preseason means the results become information.

The entries are selected from the initial school ladder. The tournament then tests those rankings against statewide competition before dual play begins. Those results count toward the same ladder the school uses during the season.

That makes the event a statewide challenge ladder.

A player who enters as the nominal No. 3 and beats several stronger-seeded players can return to school having materially changed the evidence available to the coach.

The 2050 tournament produced **11,034 boys’ and girls’ main-draw matches**.

Of those, **2,643 were recorded as upsets relative to seeding: 24 percent.**

Twenty-one State champions began outside the top eight seeds.

That amount of movement matters. If the championship simply reproduced the preseason ordering, its value as a lineup tool would be limited. Instead, it is regularly revealing players and doubles combinations that outperform their initial placement.

This explains why a policy initially discussed primarily as an access measure has become popular with coaches at programs that did not have an access problem.

Elite coaches want the event too.

It gives them information.

## The event has not diminished team tennis

This was an understandable concern when the proposal was first discussed.

The early evidence suggests the opposite framing is more useful.

Team tennis answers a different question.

A school with two exceptional singles players and little depth can win two individual State championships and still finish 6-15. Canyonlands demonstrated exactly that in 2050.

That does not weaken the meaning of team State.

It explains it.

The team championship still rewards the complete program: lineup depth, doubles construction, player development, coaching and the ability to win across the card.

Individual State recognizes excellence that the team format deliberately cannot isolate.

The two championships therefore produce different forms of legitimacy.

The JHSAA should continue to state plainly that the **team championship is the association’s primary championship**. There is no need to diminish individual State to preserve that status.

## The access argument has been validated

The original criticism that “there are already plenty of tennis tournaments” was factually true and ultimately incomplete.

There are many tournaments for students who can access them.

School sports exist precisely because access through a school is different.

The JHSAA championship does not require a family to build an outside junior ranking. It does not require a player to spend the year traveling the private tournament circuit. It does not require membership in a particular club network.

A student earns the opportunity through the school program.

That distinction becomes particularly important outside Jefferson’s traditional tennis centers, in rural communities, at smaller schools, and among families for whom year-round tennis is financially or logistically unrealistic.

This is not an argument against junior tennis. The two systems can coexist.

Junior tournaments create additional competition for players who want and can access it.

JHSAA creates a common floor of opportunity.

## Scale after two years

The event is now large enough to be considered a major part of the association calendar.

In 2049:

* **18,326 students** participated across the main and mixed events.
* The championships produced **11,767 matches**.

In 2050:

* **18,579 students** participated.
* The championships produced **11,928 matches**.

Across those two seasons, JHSAA has provided nearly **24,000 individual-championship matches**.

The annual championship package currently awards:

* 72 boys main-draw titles
* 72 girls main-draw titles
* 12 mixed doubles titles

That is **156 State championships** across twelve classifications/groups.

The number sounds large because the association is large. The relevant question is whether the titles represent distinct competitive opportunities. The early evidence says they do.

## What JHSAA should preserve

Our recommendation is to resist the natural pressure to make the event more selective as its prestige grows.

The features responsible for its value are the features most likely to be “improved” away.

**Keep the main championship open to every school.** A district qualification system would recreate the team-State access problem inside the individual event.

**Keep it in preseason.** Moving the tournament later would preserve the championships while eliminating much of the ladder-setting and coaching value.

**Keep mixed doubles below the main nine.** Its developmental function depends on reserving the event for the students who did not receive one of the main positions.

**Do not require outside junior credentials.** Seeding can use JHSAA’s own competitive information. Access should continue to come through the school.

**Keep team State first in the institutional hierarchy.** Individual State works because it expands the meaning of a successful high-school tennis career, not because it replaces the team championship.

**Continue JV.** The individual tournament is not a solution to the entire participation problem. Roughly 42 percent of current rosters still record five or fewer *varsity* appearances. JV and the individual championships solve different problems and should be evaluated separately.

## What JHSAA should measure next

The association now has enough evidence to establish an annual evaluation rather than relying on anecdotes.

We recommend tracking:

* year-to-year mixed-to-main progression
* same-school retention among mixed entrants
* transfers by prior lineup position
* main-draw results from non-State schools
* champions and finalists from losing team programs
* freshmen and sophomores advancing to later rounds
* preseason ladder movement following the tournament
* first-time participation by schools and communities
* repeat participation and four-year individual championship careers
* differences by geography, school size and public/private status

The mixed cohort should become a standing metric.

“**Mixed to Main**” is unusually understandable and useful: what percentage of returning mixed participants occupy a main-draw position one year later?

The first observed figure is **74 percent**.

That belongs in future JHSAA annual reports.

## Media and communications opportunity

The association should also change how it communicates the event.

Championship coverage will naturally focus on winners. The larger public value is visible in the pathways.

There are recurring stories JHSAA can provide to media partners every year:

**From Mixed to Main:** players who entered mixed doubles last summer and have now moved into a main flight.

**State champions from non-State teams:** students whose school did not reach team State but who won an individual title.

**Home-school breakthroughs:** players who remained at their school and climbed from mixed or lower doubles into first singles or first doubles.

**Preseason risers:** low seeds whose tournament run materially moved them up the school ladder.

**First State experience:** schools that rarely qualify for team State but sent nine students into statewide championship competition.

Those stories explain the event better than a list of 156 champions ever could.

## Conclusion

The strongest argument for the Individual State Championships is no longer theoretical.

The tournament has created a statewide competitive opportunity that team State cannot provide without changing what team State is supposed to reward.

It gives great players on ordinary teams a State championship path.

It gives schools outside the traditional power structure guaranteed access to statewide competition.

It gives coaches meaningful preseason results before lineups harden.

It gives players below the main individual lineup a mixed doubles championship of their own.

And, most strikingly, it has already produced a visible developmental pipeline: **nearly three of every four returning mixed doubles entrants moved into the main Individual State tournament one year later, almost all at the same school.**

That last finding goes directly to the problem that brought the proposal forward.

JHSAA wanted a student to be able to look at a deep lineup, look at the success of another school, consider transferring — and still have a meaningful reason to stay.

The early evidence suggests Jefferson school tennis now offers one.
