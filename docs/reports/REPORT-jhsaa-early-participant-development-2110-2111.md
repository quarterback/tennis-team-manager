# JHSAA Six-Year Early-Participant Development Study, 2110–2111

**Status:** post-2111 development review  
**Scope:** players who participated in both 7th and 8th grade and later graduated through the JHSAA system

This report treats early participation as a development-model study rather than primarily a participation or transfer study. The question is not only whether middle-school players eventually made varsity. It is whether the game's development architecture produces distinct, plausible six-year careers: early stars, hidden-ceiling players, late bloomers, ordinary developers and players whose development continues through transfer.

The study uses the two graduating classes for which contemporaneous seventh- and eighth-grade exports and senior outcomes are available: 2110 and 2111. The final exports identify 1,009 seniors who had two early seasons. 925 can be directly traced back to the original seventh-grade export. The remaining 84 are excluded from trajectory calculations rather than reconstructed from missing snapshots.

## Executive findings

The median directly traceable six-year player began at **25 OVR in seventh grade**, **29 OVR in eighth grade** and finished at **51 OVR as a senior**.

Median seventh-to-senior gain was **+22 OVR**.

- 29% gained at least 30 OVR.
- 10.4% gained at least 40.
- 2.2% gained at least 50.
- Four players gained 60 or more.

The important result is that seventh-grade OVR is not destiny. It is current ability. The development model separately carries latent ceiling, accumulated development, the early-development rolls, later maturity and exposure.

The strongest proof case is Lamont Schultze: **29 OVR as a seventh-grader and 100 OVR at graduation**. That career was not an arbitrary +71 rating award. He entered with a much higher latent ceiling than his current ability, hit an unusually strong early-development outcome that raised that ceiling further, and then spent the rest of the career gradually realizing the new headroom.

The real graduating cohorts also reproduce the intended roll frequencies almost exactly:

- Accelerator hit rate: **20.1%**
- Exception hit rate: **3.6%**

The model target is roughly 20% accelerator and 4% exception.

The development system is therefore not producing one universal growth curve. It is producing several recognizable player biographies.

## Cohort-level development

For the 925 directly traceable players:

| | 7th grade | 8th grade | Senior |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Median OVR | 25 | 29 | **51** |
| Median ceiling | 48 | — | **62** |

The distribution of seventh-to-senior gain is wide rather than tightly centered around one annual increment.

That matters because a conventional sports-game progression system tends to make today's good player tomorrow's better player and today's bad player tomorrow's slightly less bad player. JHSAA's architecture permits current ability and eventual capacity to diverge.

## Lamont Schultze: the extreme proof case

Granite Basin, stayed all six seasons:

| Season | Grade | OVR | Ceiling | Record |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2106 | 7 | **29** | 74 | 12–9 |
| 2107 | 8 | **42** | **99** | 16–6 |
| 2109 | 10 | **86** | 99 | 17–8 |
| 2110 | 11 | **98** | 99 | 23–2 |
| 2111 | 12 | **100** | 100 | 23–3 |

His early-development total was **0.8958 of base ceiling**.

His seventh-grade roll was `d+a+e`: ordinary development, an accelerator and the rare exception. The eighth-grade roll was ordinary development.

The key transition is not 29 → 42. It is the ceiling move from **74 to 99 between seventh and eighth grade**. The later climb from 42 to 100 is the system realizing that headroom over multiple seasons.

He had no ninth-grade maturity bloom. He did not transfer.

Schultze is therefore close to a pure demonstration of the early-development model:

> low current ability + high latent capacity + rare early developmental event + time = elite senior.

## Lamont is not an isolated artifact

Other players show related but meaningfully different paths.

### Dustin Nguyen, Purcell Crossing

**29 → 32 → 59 → 80 → 92**

His original seventh-grade ceiling was only 53. A seventh-grade accelerator plus exception pushed the ceiling to 90 by eighth grade and eventually 95.

He stayed all six years and went 21–7 as a senior.

### Kaliq Locke, Isla Verde

**31 → 47 → 77 → 93 → 95**

Locke is particularly useful because he got **no accelerator and no exception**: `7:d;8:d`.

His early-development total was still .3253 and his ceiling moved from 64 into the mid-90s.

He later won the 2110 2A S2 individual title.

This is an ordinary-development late bloomer rather than a rare-event story.

### Emma Shupe, Ridgeline

**26 → 40 → 53 → 78 → 84**

Two accelerator hits, no exception. She stayed at Ridgeline.

Her early ceiling moved from 51 to 85.

### Lillian Washington, Fort Lassiter

**22 → 38 → 53 → 77 → 78**

Accelerator plus exception in seventh grade. Stayed all six years.

These careers are more useful validation examples than simply finding a player who started at 65 and became 95. They show that weak current ability can coexist with a materially different long-term path.

## Roll behavior in the graduating cohorts

Across the 1,850 observed early seasons:

- Accelerator hit rate: **20.1%**
- Exception hit rate: **3.6%**

The intended design is "development always, accelerator sometimes, exception rarely." The graduating cohort reproduces that shape.

Career results by early-roll history:

| Early roll history | Players | Median 7th OVR | Median senior OVR | Median gain |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Development only | 566 | 25 | 47.5 | +20 |
| Accelerator | 296 | 25 | 54 | +26 |
| Exception, no accelerator | 28 | 26 | 54 | +27.5 |
| Accelerator + exception | 35 | 28 | **69** | **+36** |

A quarter of the accelerator-plus-exception group finished 90+.

The relationship is large without making the rare result universal.

The early-development total has a **0.49 Spearman correlation** with seventh-to-senior OVR gain.

A descriptive regression controlling for seventh-grade OVR, seventh-grade ceiling, early match volume, ninth-grade bloom and transfer status finds that an additional **0.10 of early-development lift corresponds to about +2.9 senior OVR**. A ninth-grade maturity bloom corresponds to about +2.4 OVR.

This is descriptive rather than causal, but it indicates that the early-development variable is not simply tagging players who were already strong.

## Playing early matters independently of winning early

The median player accumulated 40 varsity flights across seventh and eighth grade.

Early match volume has a **0.52 correlation with total career OVR gain**.

Among players who began at 35 OVR or lower:

| Early varsity flights | Median senior OVR | Median career gain |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2–20 | 32 | +11 |
| 21–35 | 38 | +17 |
| 36–45 | 50 | +24 |
| 46+ | **61** | **+29.5** |

Their early winning percentage is much less predictive than simply receiving developmental exposure.

That distinction is important. The model is not saying "successful 13-year-olds become good seniors." It is closer to:

> time in the developmental environment creates more opportunity for capacity to convert.

## Ceiling movement

The 2110 graduating class is particularly useful because seventh-, eighth- and ninth-grade snapshots are all available.

For its 460 directly traceable players:

- Average seventh-grade ceiling: **49.3**
- Average ninth-grade ceiling: **61.7**
- Average increase: **+12.4**
- Correlation between early-development rolls and ceiling increase: **0.87**

That is a direct fingerprint of the mechanism rather than merely a result visible in senior OVR.

### Jordyn Rice

**56 OVR / 69 ceiling → 65 / 94 → 70 / 99 → 99 → 100**

Her early rolls were `7:d+a;8:d+a+e`, total lift .9458.

### Scarlett Irby

Irby is a necessary counterexample.

She had the largest early lift in the cohort, 1.1076, including two exceptions, but began at **20 OVR with only a 36 ceiling**.

She graduated at **53**, not 100.

The roll is therefore not a universal "become elite" token. It modifies the player who actually exists.

## The bottom usually stays the bottom

There were **262 players who were exactly 20 OVR in seventh grade**.

Their senior distribution:

- Median: 33
- 14 reached 50+
- 2 reached 60+
- none reached 70
- none reached 80 or 90

This is one of the strongest checks against uncontrolled inflation.

A genuinely poor seventh-grade player almost never becomes an elite player merely because they entered early.

Schultze is exceptional because 29 OVR concealed a 74 ceiling and then he hit a genuinely rare developmental combination.

## Transfer is doing a different job from development

Among all 1,009 two-year early seniors in the two graduating classes:

- 534 stayed at the same program for six years.
- 475 finished with less than six years of tenure at their final school.
- **47.1% transferred at least once.**

But the transfer market is not where elite development is primarily happening.

Among the 925 directly traceable players:

| | Stayed | Transferred |
|---|---:|---:|
| Median 7th OVR | 26 | 25 |
| Median senior OVR | 52 | 50 |
| Median gain | +22 | +23 |
| Finished 80+ | **16.6%** | 5.8% |
| Individual State title winner | **16 players** | 2 players |
| Median senior flights | 24 | 24 |
| 20+ senior flights | 84.5% | **91.0%** |

The transfer group develops by essentially the same median number of OVR points but contains far fewer eventual elite players.

What transfer changes is opportunity.

Transferred players were more likely to receive 20+ senior flights, although their median senior winning percentage was lower: 36% versus 42% for stayers.

The cleanest interpretation is:

> **Development creates players. Transfer creates roles.**

The elite tail disproportionately stays home. The transfer market prevents developed-but-nonelite players from getting trapped behind stronger teammates.

## Transfer timing

Final-school tenure also reveals the shape of the mobility system:

| Years at final school | Players | Typical interpretation | Median senior OVR |
|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 6 | 534 | never moved | 54 |
| 5 | 46 | moved during early participation | **76** |
| 4 | 340 | rising-freshman move shape | 50 |
| 3 | 8 | moved before sophomore year | 67.5 |
| 2 | 4 | moved before junior year | 70 |
| 1 | 77 | moved for senior year | 40 |

The 340-player tenure-four group is the clearest product of the rising-freshman portal shape: two early seasons at one school and then four high-school seasons elsewhere.

They are not, in aggregate, stars. Median senior OVR is 50. But **92% play at least 20 senior flights**.

In the 2110 class, where both eighth- and ninth-grade exports are available, 181 of 460 players — **39.3%** — changed schools across that boundary.

They were only 28 OVR at the median in eighth grade and finished at 48. Their median gain after eighth grade was +21.

The move did not make them elite. It gave an unfinished player somewhere to continue becoming a player.

## Career archetypes

The six-year system is now producing several distinct biographies rather than one progression curve.

| Player | Path | Development story |
|---|---|---|
| **Lamont Schultze** | Granite Basin, stayed | 29 → 42 → 86 → 98 → **100**; rare accelerator+exception, ceiling 74→99 |
| **Dustin Nguyen** | Purcell Crossing, stayed | 29 → 32 → 59 → 80 → **92**; ceiling 53→90 after seventh-grade event |
| **Kaliq Locke** | Isla Verde, stayed | 31 → 47 → 77 → 93 → **95**; no special roll |
| **Emma Shupe** | Ridgeline, stayed | 26 → 40 → 53 → 78 → **84**; two accelerators |
| **Emma King** | Barley Gap → North Wind | 40 → 50 → 82 → 89 → **97**; transferred early, payoff came later; senior 25–1 |
| **Kaleb Secor** | California Beach → Mesa Dorada | 35 → 36 → 48 → 71 → **92**; no accelerator or exception; freshman move |
| **Harrison Leedy** | Texas Beach → Porterfield | 56 → 57 → 94 → 99 → **100**; later move, finished elite |
| **Linda Świderski** | Coastal Christian → Ibarra → Chickasaw | 51 → 52 → 56 → 99 → **100**; ninth-grade maturity event, two transfers |
| **Tristan Taylor** | California Beach, stayed | 61 → 76 → 90 → 98 → **99**; three individual State titles |
| **Danielle Browning** | Purcell Crossing, stayed | 61 → 65 → 81 → 92 → **97**; won 2A D3 State as an eighth-grader |

These can be grouped into several broad types.

### Early phenom

Already unusually strong at 12–13 and playing substantial varsity tennis immediately.

Examples: Tristan Taylor, Danielle Browning.

Early participation gives these players a long career rather than fundamentally changing who they are.

### Hidden-ceiling player

Low current ability, much higher latent capacity, and an early event that materially increases the ceiling.

Examples: Lamont Schultze, Dustin Nguyen.

These are among the clearest successes of separating current ability from potential.

### Ordinary-roll late bloomer

No rare exception is required. The player simply has enough time and capacity to keep converting development.

Examples: Kaliq Locke, Kaleb Secor.

### Development-plus-mobility player

Early participation begins development, but the original school does not remain the best role. The player moves and continues developing elsewhere.

Examples: Emma King, Harrison Leedy.

### Later maturity player

The early seasons create runway, but the decisive jump occurs later through ninth-grade maturity or later development.

Example: Linda Świderski.

## What the study says about the model

The early-participation rule has become more than a roster-access rule.

It is a mechanism for revealing variance.

Two extra seasons create more opportunity for development to accumulate and more opportunity for the game's stochastic development events to matter. Those events do not simply grant a fixed boost. They alter the amount of headroom the career can eventually realize.

This creates several useful properties at once:

- early stars can have six-year careers;
- ordinary players usually remain ordinary;
- very weak players do not routinely become elite;
- hidden high-capacity players can emerge late;
- rare events create extreme careers without dominating the population;
- meaningful development can continue after transfer;
- transfer can solve role congestion without being the source of development itself.

The most important validation result is therefore not that some early participants became good.

It is that **29 → 100 exists while 20 → 100 remains essentially absent**.

That is evidence that the model can produce exceptional late bloomers without collapsing current ability, latent capacity and realized development into the same number.

## Association judgment

The development architecture should not be retuned on the basis of these graduating cohorts.

The observed accelerator and exception rates are already close to the intended design. The relationship between early-development rolls and later ceiling is strong. The senior distribution remains broad. Weak seventh-graders overwhelmingly remain weak or ordinary, while rare hidden-capacity players can produce exceptional outcomes.

The transfer rate also does not indicate a failure of the early-participation system. The evidence suggests the opposite: development and mobility are performing different functions.

Early participation buys time and developmental possibility. The development model determines how that possibility converts. Transfer determines where many of the resulting players can actually use it.
