# JHSAA Road to State: Pre–Semi-Conference 2A-1A Baseline

**Date:** August 16, 2026  
**Status:** Historical analysis / pre–Semi-Conference format  
**Season analyzed:** 2027  
**Classification:** 2A-1A girls and boys  
**Purpose:** Preserve a measurable baseline of the old 40-team Road to State before the Semi-Conference layer is applied, so the new format can be evaluated against the system it replaced.

## Executive summary

The 2027 2A-1A girls and boys seasons are useful control cases because both already used the expanded **40-team State field** before Semi-Conference existed.

The old structure successfully produced broad access without reducing State to a TOSS at-large tournament. Teams still had to win their final berth-bearing dual. It also produced real Cinderella outcomes: teams eliminated well before Regionals could return through Conference and prove they belonged once State began.

The weakness was not the existence of those late recovery opportunities. It was the **ordering and cost of access to Conference**.

Under the old format:

- 2A-1A girls had **137 programs** competing for 40 State places.
- 2A-1A boys had **126 programs** competing for 40 State places.
- Each Conference had **28 teams playing for 14 State berths**.
- Across both genders, **44 of 56 Conference entrants (78.6%)** had never reached Regionals.
- Those pre-Regional entrants won **23 of the 28 Conference berths (82.1%)**.
- As a result, **23 of the combined 80 State teams (28.8%)** had been eliminated before Regionals and later returned through Conference.

That is not automatically excessive. A 40-team State field is larger than the 32-team Regional field, so **at least eight State entrants per classification must come from outside Regionals** unless Regionals itself is expanded. Some form of deep recovery is therefore mathematically required.

The old problem was that a Ward, Sectional, or even Area loser could be placed directly into a **berth-bearing Conference dual**, while a team that had advanced into Regionals and then survived several recovery rounds could arrive at the same match only after playing much more tennis.

The new Semi-Conference format should be judged primarily on whether it fixes that ordering problem without destroying the useful breadth of the 40-team field.

---

## Format under review

This report describes the format **before Semi-Conference was added**.

The relevant ladder was:

`Areas → Sectionals → Wards → Regionals → Zonals`

with recovery:

`Super Regionals → Semi-State → Divisionals → Conference`

The 40-team State field was built from:

- **8 Zonal champions**
- **12 Semi-State winners**
- **6 Divisional winners**
- **14 Conference winners**

Conference therefore supplied **35% of the entire State field**.

The old Conference pool combined:

- Divisional losers;
- selected Ward losers;
- selected Sectional losers;
- and, where the pool needed additional bodies, Area losers.

The key historical defect was that Conference was itself berth-bearing. A team selected from an early ladder loss did not have to win an intermediate qualifying match before facing a Divisional loser for State.

# 2A-1A Girls

## Field size

- Programs: **137**
- State field: **40**
- State participation rate: **29.2%**
- Regional field: **32**
- Minimum number of State teams that must come from outside Regionals: **8**

## Recovery shape

The old girls' recovery path was:

- 16 Regional losers
- 8 Super Regional duals
- 24-team Semi-State field
- 12 Semi-State duals
- 12-team Divisional field
- 6 Divisional duals
- 28-team Conference
- 14 Conference winners to State

In this particular season, the recovery arithmetic did **not** strand any Super Regional or Semi-State loser before the next recovery stage. All eight Super Regional losers were readmitted to Semi-State, and all twelve Semi-State losers appeared in Divisionals.

That makes the girls' season a useful clean baseline: the later Semi-Conference fix was not needed here to rescue an omitted recovery team, but the old Conference-access asymmetry is still visible.

## Conference composition

| Last previous loss | Conference entrants | Conference winners | Win rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Divisional | 6 | 3 | 50.0% |
| Ward | 8 | 4 | 50.0% |
| Sectional | 14 | 7 | 50.0% |
| **Total** | **28** | **14** | **50.0%** |

Thus:

- **22 of 28 Conference entrants (78.6%)** had never reached Regionals.
- **11 of 14 Conference berths (78.6%)** went to teams that had never reached Regionals.
- Those 11 teams represented **27.5% of the entire 40-team State field**.

## Case study: Huckle Butte

Huckle Butte is the best argument for preserving broad recovery.

- TOSS rank: **55**
- Initial postseason exit: **Sectionals**
- Conference: won
- State seed: **40**
- State result: **Octofinalist**

Huckle Butte did not merely occupy a State berth. It won the Qualifiers Round, won the First Round, and reached the final 16.

The lesson is important: a team eliminated early in the geographic ladder can still be a legitimate State-level team. The reform should not assume that an early loss proves non-competitiveness.

What was questionable was that Huckle Butte went directly from a Sectional loss to a berth-bearing Conference opportunity. Semi-Conference adds the missing proof point without removing the possibility of this kind of run.

## Case study: Ninemile

Ninemile shows the opposite side of the old system.

- TOSS rank: **22**
- District champion
- Lost Regional
- Lost Super Regional
- Readmitted to Semi-State
- Lost Semi-State
- Selected into Divisionals
- Lost Divisional
- Won Conference
- State seed: **29**

Ninemile accumulated **four postseason losses before finally winning the dual that put it into State**.

That is not necessarily a defect by itself. The old Road to State was explicitly a repeated-opportunity qualification system rather than strict single elimination.

The inequity was comparative: Ninemile had to survive repeated recovery selection after advancing deeply enough to reach Regionals, while some Sectional losers entered the same berth-bearing Conference without first winning any recovery dual.

# 2A-1A Boys

## Field size

- Programs: **126**
- State field: **40**
- State participation rate: **31.7%**
- Regional field: **32**
- Minimum number of State teams that must come from outside Regionals: **8**

The boys' classification therefore sent nearly one-third of all programs to State. That makes it an especially useful stress test of whether a 40-team championship can remain meaningful while maintaining a broad recovery ladder.

## Recovery shape

The boys' recovery shape was the same:

- 16 Regional losers
- 8 Super Regional duals
- 24-team Semi-State field
- 12 Semi-State duals
- 12-team Divisional field
- 6 Divisional duals
- 28-team Conference
- 14 Conference winners to State

As with the girls, this simulation did not strand a recovery team before the next recovery stage. Every Super Regional loser returned at Semi-State, and every Semi-State loser reached Divisionals.

## Conference composition

| Last previous loss | Conference entrants | Conference winners | Win rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Divisional | 6 | 2 | 33.3% |
| Ward | 8 | 5 | 62.5% |
| Sectional | 11 | 4 | 36.4% |
| Area | 3 | 3 | 100.0% |
| **Total** | **28** | **14** | **50.0%** |

Thus:

- **22 of 28 Conference entrants (78.6%)** had never reached Regionals.
- **12 of 14 Conference berths (85.7%)** went to teams that had never reached Regionals.
- Those 12 teams represented **30.0% of the entire State field**.

The boys' example makes the old access problem more obvious than the girls' example because Conference reached all the way back to **Area losers**.

## Case study: Assay

Assay demonstrates both the value and the weakness of the old system.

- TOSS rank: **39**
- Initial postseason exit: **Area**
- Conference: beat St. Francis Xavier College Prep
- State seed: **33**
- State Qualifiers Round: beat Archbishop Quinlan
- Reached the State First Round

Assay proved that its Conference inclusion was not absurd. It beat a Regional-level opponent to qualify for State and then won again once State began.

But Assay also illustrates the access-cost problem perfectly: an Area loser was allowed to move directly into a match worth a State berth.

Under Semi-Conference, the desirable part of this story can remain. Assay can still play its way back into contention. It simply has to **win once to earn the Conference seat before winning again to earn State**.

## Case study: Haddon Siding vs. Jessup Bar

This matchup is the strongest illustration of the old hierarchy failure.

Haddon Siding:

- TOSS rank: **36**
- **District champion**
- Entered at Regionals
- Lost Regional
- Lost Super Regional
- Lost Semi-State
- Lost Divisional
- Reached Conference

Jessup Bar:

- TOSS rank: **35**
- Lost in an **Area**
- Entered Conference directly

They eventually met in Conference, where Jessup Bar won 3-2 and took the State opportunity.

There is nothing wrong with Jessup Bar beating Haddon Siding. The result should stand.

The issue is how each team earned the right to play that berth-bearing dual.

Haddon Siding had already demonstrated district-level success, Regional qualification, and repeated survival in the recovery selection hierarchy. Jessup Bar had one Area loss and then received direct access to the same State-deciding round.

Semi-Conference corrects that without insulating Haddon Siding from competition: Jessup Bar must now win its way into parity first.

# Combined findings

Across the two 2A-1A fields:

| Metric | Girls | Boys | Combined |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Programs | 137 | 126 | 263 |
| State berths | 40 | 40 | 80 |
| Conference entrants | 28 | 28 | 56 |
| Conference berths | 14 | 14 | 28 |
| Divisional-loser Conference entrants | 6 | 6 | 12 |
| Pre-Regional Conference entrants | 22 | 22 | 44 |
| Divisional-loser Conference winners | 3 | 2 | 5 |
| Pre-Regional Conference winners | 11 | 12 | 23 |

Several conclusions follow.

## 1. Broad recovery is required by the field geometry

A 40-team State tournament cannot be filled entirely by a 32-team Regional field.

Even if **every single Regional participant** eventually qualified, eight additional State teams would still have to come from outside Regionals.

Therefore the existence of Ward-or-earlier recovery is not an accidental generosity. It is built into the relationship between the field sizes.

The design question is not:

> Should teams eliminated before Regionals ever reach State?

They must, unless the Regional field grows.

The real question is:

> What should a team eliminated earlier have to prove before it is allowed to contest a State berth?

## 2. The old Conference was doing too many jobs at once

Conference simultaneously served as:

- the final chance for Divisional losers;
- a recovery outlet for Ward losers;
- a recovery outlet for Sectional losers;
- and, when necessary, a recovery outlet for Area losers.

All of those teams entered the same **berth-bearing** round.

That flattened distinctions the rest of the Road to State had spent several stages establishing.

## 3. Early-ladder Conference teams were not obviously weak

The data does not support removing them simply because they lost early.

Examples such as Huckle Butte, Linden, Mission Ridge, and Assay won additional State matches after recovering.

The old system was therefore detecting some teams that the initial ladder had misclassified through a single result.

That is evidence for retaining recovery breadth.

## 4. The problem was the cost of re-entry

The old format asked too little of its most distant recovery entrants.

A Divisional loser had accumulated a long postseason record before Conference.

A Sectional or Area loser could sometimes arrive at Conference without a single recovery win.

The same State berth was therefore being contested by teams with radically different demonstrated postseason résumés.

Semi-Conference is best understood as a **price-of-re-entry correction**.

## 5. Rating should buy another match, not a berth

The old and new systems share an important principle worth preserving:

**TOSS/ATR can decide who receives another opportunity, but the team still has to win.**

The strong outcomes from low-seeded State qualifiers support this. Selection by rating can identify a plausible team the bracket discarded, but the court remains the final test.

---

# What Semi-Conference is intended to fix

The new format should not be evaluated merely by whether it changes which schools reach State.

Its more important job is preserving the ordering of postseason claims.

The intended hierarchy is:

1. **Divisional losers** proceed directly to Conference.
2. **District champions still outside the field** receive priority for Semi-Conference.
3. **Teams stranded by recovery arithmetic** — for example, a Super Regional loser not readmitted to Semi-State or a Semi-State loser not selected into Divisionals — are considered before the system reaches backward.
4. Only after those claims are exhausted does the system use the **top remaining Ward-level pool** to fill Semi-Conference.
5. Semi-Conference winners earn the right to face the Divisional losers in Conference.
6. Conference winners take the final State berths.

The core rule is:

> **A team the ladder dropped later should never be skipped for a team it dropped earlier.**

The old format could violate that rule because it built the final Conference pool directly from available bodies. The new round creates an explicit place to reconcile those claims before the berth-bearing match.

# Metrics to compare after Semi-Conference lands

The next full simulation should be compared against this baseline on more than just the final 40 names.

## Conference composition

Record:

- Divisional losers entering Conference;
- Semi-Conference winners entering Conference;
- original elimination stage of every Semi-Conference winner;
- number of district champions still alive at Semi-Conference;
- number of stranded Super Regional / Semi-State teams rescued by Semi-Conference.

Expected invariant:

> No Ward-or-earlier team reaches Conference directly.

## State-field provenance

For each State qualifier, record the deepest main-ladder stage reached before recovery:

- Zonal champion;
- Zonal loser;
- Regional loser;
- Ward loser;
- Sectional loser;
- Area loser.

The old 2A-1A baseline is:

| Provenance | Girls | Boys |
|---|---:|---:|
| Never reached Regionals but made State through Conference | 11 | 12 |
| Share of State field | 27.5% | 30.0% |

The new format does not necessarily need these numbers to fall dramatically. A similar number could be healthy if those teams now have to win Semi-Conference first.

## Recovery burden

For every State qualifier, record:

- postseason wins before State;
- postseason losses before State;
- number of recovery duals played;
- earliest loss;
- final berth-bearing win.

This is important because the old system could produce extreme paths such as Ninemile's repeated losses while simultaneously admitting an Area loser directly into Conference.

The goal should not be identical paths. The goal should be **legible proportionality**.

## Advancement after qualification

Track State results by qualification route.

Questions worth answering:

- Do Semi-Conference/Conference qualifiers win State matches?
- How often do they reach the Octofinals?
- Are they overwhelmingly eliminated in the Qualifiers Round?
- Does the new extra win filter out genuine noncompetitive teams, or merely add process?

The pre-Semi-Conference data suggests some early-ladder recovery teams are genuinely State-caliber. If the new format eliminates nearly all of them, that may indicate the correction became too punitive.

## Rating versus postseason evidence

Compare TOSS/ATR rank with:

- deepest main-ladder stage;
- recovery entry point;
- State qualification;
- State finish.

The new hierarchy should ensure that rating resolves choices **within a stage or claim tier** rather than allowing a highly rated early loser to jump a team that advanced farther.

# Baseline judgment

The pre-Semi-Conference 40-team format was not fundamentally broken.

It had three strong ideas:

1. A single upset should not necessarily end the season of a credible team.
2. Ratings may award another opportunity but never a State berth by themselves.
3. A 40-team field should allow a broad range of teams to prove themselves on court.

The data supports all three.

Its weakness was that the final recovery pool did not sufficiently preserve the hierarchy created by the rounds before it. Conference became a common endpoint for teams whose postseason claims were not actually equivalent.

The boys' 2A-1A example makes this especially clear: an Area loser could arrive directly at the same State-deciding round as a district champion that had subsequently lost in Regionals, Super Regionals, Semi-State, and Divisionals.

Semi-Conference is therefore not primarily an expansion of the postseason. It is an **ordering layer**.

It inserts one earned win between an early-ladder loss and the final berth-bearing Conference, while also creating a place to rescue teams that the recovery arithmetic previously stranded.

That should preserve the useful openness of the 40-team system while making the path into the last 14 berths materially more defensible.
