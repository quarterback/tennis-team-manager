# 2078 JHSAA Transfer Policy

**Status:** governing transfer-policy doctrine for the 2078 offseason and the implementation baseline for future automated JHSAA transfer-market work.

This document supersedes the 2075 transfer doctrine where the two differ. The 2075 document remains useful historical context, especially its separation of ordinary congestion clearing from elite-player rescue, but 2078 adds a third problem that the older doctrine did not model explicitly: **role congestion among established varsity players**.

The 2078 policy is not a parity mechanism. It is not a generic redistribution engine. It is not permission to strip elite programs of talent or to dump upper-classification reserves into 1A.

The governing principle is:

> **Experienced players should have a meaningful competitive role somewhere in the association, while strong source programs must retain enough internal depth to remain strong and weak receiving programs should gain players who replace genuinely weak or vacant roles rather than displace already-good incumbents.**

The market therefore operates through three distinct pathways:

1. deep-roster congestion clearing;
2. varsity role liberation;
3. narrow elite-player rescue.

A fourth mechanism — concentrated rebuild hosts — is a destination strategy used mainly by the first two pathways.

---

## 1. Deep-roster congestion clearing

The ordinary clearing market still begins with players who are too good for the role available to them at their current school.

The 2078 eligibility bands are classification-specific.

### Wide-roster / wide-card classifications

For **9A, 8A, 7A, and Group 1**, inspect the deep reserve population beginning roughly around:

> **rank 15 through 20+**

These classifications legitimately carry deeper programs and, in the wide-card groups, need more bodies for 4S/5D postseason tennis. A player at No. 12 or No. 13 is not automatically congested.

The strongest concern is the upper tail of talent at ranks 17-20+ where a player is clearly capable of being a varsity regular elsewhere.

### Every other classification

For **6A, 5A, 4A, 3A, 2A, 1A, Group 2, and Group 3**, inspect congestion beginning roughly around:

> **rank 12 through 16+**

These programs have narrower meaningful varsity demand, so the point at which strong talent becomes stranded arrives earlier.

### Rank is only a screen

A player should not transfer merely because they fall inside one of those rank bands.

The actual question is:

> **Is this player's ability substantially above the normal talent level for that classification's deep reserve/JV population, and can another program give them a meaningful varsity role?**

A correctly placed freshman at No. 18 is not a problem. An experienced 9A player at No. 18 whose OVR would make them No. 5 in much of 7A is.

---

## 2. The market should cascade downward rather than teleport players

The market should strengthen varsity tennis across the classification ladder, not simply remove talent from elite-program JV and deposit it at the bottom.

For a deep-roster candidate, destination search should proceed in this order:

1. same classification;
2. one classification down;
3. two classifications down;
4. farther downward only when no meaningful varsity seat exists above.

The lower classifications, including 1A and Group 3, are valid terminal clearing markets. A player may eventually land there. But they should normally reach the bottom only after higher levels have failed to provide a real role.

This allows talent to propagate through the association:

- a blocked 9A player fills a weak 8A varsity seat;
- the 8A player displaced by that arrival may then fit naturally in 6A;
- the 6A player displaced by that move may fit in 5A or 4A;
- the cascade can continue until every selected player has a useful role.

The purpose is not to preserve every incumbent's existing spot. It is to move the association toward a more appropriate distribution of talent by role.

---

## 3. JV should remain, but its upper tail should look different

The objective is **not** to eliminate JV or reserve tennis.

Healthy JV populations should increasingly contain:

- newly generated freshmen;
- younger developmental players;
- high-potential players not yet ready to displace experienced varsity players;
- ordinary depth appropriate to the classification.

The problem is when experienced, already-developed players remain on JV despite being plainly good enough to start somewhere else.

The desired result is therefore:

> **The No. 15-20 players in the biggest programs should increasingly be younger/lower-current-OVR developmental players rather than experienced varsity-caliber players who have nowhere to go.**

Future reports should measure this directly through:

- OVR at ranks 15-20 in 9A/8A/7A/Group 1;
- OVR at ranks 12-16 in the other classifications;
- 90th and 95th percentile JV OVR;
- grade distribution of the JV upper tail;
- number of rising juniors/seniors remaining below meaningful varsity use;
- number of freshmen/sophomores promoted into roles vacated by transfers.

---

## 4. Role congestion is a separate transfer problem

The 2075 doctrine treated varsity access as the primary opportunity problem. The 2077 data showed that this is incomplete.

A player can already be a varsity regular and still be badly placed.

The clearest cases are experienced players on elite programs who spend multiple seasons in lower doubles roles even though their absolute ability would make them a high singles player elsewhere.

Examples of role congestion include:

- D5/D4 player capable of S1 or S2 at another program;
- D3 player capable of S1/S2 one or two classifications down;
- D2 player with almost no singles access despite an OVR comparable to another classification's typical S1;
- upper-varsity player whose projected senior role is essentially unchanged after several years in the same secondary role.

This is not the same problem as JV congestion.

The governing question is:

> **Has this player earned the opportunity to be seen in a different competitive context?**

---

## 5. Age and career stage matter heavily for role transfers

Role-liberation transfers should focus primarily on experienced players.

### Rising seniors

Rising seniors receive the strongest presumption toward role liberation.

If a senior has already spent substantial time in lower doubles or fringe varsity roles and has the ability to become a high singles player elsewhere, the market should actively search for that opportunity.

A senior should not spend a fourth year at D4 merely because the source program remains deep enough to keep them there.

### Rising juniors

Rising juniors are also legitimate candidates when the current program's roster makes a substantially better future role unlikely.

The market should be more selective than with seniors, but it should not require a player to wait until their last season before acknowledging that the role ceiling is structural.

### Freshmen and sophomores

Younger players should generally remain in the development pipeline unless their mismatch is extreme.

A sophomore at No. 16 on a major 9A program may still have a credible path upward. The same placement for a rising senior is a different problem.

---

## 6. A role transfer must improve role, not merely change school

A role-congested player should not transfer merely to remain a lower doubles player somewhere else.

The destination should create a material role improvement, such as:

- D5 -> S2;
- D4 -> S1/S2;
- D3 -> S1/S2;
- D2 -> S1;
- fringe varsity -> permanent upper-lineup starter;
- occasional starter -> centerpiece of a weaker program.

The precise court is not an iron rule, because team construction and doubles specialization still matter. But the transfer must plausibly improve the player's competitive responsibility.

A player already receiving substantial varsity use is only a successful role transfer when their role changes materially.

---

## 7. Source-program integrity is a hard constraint

A transfer market that takes eight or nine useful players from one strong team and leaves that team hollow is a failure.

The source program must be evaluated after every proposed departure.

A role transfer is permitted only when the source has a credible internal replacement chain.

That can include:

- a younger high-potential JV player ready to move upward;
- an upper-JV player whose current ability is close enough to absorb the vacated doubles role;
- an existing varsity player who can move into the vacated court while another younger player enters the lineup;
- sufficient remaining depth that the source remains recognizably the same caliber of program.

The replacement does **not** need to be equally good. Some source decline is acceptable and natural. The policy is not designed to freeze elite programs at peak strength.

The constraint is that the source should not be dismantled.

### Operational donor limits

For the 2078 manual class:

- role-transfer donors were generally capped at **two** such departures;
- no program was allowed to lose more than **four total players** across all transfer pathways;
- source rosters were recalculated as departures accumulated.

Future automated versions should preserve this principle even if the exact numeric limits change.

The preferred implementation is dynamic rather than a fixed cap:

1. propose one departure;
2. rebuild/re-rank the source;
3. identify the next internal replacement;
4. determine whether another departure remains safe;
5. stop when another move would materially damage the source lineup rather than expose legitimate internal depth.

---

## 8. Internal promotion is part of the policy, not a side effect

A successful transfer can deliberately create an opportunity at the source school.

Example:

- an experienced 9A D4 player transfers to become 6A S1;
- a high-potential sophomore moves from 9A JV into the vacated D4 slot;
- the source remains strong;
- the older player receives a career-defining role;
- the younger player receives developmentally appropriate promotion.

That is a desirable outcome.

The market therefore should measure not only the mover's destination result but also:

- who inherited the mover's source role;
- the replacement player's grade;
- replacement OVR/POT;
- whether the source remained competitively credible;
- whether the move converted a younger JV player into useful varsity participation.

---

## 9. Concentrated rebuild hosts

The 2077 data showed that many weak 7A/8A/9A programs have large numbers of genuinely replaceable lower-varsity positions.

For those programs, a single incoming player often changes little. A concentrated cohort can change the program's competitive context entirely.

The 2078 policy therefore permits selected weak programs to absorb **large multi-school transfer cohorts**, potentially seven, eight, nine, or ten arrivals, provided the destination roster actually has that much replaceable capacity.

The cohort should be assembled from many donor programs, not stripped from one source.

A plausible rebuild class might contain:

- one role-blocked player from an elite 9A program;
- one from another 9A/8A power;
- several high-end reserve players from other deep programs;
- a few players cascading from adjacent classifications.

The result should be a newly coherent varsity core at the receiving school.

### What qualifies as a rebuild host

A host should generally be:

- a poor or chronically poor program in 7A, 8A, or 9A;
- carrying several weak players in meaningful varsity positions;
- capable of replacing those weak positions with incoming players without displacing an existing strong core;
- large/deep enough institutionally to plausibly carry the additional roster;
- not already a contender simply looking to stack more talent.

The policy prefers **replaceable lower varsity** over open roster spots alone.

A weak program whose No. 7-14 players are far below classification norms is a much better rebuild host than a good program with one nominal opening.

### Purpose of concentrated rebuilds

This mechanism creates a different transfer narrative:

> **I can remain D3/D4 at one of the best programs in Jefferson, or I can become S1/S2 and help build a weak upper-classification program into something competitive.**

That is neither congestion clearing nor elite rescue in the old sense. It is an opportunity to become central to a program.

---

## 10. Rebuild cohorts must not merely stack already-good teams

Do not send role-congested players to strong programs simply because those programs can use them.

The receiving-side question is:

> **Does this player replace a genuinely weak or vacant role, or are they merely pushing an already-good player into JV?**

For rebuild hosts, incoming players should mostly replace players who are materially weaker than the classification's competitive norm.

This is what permits a host to take a large cohort without recreating congestion immediately.

Future implementation should score destination capacity using the actual strength of ranks roughly 5-14, not only total roster size.

---

## 11. The cascade must recalculate both donor and destination

Every move changes two rosters.

After each move or batch:

### Recalculate the source

- who moves into the vacated varsity role?
- does a younger player rise from JV?
- is another experienced player no longer congested because their path just opened?
- is the source still strong enough that another departure is safe?

### Recalculate the destination

- where does the arrival actually rank?
- what court/role becomes plausible?
- which incumbent is displaced?
- is that incumbent now a legitimate next-stage transfer candidate?
- did the arrival merely recreate the same problem one school lower?

The market is therefore inherently iterative.

A correct future implementation should not build the entire transfer slate against frozen preseason ladders.

---

## 12. Elite-player rescue remains separate

The narrow elite-rescue market from the 2075 doctrine still exists.

Its question remains:

> **The player is already a star, but the program cannot provide an appropriate competitive environment.**

That is distinct from:

- deep-roster congestion;
- role congestion;
- rebuild-host placement.

Elite rescue should remain rare.

Junior and historic-career retention remain important:

- established juniors have a strong presumption toward staying;
- multiple POYs, State S1 titles, repeated All-State careers, and other major legacy markers should strongly anchor a player to the source school;
- legendary careers at weak programs are desirable and should remain possible.

Do not use elite rescue to erase those stories.

---

## 13. Exchange students are excluded from the transfer market

Real exchange students are one-season JHSAA arrivals and leave automatically after that season.

They are not transfer candidates.

Do not infer exchange status from a foreign flag alone: ordinary Jefferson players can also carry non-US nationality markers.

Any automated transfer-market implementation must identify/exclude true exchange-student seats using the actual exchange-generation rule or equivalent stable identity logic, not nationality.

---

## 14. 2078 manual implementation baseline

The manually constructed 2078 class used this policy as an initial implementation experiment.

It contained **839 total moves**:

- **768 deep-congestion clearing moves**;
- **71 role-liberation / rebuild-host moves**;
- **30 selected weak 7A/8A/9A rebuild programs**;
- role-transfer donors generally capped at two such departures;
- no program allowed to lose more than four total players;
- exchange-student proxies excluded;
- graduating seniors excluded;
- wide-class congestion screens beginning around No. 15+;
- other-class congestion screens beginning around No. 12+.

These counts are not permanent quotas. They are the 2078 test class.

Future agents should preserve the governing mechanics and re-derive the counts from each season's actual population.

---

## 15. What 2078 is testing

The 2078 experiment should answer several separate questions.

### A. Can the association reduce role congestion, not merely JV congestion?

Measure how many experienced players move from lower doubles/fringe roles into high singles or upper-lineup roles elsewhere.

### B. Can strong donor programs absorb those departures internally?

Measure whether younger high-potential players inherit the vacated roles without catastrophic source-program decline.

### C. Can concentrated cohorts rebuild bad 7A/8A/9A programs?

Measure whether selected hosts materially improve:

- top-11 strength;
- ranks 5-14 strength;
- win percentage;
- TOSS rank;
- district competitiveness;
- postseason qualification/performance.

### D. Does the cascade improve the association's talent distribution?

Measure whether the upper JV/reserve tail gets younger and lower-OVR while lower classifications gain stronger varsity depth.

### E. Does destination congestion simply reappear?

Track incumbents displaced by incoming transfers and whether those players subsequently obtain appropriate roles.

---

## 16. Required reporting after the 2078 season

A proper post-2078 report should include at least:

### Movers

- total returning movers;
- destination rank;
- varsity appearances;
- JV appearances;
- primary court before and after;
- singles appearances before and after;
- share receiving a material role upgrade;
- movement distance by classification;
- grade distribution.

### Donors

- departures per source program;
- source top-11/top-14 strength before vs after;
- replacement player's identity, grade, OVR and POT;
- whether a younger JV player inherited varsity use;
- source win/TOSS change;
- programs materially damaged by the market.

### Rebuild hosts

- cohort size;
- incoming-player origins;
- pre/post ranks 5-14 strength;
- pre/post top-11 strength;
- win/TOSS change;
- district/state outcome;
- number of existing strong players displaced;
- number of incoming players who actually became major varsity contributors.

### Association-wide

- p90/p95 JV OVR by classification;
- OVR distribution at ranks 15-20 in wide classes;
- OVR distribution at ranks 12-16 elsewhere;
- grade distribution of those reserve bands;
- percentage of rising seniors still trapped below meaningful roles;
- number of experienced doubles-only players remaining on elite programs despite S1/S2-equivalent ability elsewhere.

---

## 17. What future agents should optimize

For 2078 and beyond, optimize in this order:

1. identify genuinely misplaced experienced players, not merely deep roster ranks;
2. liberate rising juniors/seniors from structurally capped roles where a materially better role exists elsewhere;
3. clear truly varsity-caliber players from deep reserve/JV positions;
4. prefer same-class, one-down, or two-down meaningful roles before cascading farther;
5. use 1A/Group 3 as legitimate terminal clearing markets when necessary;
6. protect donor programs through dynamic internal-backfill checks;
7. promote younger/high-potential players into vacated source roles where appropriate;
8. favor weak destinations with genuinely replaceable lower varsity over already-good teams;
9. permit concentrated rebuild cohorts when a weak 7A/8A/9A program has enough replaceable capacity;
10. recalculate source and destination after every meaningful transfer batch;
11. keep elite rescue separate and rare;
12. retain juniors/legacy stars when their accumulated school history makes staying meaningful;
13. exclude true exchange students entirely;
14. evaluate role quality and roster distribution before looking at parity or raw mover counts.

The transfer market is now best understood as a **career-role and roster-clearing system**.

Its purpose is not merely to answer, "Can this player make varsity somewhere?"

It should answer:

> **Given this player's age, ability, current role, and the association's available roster capacity, where can they have the most meaningful remaining high-school career without needlessly damaging the program they leave or displacing an equally deserving player at the program they join?**

That is the governing 2078 doctrine unless the owner explicitly changes it.
