# 2070 Season Review

## Outcomes, players, and the 8A/9A format change

**Prepared for the JHSAA Executive Board**  
**Scope:** All 24 classification-genders, 2070 JHSAA research exports  
**Match-level scope:** ~173,000 varsity flights across boys and girls  
**Purpose:** First-year review of the 8A/9A move to 4S/5D, with 2069 as the reform-year baseline

> **Reconciliation note.** This report merges two independent analyst reviews and reconciles them against the raw 2070 export. In particular, the 8A/9A State sample is **78 boys duals and 78 girls duals**, all with nine flights. That produces one-flight-margin rates of **39.7% boys / 32.1% girls**.

## Executive summary

2070 was the first clean test of the JHSAA's new large-school postseason model. The 2069 gap-response curve and Epiregional seeding reform remained in place, transfers were largely left alone, and 8A/9A moved from 1S/4D to 4S/5D for the postseason and its rehearsal window.

The central finding is straightforward:

**4S/5D worked.**

It changed actual winners, rewarded deeper rosters rather than stars, substantially erased the advantage of top-heavy roster construction, expanded championship participation, and still produced a large number of close duals. At the same time, it exposed a new measurement issue: the regular-season rating system does not yet fully capture the kind of team that the 4S/5D postseason rewards.

The rest of the association also continued to stabilize around the 2069 reforms. The favorite-win curve reproduced almost exactly. Team championships became even more concentrated among elite seeds. The Epiregional remained functional. And JV continued its shift from a senior consolation structure toward a true developmental circuit.

The recommendation is therefore:

**Keep 4S/5D. Keep the match curve. Keep the JV structure. Do not intervene heavily in the transfer market yet. Use 2071 to refine how JHSAA measures 8A/9A team strength.**

## I. Verification of the 2070 change

The format change was correctly scoped.

- 8A and 9A postseason duals used **4 singles / 5 doubles**
- league play remained **3S/4D**
- other classifications retained their existing postseason formats
- 1A retained its separate pilot
- TOC remained **1S/4D**
- Individual State remained unchanged

Across the full season, the export contains:

- **630 boys 4S/5D duals**
- **631 girls 4S/5D duals**

Those include the early rehearsal window plus the full 8A/9A postseason.

At State specifically:

- **78 boys duals**
- **78 girls duals**

Every one of those State duals contained all nine flights.

There was no leakage of the format into classes that were not supposed to use it.

## II. The match curve passed its second-year test

The 2069 gap-response curve was not a one-year calibration accident. It reproduced itself in 2070.

| OVR gap | Boys 2069 | Boys 2070 | Girls 2069 | Girls 2070 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0–3 | 55.1% | 54.4% | 54.6% | 54.4% |
| 4–6 | 63.3% | 63.4% | 63.0% | 64.0% |
| 7–9 | 72.1% | 71.9% | 72.3% | 72.4% |
| 10–12 | 81.0% | 80.4% | 80.4% | 80.8% |
| 13–15 | 87.1% | 87.6% | 87.6% | 87.9% |
| 16–18 | 91.4% | 93.4% | 93.0% | 92.5% |
| 19–21 | 95.9% | 96.0% | 96.3% | 95.7% |
| 22–24 | 97.1% | 98.0% | 97.8% | 97.9% |
| 25–27 | 99.0% | 98.8% | 99.2% | 98.8% |

The practical conclusion is that the match engine is now stable.

A modest OVR gap matters. A large gap matters a great deal. Huge-gap upsets are rare. But near-peers remain genuinely competitive.

The 2069 three-set finding also held: **who is more likely to win and how long the match lasts are not the same thing at ordinary OVR gaps.** Favorites become more reliable well before matches become routinely short.

There is no evidence here that the favorite-win curve needs further tuning.

## III. The format change altered actual winners

This is the clearest first-year test.

Because 1S/4D is contained inside 4S/5D, every 2070 large-school dual can be replayed as a clean counterfactual:

**What would the result have been if only S1 and D1–D4 counted?**

The answer:

| | All 4S/5D duals | 8A/9A State |
|---|---:|---:|
| Boys | **98 of 630 changed winner (15.6%)** | **14 of 78 (17.9%)** |
| Girls | **99 of 631 changed winner (15.7%)** | **14 of 78 (17.9%)** |

Roughly **one in six** large-school duals had a different winner because the additional singles positions and D5 existed.

At State, it was nearly **one in five**.

That makes the policy impact unambiguous:

**4S/5D is not a cosmetic access expansion. It materially changes competitive outcomes.**

---

## IV. The format rewarded depth and reduced star dependence

The preseason premise was that a nine-flight postseason should make the strength of the whole roster matter more and make one elite player matter less.

That happened.

Among teams with at least three 4S/5D duals:

| Roster measure | Boys correlation with 4S/5D win rate | Girls |
|---|---:|---:|
| Best player | **+.354** | **+.343** |
| Top 5 average | +.586 | +.576 |
| Top 9 average | +.648 | +.659 |
| Top 11 average | +.662 | +.685 |
| **Top 14 average** | **+.684** | **+.690** |
| #10–#14 average | +.549 | +.579 |

The farther down the roster the measure reaches, the better it predicts team success.

The top player is no longer the best shorthand for the strength of an 8A/9A postseason team. The top fourteen are.

The year-over-year treated-versus-control comparison says the same thing:

| Group | Top-player correlation 2069 | 2070 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Boys 9A | +.651 | +.552 | **−.099** |
| Boys 8A | +.589 | +.467 | **−.122** |
| Girls 9A | +.666 | +.472 | **−.194** |
| Girls 8A | +.573 | +.354 | **−.219** |
| Boys 7A control | +.579 | +.772 | +.193 |
| Girls 7A control | +.539 | +.683 | +.144 |

All four classes that changed formats became **less dependent on their best player**.

The neighboring class that did not change formats moved the other way.

That is unusually strong first-year evidence that the format itself changed what wins.

## V. Top-heavy roster construction stopped paying

The second design premise was that large schools should no longer be able to hide a weak tail behind one dominant player.

That also happened.

Correlation of internal roster spread with team success:

| Group | 2069 | 2070 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Boys 9A | +.244 | **+.023** |
| Boys 8A | +.221 | **+.067** |
| Girls 9A | +.432 | **+.132** |
| Girls 8A | +.182 | **+.024** |
| Boys 7A control | +.106 | **+.330** |

The treated 8A/9A groups all collapsed toward zero.

Using the broader #1-to-#14 spread produces essentially the same result:

- boys: **−.005**
- girls: **+.063**

In plain terms:

**A huge No. 1 player plus a weak bottom is no longer a particularly effective large-school postseason build.**

That is precisely the behavior 4S/5D was intended to produce.

Depth itself rose or remained very strong in three of the four treated groups:

- boys 9A: +.744 → **+.816**
- girls 9A: +.740 → **+.762**
- girls 8A: +.717 → **+.752**
- boys 8A: +.807 → **+.755**

Even the one decline leaves boys 8A at a very high depth correlation.

## VI. Competitive closeness survived

Nine flights should reduce the probability that one result decides the whole dual. The question was whether it would reduce it too much.

It did not.

### 8A/9A State score distribution

**Boys**

- **5–4: 39.7%**
- 6–3: 30.8%
- 7–2: 16.7%
- 8–1: 10.3%
- 9–0: 2.6%

**Girls**

- **5–4: 32.1%**
- 6–3: 33.3%
- 7–2: 23.1%
- 8–1: 10.3%
- 9–0: 1.3%

The 2069 association-wide one-flight-margin baseline was 42.7%.

The new format therefore reduced single-flight finishes, as expected, but did not eliminate them.

Boys 8A/9A State remained especially close: almost **four in ten duals finished 5–4**.

Girls came in lower at about **one in three**. That is worth tracking in 2071, but it remains above the pre-pilot warning threshold of roughly 30%.

The correct interpretation is:

**4S/5D averages more information without turning the championship into a series of blowouts.**

---

## VII. Flight leverage: the first-year test is different under 4S/5D

Under the old mixed postseason landscape, analysts could compare how often individual flights appeared in close duals because not every format contained the same flights.

Under 4S/5D, all nine flights are played in every dual. Their raw presence in a one-point dual is therefore identical by construction.

So 2071 should use a more useful leverage test:

- which flights most often flip a 4–4 partial result into 5–4;
- which flights are most often won by the eventual dual winner;
- OVR gap by flight;
- upset rate by flight;
- how often S4 or D5 reverses the old 1S/4D counterfactual outcome.

The first-year winner-counterfactual already establishes that the new flights are not decorative: **they changed 17.9% of State winners.**

## X. Postseason seeding: the title layer is now extremely chalky

The four-season trend is clear:

| | 2067 | 2068 | 2069 | 2070 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Median champion seed | — | B3 / G7 | 2 | **1** |
| Champions seeded top 4 | — | — | 17/24 | **21/24** |
| Champions seeded 10th+ | 9/24 | 3/24 | 1/24 | **0/24** |
| No. 1 seeds winning | — | — | 7/24 | **14/24** |

No double-digit seed won a State team title.

That does **not** mean State contained no upsets. It means low-seeded teams are now much less likely to string together enough unlikely results to win the entire tournament.

The title bracket increasingly resolves toward elite teams.

Whether the board wants that much championship accuracy is a policy judgment. The data itself says only that the trend is real.

## XI. A new problem: State seeding may not fully understand 4S/5D teams

The title layer was chalky, but individual large-school State rounds were not uniformly so.

In boys 8A/9A, seed upsets actually increased.

### Boys 8A/9A State seed-upset rate

- 2069: **26.0%**
- 2070: **37.3%**

Boys 9A alone rose to **43.6%**.

The nominally stronger boys 9A team by TOSS/class rank lost **46.2%** of State duals.

That looks strange until roster strength is compared directly with the new format.

In boys 8A/9A State duals:

- State seed predicted the winner: **62.7%**
- TOSS/class rank: **60.0%**
- top-14 roster strength: **72.0%**
- top-9 roster strength: **76.0%**

Girls:

- State seed: **65.3%**
- TOSS/class rank: **69.3%**
- top-14 strength: **69.3%**
- #10–#14 depth alone: **70.4%**

This suggests that some apparent State “upsets” may actually be **rating errors caused by the format transition**.

Regular-season TOSS is largely observing teams in 3S/4D.

The postseason asks how good fourteen players are.

Those are related, but they are not identical questions.

A team can therefore be properly rated for the regular season and still be mispriced for 4S/5D.

That should be one of the main 2071 research questions.

## XII. Epiregional Year 2

The Epiregional became less volatile in its second season.

Lower-seeded winner rate:

| | 2069 | 2070 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Boys | 31.3% | **18.8%** |
| Girls | 33.3% | **18.8%** |

This is not necessarily a trend toward an 18.8% steady state.

It is evidence that the mechanism responds to the strength distribution of that season's Zonal champions rather than manufacturing a fixed level of turnover.

The basic design remains intact:

- Zonal title earns State
- Epiregional determines guaranteed seed protection
- season-long merit fills the remaining protected lines

There is no first-order evidence that this structure needs revision.

### Girls

**4S/5D selects the large-school champion.  
1S/4D tests that champion against champions produced under other competitive environments.**

## XIV. Participation: the new format substantially widened State access

The expansion from 1S/4D to 4S/5D changed the number of athletes directly involved in large-school State.

Unique 8A/9A State participants:

| | 2069 | 2070 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Boys | ~705 | **1,112** |
| Girls | ~728 | **1,122** |

That is a major expansion in actual championship participation.

But these players were not generally appearing at State with no competitive preparation.

Among 2070 8A/9A State participants:

- median prior varsity dual appearances: **26 boys / 26 girls**
- fewer than five prior varsity dual appearances:
  - **2.2% boys**
  - **2.3% girls**

About 16% had ten or fewer prior varsity dual appearances.

Even that understates preparation, because it excludes JV matches.

The earlier concern that a large share of #12–#14 players would arrive at State essentially untested did not materialize.

It should no longer be analyzed as merely a consolation tournament.

This remains an important source of variance in an association whose varsity team championship has become much more predictable.

## XVIII. Individual State remains much more volatile than Team State

Individual State continues to produce substantially more variance than the team championship.

Across the individual flights:

- boys title chalk rate: **50%**
- girls: **60%**

That contrasts sharply with **21 of 24 team champions coming from the top four seeds**.

Notable results:

- **Chaminade boys won five of six 6A individual flights** in addition to the team championship
- **Notre Dame girls swept all six 9A individual flights** alongside the 9A team title
- **Agate girls won five of six Group 3 individual flights** plus the team title
- Titus Lawson / Matthew Nurse won 8A D3 from the **No. 25 seed**
- Derrick Luna / Nicholas Stauffer won 4A D3 from the **No. 31 seed**
- Oscar Lowden / Darrian Imamura won 8A D1 from the **No. 11 seed**

The association therefore has different levels of uncertainty operating simultaneously:

- Team State increasingly rewards the strongest complete programs
- Individual State remains far more open
- JV State remains especially capable of producing breakout runs

That is a useful competitive balance rather than a contradiction.

## XIX. Transfer restraint made 2070 more informative

2070 was largely free of the mass transfer intervention used before 2069.

That matters analytically.

The association still contains trapped players, particularly in 7A and below, where postseason lineups did not expand.

But those players now have more legitimate outlets:

- JV season
- JV district and State competition
- Individual State pathways
- development toward future varsity positions

The lack of a mass transfer wave makes 2070 a cleaner baseline for measuring natural roster congestion.

Before another large intervention, JHSAA should identify which players are actually failing to receive meaningful competition despite the expanded secondary structures.

## XX. What 2071 should measure

The first-year 4S/5D question has been answered.

The format changes outcomes and rewards depth.

The next analytical questions are narrower.

### 1. Develop a postseason-specific large-school strength measure

Compare:

- TOSS
- State seed
- top-9 quality
- top-11 quality
- top-14 quality
- #10–#14 depth

against actual 4S/5D results.

The goal is not necessarily to replace TOSS. It is to understand what part of postseason team strength the regular-season measure is failing to observe.

### 2. Track whether the 2070 depth effects persist

Specifically:

- top-player correlation
- top-14 correlation
- #10–#14 correlation
- internal spread correlation

If the 2070 treated-versus-control pattern reproduces, the causal case for the format will become substantially stronger.

### 3. Track 5–4 rates

The first-year State rates were:

- boys **39.7%**
- girls **32.1%**

The girls figure deserves another year of observation.

### 4. Build a better flight-leverage measure

Because all nine positions now appear in every 4S/5D dual, simple presence in a one-flight-margin dual is no longer informative.

Instead measure which flights actually reverse dual outcomes.

### 5. Measure the JV-to-varsity pipeline

Track:

- JV sophomores/juniors becoming top-14 varsity players
- JV State participants appearing in varsity postseason lineups
- ladder movement from #12–#20 into #1–#14
- combined JV/varsity match volume before first postseason role

This is now necessary because JV has become a developmental circuit.

### 6. Continue TOC compression monitoring

One season found no obvious penalty.

That should be measured over several seasons before concluding that 4S/5D-to-1S/4D reversion is neutral.

### 7. Do not infer a need for transfers from roster congestion alone

Measure whether a trapped player actually lacks meaningful competition before treating transfer redistribution as necessary.

## Board conclusion

2070 provides strong first-year evidence that the large-school format reform accomplished what it was designed to accomplish.

The change:

- materially altered dual winners
- increased the predictive value of full-roster strength
- sharply reduced dependence on one elite player
- erased most of the advantage of top-heavy roster construction
- expanded State participation
- preserved a substantial number of close duals
- produced credible champions
- did not create an obvious Tournament of Champions penalty

At the same time, the rest of the association continues to reflect the 2069 reform package:

- favorite-win behavior is stable
- Epiregional seeding remains functional
- low-seeded team champions have nearly disappeared
- individual championships retain considerably more variance
- JV has become a genuine developmental circuit

The largest new issue is not the format itself.

It is measurement.

**JHSAA now has a postseason format in 8A/9A that evaluates fourteen-player roster strength more directly than its regular-season rating system does.**

That is the problem the analysis team should spend 2071 solving.

### Recommendation

1. **Retain 4S/5D in 8A/9A.**
2. **Make no further change to the gap-response curve.**
3. **Retain the Epiregional structure.**
4. **Continue the full JV championship system.**
5. **Avoid another mass transfer intervention until the natural 2070 roster-congestion baseline has been studied.**
6. **Develop a 4S/5D-specific large-school strength diagnostic for seeding analysis, without assuming it must replace TOSS.**
