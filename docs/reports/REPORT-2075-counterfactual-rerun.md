# 2075 Counterfactual Rerun: Transfers, Opportunity, and Postseason Architecture

## Why this season matters

A crash forced Season 49 / calendar year 2075 to be simulated again after later JHSAA rules had already landed in the codebase. Ordinarily that would just be an unfortunate duplicate season. In this case it created an unusually useful accidental experiment.

The original 2075 and the rerun inherit essentially the same historical population from 2074, but they expose that population to materially different institutional rules.

| 2075 version | Player movement | Postseason architecture |
|---|---|---|
| Original 2075 | Massive opportunity-transfer pass plus stranded-star repair | Earlier committee / field rules |
| Rerun 2075 | Essentially no returning-player school movement | Later, broader committee / field rules |

The rerun therefore should not be treated as a replacement for the lost original 2075 analytically. The two seasons are more valuable together. They provide something close to a counterfactual comparison: what happens to the same inherited player population when mobility policy and championship access change?

The strongest findings are:

- The opportunity-transfer mechanism is dramatically effective for buried players. The same players who received roughly 20 varsity appearances after transferring in the original 2075 receive only one or two when left at their old schools in the rerun.
- Transfers mostly **reallocate** playing opportunity rather than increase the association-wide supply of it. Overall participation rates barely move.
- The stranded-star transfer mechanism is a different policy entirely. Those players already play heavily if they stay home; moving them changes competitive context, concentrates elite talent, and materially changes championship outcomes.
- The broader committee / Parastate architecture scales surprisingly well. The rerun nearly doubles the number of at-large selections without diluting the pool; marginal selections win opening matches at a higher rate than the original 2075 committee class.
- The association remains structurally recognizable across both worlds. Rankings remain strongly correlated and most elite programs remain elite. What changes is the championship frontier: which good team wins, which buried player gets a career, and which strong team outside the old qualification road receives a State opportunity.

## I. The transfer experiment now has a control group

The original 2075 contained 2,205 returning-player school changes: 1,109 boys and 1,096 girls. It was not one homogeneous market.

The first intervention moved 1,852 players primarily for playing opportunity. These were mostly sophomores and juniors projected outside a useful varsity role. A later repair pass targeted a different population: strong players, usually the No. 1 player at a weak program, whose problem was not access to the lineup but competitive context.

In the rerun, the matched returning-player population records essentially no school changes. That gives the original transfer class an unusually clean comparison: what happened to the same players when they simply stayed home?

### Buried-player outcomes

For players who had been ranked No. 12 or lower at their source program, the effect is enormous.

| Original-2075 movers who were source rank 12+ | Original 2075 after transfer | Rerun 2075 staying home |
|---|---:|---:|
| Girls median varsity appearances | 20 | **2** |
| Girls with 10+ appearances | 76.4% | **12.8%** |
| Girls with zero appearances | 1.2% | **19.0%** |
| Boys median varsity appearances | 19 | **1** |
| Boys with 10+ appearances | 73.7% | **13.1%** |
| Boys with zero appearances | 1.9% | **23.6%** |

This is about as strong a validation of the opportunity-transfer thesis as the simulation can produce.

The original 2075 analysis showed that the opportunity class had a median destination rank around No. 10, that 75.1% made at least ten varsity appearances, and that only 1.6% recorded no varsity appearances. The rerun supplies the missing counterfactual. Without movement, the exact population the mechanism targeted overwhelmingly remains buried.

The transfer system is therefore not merely moving names around the association. For this population it changes whether a player has a meaningful varsity career at all.

## II. Stranded stars are a different transfer market

The source-star population behaves almost completely differently.

Players who were already top three at their 2074 program played heavily whether they transferred or stayed.

- Girls in this population: median 29 varsity appearances after transferring versus 26 when staying home.
- Boys: median 29 after transferring versus 28 when staying home.
- Virtually all reached ten varsity appearances in either world.

That means the stranded-star intervention should never be evaluated as an opportunity program. These players did not need transfers to get on court.

Their problem was competitive context.

The original repair slate made that explicit. Of 367 proposed players:

- 288 were their source program's No. 1;
- 70 were No. 2;
- 9 were No. 3;
- 78.5% were source No. 1 players;
- the median source winning percentage was .346;
- 74.7% came from teams at .400 or worse.

Of those, 353 appeared at their intended destination. Every one finished in the destination top 11 and made at least ten varsity appearances; their median destination rank was No. 2 and their median varsity workload was 29 appearances.

The rerun confirms the conceptual distinction:

- **Opportunity transfers:** buried players move because they need a varsity seat.
- **Stranded-star transfers:** established players move because their current program cannot give their talent a competitive future.

Those are different markets and should remain different mechanisms.

## III. Transfers reallocate opportunity more than they create it

The player-level effect is dramatic, but the association-wide participation totals barely change.

| Participation measure | Original 2075 | Rerun 2075 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Girls with 10+ varsity appearances | 58.1% | 58.4% |
| Boys with 10+ varsity appearances | 57.1% | 57.3% |
| Girls with zero varsity appearances | 6.3% | 6.9% |
| Boys with zero varsity appearances | 6.6% | 8.0% |

This is an important policy result.

Transfers are primarily an **allocation mechanism**. They redistribute a relatively fixed supply of varsity court time toward players who would otherwise be trapped behind stronger incumbents. They do not materially expand the total number of varsity opportunities available across the association.

That means mobility cannot substitute for mechanisms intended to expand participation itself:

- JV competition;
- additional developmental schedules;
- lineup-format changes;
- reserve competitions;
- other structures that create actual additional playing slots.

Looking only at association-wide participation rates would have hidden how successful the transfer intervention was for the specific players it targeted.

## IV. The old transfer market concentrated talent

The original 2075 produced visibly greater between-program talent inequality than the no-transfer rerun.

Using each program's top 11 as a rough varsity-strength measure:

| Strength dispersion | Original transfer 2075 | No-transfer rerun |
|---|---:|---:|
| Girls top-11 strength SD | 8.13 | 7.79 |
| Boys top-11 strength SD | 8.52 | 8.08 |
| Girls top-3 strength SD | 9.34 | 8.68 |
| Boys top-3 strength SD | 9.59 | 8.76 |

The effect is strongest at the top of the ladder.

That is exactly what the stranded-star mechanism should be expected to do. It removes elite players from weak programs and places them onto rosters where they often become No. 1 or No. 2. It therefore makes the strongest competitive units stronger even when it may improve the individual player's competitive experience.

The original 2075 showed how consequential this could become. Seventeen of the 24 State champions received at least one repair player.

Walter-Kenny boys added Brayden Davis and Landyn Moore from weak source programs; they became No. 1 and No. 2 and Walter-Kenny won 4A State. West Oberlin boys added Cole Edwards and Matias Goodson as its top two players and won its first Group 1 State championship. Truman girls added Isabelle Davis and Daphne Talbert, reached the 4A final, and won both No. 1 and No. 2 Singles State.

The rerun provides evidence that the competitive-concentration concern was not theoretical.

## V. Championship order changes, but the association does not become random

Only 10 of the 24 Team State champions repeat between the two versions of 2075.

Six boys champions repeat:

- Baptist — 7A
- The Dalles — 5A
- Vale — 2A
- Salmon Bay — 1A
- Pacific Friends — Group 2
- Espoo — Group 3

Four girls champions repeat:

- Condotti Vanguard Academy — 7A
- Walter-Kenny — 4A
- Pacific Friends — Group 2
- Sotkamo Union — Group 3

The contrast is especially useful in groups whose basic postseason structure remained comparable.

| Group | Original boys | Rerun boys | Original girls | Rerun girls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9A | Notre Dame | **Potrero Hill** | Notre Dame | **Port Meridian Central** |
| 8A | Minnesota City | **Plainfield** | North Coast | **Seawall** |
| 7A | Baptist | Baptist | Condotti Vanguard Academy | Condotti Vanguard Academy |
| Group 1 | West Oberlin | **Utah** | Grant | **Valois Bluffs** |
| Group 2 | Pacific Friends | Pacific Friends | Pacific Friends | Pacific Friends |

Only four of those ten gender/group champions repeat.

Yet the underlying association remains recognizable.

TOSS rankings across the two versions still have Spearman correlations of approximately:

- girls: **.84**
- boys: **.82**

Win percentages correlate at roughly .80, and most groups retain six to nine of the original top ten programs in the rerun top ten.

This is the right scale of institutional effect. Mobility policy does not generate an unrelated universe. The broad power structure survives. What changes is the margin where championships are decided.

Redistributing a relatively small share of returning players is enough to change which strong program becomes champion without erasing the underlying competitive hierarchy.

## VI. Mobility policy is also a narrative engine

The individual-player histories diverge just as strongly.

Only five of twelve boys No. 1 Singles champions and five of twelve girls No. 1 Singles champions repeat between the two versions.

Daphne Talbert is the clearest example.

In the original universe, Talbert transferred from Rimrock to Truman. She became Truman's No. 2 behind Isabelle Davis, and the pair won 4A No. 1 and No. 2 Singles State while Truman reached the team final.

In the rerun, Talbert remains in her original competitive context and instead wins **Group 3 No. 1 Singles**.

The transfer therefore did not merely alter the school name attached to her player record. It changed the category in which her career history was written.

Other original 2075 stories disappear or change as well. Some breakout champions do not repeat; different players occupy the individual championship paths; contenders assembled through transfer mobility no longer have the same ladder.

Once the simulation contains persistent careers, mobility policy is not a peripheral roster-management mechanic. It is one of the primary generators of historical narrative.

## VII. The broader committee / Parastate model scales better than expected

The clearest later-rule innovation in the rerun is the expansion of committee access.

Original 2075 used committees in:

- 9A
- 8A
- 7A
- Group 1

That produced 112 at-large selections across boys and girls. Those teams won 31 Parastate matches, a **27.7%** opening win rate.

The rerun extends committee access much farther:

- 6A through 2A become 40-team fields with eight at-larges;
- 1A expands from 24 to 32 with eight at-larges;
- Group 3 expands from 24 to 28 with four at-larges;
- 9A, 8A, 7A and Group 1 retain their committee structures;
- Group 2 remains without a committee field.

Total committee selections rise from 112 to **216**.

The obvious concern is dilution: if the committee keeps reaching farther down the rankings, marginal bids should become substantially weaker.

That does not happen.

The rerun's 216 at-large selections win **84 opening Parastate matches**, a **38.9%** win rate.

The newly added classifications produce credible teams rather than ceremonial entrants. Examples include:

- 6A boys: 8 at-larges, 4 opening wins, 9 total bracket wins by at-large teams;
- 4A girls: 5 of 8 at-larges win their opening match;
- 2A boys: 5 of 8 win immediately;
- Group 3 girls: 2 of 4 win Parastate.

At the same time, no at-large team wins a State championship.

That is close to the ideal institutional behavior for Parastate. The mechanism identifies teams strong enough to demonstrate that they belonged in the field, but it does not become a parallel championship route dominated by teams that failed ordinary qualification.

The original 2075 evidence already supported the committee principle in the largest groups. The rerun suggests the logic generalizes substantially farther down the classification structure.

## VIII. Larger fields add volatility without producing chaos

Original 2075 was unusually consolidated:

- 13 of 24 State champions were No. 1 seeds;
- 23 of 24 were top-four seeds;
- the deepest champion was B.T. Washington girls, No. 8 in 2A.

The rerun still produces **13 No. 1-seed champions**.

But only **20 of 24** champions come from the top four.

Notable deeper champions include:

- Valley Christian boys — 6A, No. 5
- Fletcher-Garrison Hall boys — 3A, **No. 13**
- Mill Bar girls — 3A, No. 5
- Valois Bluffs girls — Group 1, No. 5

The No. 13 3A champion is especially informative. It is not an at-large champion. The larger championship architecture exposed more of the actual competitive distribution without simply allowing committee selections to take over the tournament.

Individual State shows the same moderate increase in volatility.

| Individual State | Original 2075 | Rerun 2075 |
|---|---:|---:|
| No. 1-seed champions | 83 / 144 (57.6%) | **74 / 144 (51.4%)** |
| Top-four champions | 132 / 144 (91.7%) | **130 / 144 (90.3%)** |
| Champions seeded 10+ | 1 | **1** |

The rerun is less chalky, but it is nowhere close to random. The top four still win more than nine out of ten individual titles.

## IX. What the accidental A/B test establishes

### 1. Opportunity transfers work

For the buried players they target, the effect is enormous.

Without movement, the same players generally receive one or two varsity appearances. After moving, they receive roughly twenty and three-quarters reach ten or more.

This is no longer merely evidence that transfer recipients happened to play at their destinations. The rerun shows what happens when those same players do not move.

### 2. Transfers do not create court time

Association-wide participation changes very little.

Mobility reallocates scarce varsity opportunity toward different players. Policies intended to increase the total supply of participation still need JV, reserve competition, schedule expansion, or format changes.

### 3. Stranded-star movement is not an opportunity intervention

Elite source players play heavily either way.

Their transfer mechanism is about competitive ambition and institutional mobility, not access to the lineup.

### 4. Stranded-star movement concentrates talent

Moving source No. 1s from weak teams toward contenders increases top-of-roster inequality and materially changes championship competition.

That may be desirable as a simulation of an open transfer market, but it is not competitively neutral and should not be described as such.

### 5. Committee access scales

Expanding from 112 to 216 at-large selections did not produce a diluted population. Opening Parastate win rate rose from 27.7% to 38.9%, while no at-large won State.

The committee appears capable of finding legitimate championship-field teams well below the largest classifications.

### 6. Institutional rules alter history at the margin, not the entire universe

Rankings remain strongly correlated across both runs. Most established powers remain strong.

But championships, player careers, and the composition of the final contenders change substantially.
