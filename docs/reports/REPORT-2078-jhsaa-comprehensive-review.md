# JHSAA 2078 — Comprehensive Review

**Status:** governing post-season review for 2078. Read alongside `DOCTRINE-2078-jhsaa-transfer-policy.md` and `ADDENDUM-2078-jhsaa-transfer-second-pass.md`.

## Executive summary

2078 tested **two deliberately different transfer interventions** that must not be collapsed into one mechanism:

1. the original **839-player association-wide class**, built around deep-roster congestion clearing, role liberation, donor backfill, and weak-program rebuild hosts;
2. a separate **97-player feeder/reload pass**, with **zero PID overlap** with the first class, designed as a **two-way flow**:
   - **12 up-flow moves** into four reload programs that needed elite top-end talent;
   - **85 down-flow moves** into feeder programs that could absorb blocked big-school players into meaningful varsity roles.

The combined 2078 transfer season therefore contained **936 unique moves**.

The correct conclusion is not that the 97-player pass was a weaker or less coherent version of the 839-player market. It was a separate, deliberate experiment in moving talent **both upward and downward according to program need**.

The season validated both directions.

---

## I. The rebuilds worked, and two of them spectacularly

This was the season the reload experiment cashed out. All four target programs moved, and the girls side produced two of the most dramatic single-season turnarounds in the recent archive.

| Program | 2077 | 2078 |
|---|---|---|
| **Mater Dei girls** | 7-20, rank **70** | **23-8, rank 3** |
| **Jesuit girls** | 19-12, rank 26 | **29-1, rank 1** |
| Mater Dei boys | 22-7, rank 18 | 29-4, rank 7, **State finalist** |
| **Rockridge girls** | 30-4, rank 1 | 38-4, **State champion** |
| **Larchmont Ridge girls** | 36-2, lost final | 31-2, **State champion** |

Mater Dei girls moved from **70th in 9A to 3rd** in one season. This was the largest rank improvement measured in the recent archive. Penelope Gonzales (99/99) and Peyton Mansour (98/98) arriving together did what the reload projection was designed to test: a program with an emptied top end became immediately competitive.

Jesuit girls moved from rank 26 to **rank 1**, going 29-1 after the targeted reload class landed.

Rockridge girls and Larchmont Ridge girls both completed the broader reload objective by winning their classifications.

The important methodological point is that these programs were not all diagnosed the same way.

- Mater Dei girls needed stars because their roster had emptied at the top.
- Jesuit girls needed a smaller set of elite reloads.
- Larchmont Ridge boys already possessed substantial top-end strength; adding three 96-97 caliber players improved the roster without producing a comparable ranking jump, which is consistent with the diagnosis that top-end talent was not their main deficiency.

The intervention was therefore **program-specific**, not a generic rule that more talent always produces the same outcome.

## II. Transfer conversion: both classes performed at essentially the same rate

The 97-player feeder/reload pass and the original 839-player class converted surprisingly similarly.

| Class | Found at destination | Median new rank | Median apps | Top 11 | Never played |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 97-player pass — boys | 44/46 | 11 | 18 | 59% | 7% |
| 97-player pass — girls | 50/51 | 9 | 24 | **70%** | **0%** |
| 839-player class — boys | 420/420 | 9 | 21 | 71% | 6% |
| 839-player class — girls | 419/419 | 9 | 22 | 70% | 3% |

Nine hundred thirty-six players moved across the two passes. Virtually all appeared at their intended destination, roughly 70% cracked a top-11 lineup, and the girls side of the feeder/reload pass had **zero players fail to appear**.

That is important because the two passes were built for different purposes but nevertheless produced similar player-level conversion.

## III. What the 97-player pass actually tested

### A. Up-flow: 12 players into four reload programs

The up-flow was not ordinary congestion clearing. It deliberately moved elite or near-elite players **upward into programs whose problem was insufficient top-end talent**.

Representative cases:

- **Penelope Gonzales (99/99, Obama 6A) -> Mater Dei girls.** Gonzales entered as the reigning 6A Player of the Year at 36-2. Mater Dei had gone 1-24, 6-19, 7-18, 7-20 and its best returning player was 69 OVR. One elite arrival materially changed the program's competitive ceiling.
- **Peyton Mansour (98/98 sophomore) -> Mater Dei girls.** Mansour gave Mater Dei both immediate elite ability and multiple years of remaining eligibility.
- **Violet Kasper (96/97) -> Mater Dei girls.** The third top-end arrival completed the reload rather than merely providing one star surrounded by the same weak roster.
- **Williamson -> Jesuit girls**, supplemented by Lorena Gálvez (92/94, two-time 2A S1 finalist) and Nina Cunningham (91/97 sophomore).
- **Larchmont Ridge boys** received Oliver Dickey (97/97 from Mission Butte), Graysen Sandoval (1A S1 champion), and Frank Lachman, three 96-97 caliber additions against a returning top end whose best player was 72.

The governing idea is that some programs do not need depth redistribution; they need a **reload at the top**.

That is a separate transfer use case and should remain separate in future implementations.

### B. Down-flow: 85 players into feeder programs

The down-flow did the opposite.

These players were largely blocked big-school players ranked 13th or worse on their own rosters, overwhelmingly rising seniors, moved into strong smaller-classification feeder programs where they could dress and play meaningful varsity tennis.

Examples of the logic:

- **Aiden Velasquez (41/86)** and **Ethan London (41/80)** were 13th-ranked at Baptist and Carondelet respectively. Their current level left them buried in deep programs, but their ceilings made them potentially valuable long-term players at Bay Oregon.
- A small number of younger players were included only where their ceilings remained low enough that they were unlikely ever to penetrate the proper lineup at their original program.

The feeder hosts were not chosen because they were bad programs needing rescue. They were chosen because they could absorb blocked players **without losing their own competitive identity** and could give those players meaningful roles.

Westfield Friends, Walter-Kenny, Bay Oregon, Texas Beach and the other feeders were therefore functioning as **receiving institutions in a talent pipeline**, not as turnaround projects.

---

## IV. The feeder programs mostly held position — which was the intended outcome

The feeder programs did not need to become dramatically better for the pass to succeed.

The intended outcome was:

1. absorb large numbers of blocked players;
2. provide them useful varsity opportunities;
3. remain competitively strong rather than being destabilized by the influx;
4. create a viable downward pathway for deep-roster players.

That is what happened.

Examples:

- **Westfield Friends boys remained rank 1**;
- **Bay Oregon boys remained rank 2 and reached the State final**.

Across the pass, the feeder programs absorbed **85 call-ups** while mostly holding their competitive position.

That is evidence of a successful feeder role, not evidence that the pass failed to create a turnaround.

The wrong evaluation question is:

> Did these already-good programs climb dramatically?

The correct question is:

> Did they absorb blocked players, create meaningful roles, and remain healthy programs?

For the feeder cohort, the answer was yes.

## V. The original 839-player market and the 97-player pass should be evaluated differently

Future agents must not flatten the combined 936-player class into one undifferentiated transfer market.

### Original 839-player class

Evaluate primarily on:

- reduction of deep-roster congestion;
- role liberation;
- destination rank and varsity appearances;
- source-program backfill;
- concentrated rebuild-host improvement;
- whether older blocked players obtained better competitive roles.

### 97-player feeder/reload pass

Evaluate as two separate submarkets.

#### Up-flow

Ask:

- did an elite reload materially improve programs whose problem was top-end talent?
- did the incoming players actually occupy the intended high-order roles?
- did the program improve in the way the diagnosis predicted?

#### Down-flow

Ask:

- did blocked players obtain useful varsity roles?
- did feeder programs absorb them without destabilization?
- did high-potential players gain a viable developmental path that did not exist at the source?
- did younger low-ceiling players correctly leave deep rosters where they were unlikely to penetrate the lineup?

The two directions are complementary, not contradictory.

Portola-Caverly girls won 1A as the No. 1 seed in the same season, producing a school sweep.

This ended Port Meridian Central's girls run, while the **Port Meridian Central boys won their fourth consecutive 9A title** at 32-3.

## VII. What 2078 says about the transfer method

The strongest lesson is that transfer policy should be **diagnostic rather than directional**.

There is no single correct flow of talent.

### Some players should move downward

Blocked big-program players may need to move to smaller classifications or feeder programs to obtain meaningful varsity roles.

### Some players should move laterally

Role-congested players can move into weak or rebuilding programs within similar classifications and become S1/S2 rather than spending another season in lower doubles.

### Some elite players should move upward

A program whose roster has collapsed at the top may legitimately need elite reloads. Mater Dei girls are the clearest 2078 example.

The system should therefore ask two questions before choosing direction:

1. **What does the player need?**
2. **What does the program need?**

The 97-player pass was a deliberate test of this bidirectional logic.

---

## VIII. 2078 policy implications for future agents

### 1. Preserve the two-way feeder/reload concept

Do not rewrite the supplemental pass as a weak-host rebuild experiment. It was deliberately designed to move players **both directions**.

### 2. Keep feeder programs as a distinct destination type

A feeder program does not need to be poor.

Its defining characteristics are:

- enough varsity capacity to absorb blocked players;
- competitive stability after receiving them;
- ability to give players meaningful roles;
- institutional strength sufficient to remain a healthy destination rather than simply becoming another congestion point.

### 3. Keep reload programs as a distinct destination type

Reload hosts are programs whose deficiency is specifically at the top of the roster.

They should receive a small number of genuinely elite additions when the data shows that one or two stars would materially change the program.

### 4. Preserve donor protection

The feeder/reload concept is not permission to strip one donor.

Large destination cohorts should continue to be assembled from many sources, with source rosters recalculated and younger replacement players promoted where appropriate.

### 5. Evaluate transfer success by intended function

A feeder host holding rank after taking five players can be a success.

A reload host jumping 60+ ranking places can be a success.

A rebuild host becoming a State semifinalist can be a success.

Those are different outcomes from different mechanisms and should not be judged against one universal metric.

---

## IX. Governing interpretation of the combined 2078 class

The 936-player transfer season should now be understood as a **portfolio of transfer mechanisms**:

1. **deep-roster clearing** — move overqualified reserve players into meaningful varsity use;
2. **role liberation** — move experienced lower-doubles/fringe players into larger competitive roles;
3. **weak-program rebuild cohorts** — assemble enough transfers from many donors to create a viable varsity core;
4. **feeder down-flow** — move blocked big-school players into stable smaller-class programs that can absorb them;
5. **elite reload up-flow** — move elite players into programs whose primary deficiency is a collapsed top end;
6. **elite rescue** — remain a separate, narrow exception market constrained by legacy and career stage.

The first three came from the original 839-player class. The feeder/reload experiment was the deliberate 97-player second pass.

Future implementation should preserve these distinctions rather than treating all 936 players as products of one generic transfer score.

The central lesson of 2078 is therefore broader than "transfers worked."

> **The association can support multiple transfer markets simultaneously, as long as each move is tied to a specific role problem at the player level and a specific roster problem at the program level.**
