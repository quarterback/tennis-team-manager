# BRIEF: JHSAA Opportunity Clearing Market

**Status:** Offseason transfer-market design  
**Initial data basis:** 2038–2039 JHSAA transfer experiments  
**Primary experiment:** 2040 offseason

## Summary

The JHSAA needs a separate transfer mechanism for **bad, marginal, lower-middle, and buried players whose primary problem is lack of useful playing opportunity**.

This market is distinct from both the top-end portal and the affiliate/loan system for unusually strong JV cohorts.

The 2039 transfer experiment moved a large amount of low-end depth downward. It succeeded at creating more playing opportunity and dramatically thickened 1A–3A rosters, but it also exposed a structural problem: too many players skipped over programs at roughly their own competitive level, leaving parts of the middle comparatively thin.

The next version should operate as a **sequential clearing market**.

The governing rule is:

> **Every player searches laterally first. Only unresolved surplus moves down one real competitive step. The receiving level then clears itself again before more surplus spills downward.**

The objective is to improve competitive density throughout the association while still guaranteeing that every player selected for this low-end market eventually finds a useful home.

---

## Who belongs in this market

This mechanism is only for the lower end of the player population.

Typical candidates include:

- players buried well below varsity;
- players with little or no varsity participation;
- low-ceiling players unlikely to become useful at their current program;
- lower-middle players blocked by a stronger roster;
- older players whose remaining priority is simply to play.

Eligibility should be relative to the source competitive level rather than governed by one absolute OVR/POT cutoff.

A player can be weak surplus at an elite 9A program while still being useful at another 9A or 8A program.

The market should consider:

- current OVR relative to competitive level;
- POT;
- varsity participation;
- JV participation;
- current ladder position;
- projected post-graduation ladder position;
- grade.

### Who does not belong

Do not put these players into the clearing market merely because they are buried:

- genuinely strong blocked talent;
- elite JV players who would be major varsity players elsewhere;
- established varsity players making competitive moves;
- family-driven transfers;
- affiliate/loan cohorts.

Those are different transfer problems with different objectives.

---

## The competitive ladder

The numbered JHSAA classifications are not nine equal competitive steps.

For this market, the actual ladder is:

> **9A / 8A → 7A / 6A → 5A → 4A → 3A → 2A → 1A**

### 9A and 8A are one competitive level

Movement between 9A and 8A is lateral.

A buried 9A player should search 9A first, then 8A, before being considered unresolved surplus. The reverse is also true.

An 8A destination is not a demotion for a 9A player.

### 7A and 6A are one competitive level

Movement between 7A and 6A is also lateral.

A 7A player should search 7A and 6A before moving down. A 6A player should search 6A and 7A before moving down.

The next real step from this level is **5A**.

### Every boundary below 7A/6A is real

After the 7A/6A band, each classification is its own competitive level:

- 7A/6A → 5A
- 5A → 4A
- 4A → 3A
- 3A → 2A
- 2A → 1A

The clearing process should respect those steps rather than skipping directly to the lowest classification where a player could dominate.

---

## Why same-level clearing comes first

The 2039 system primarily asked:

> Where can this player become useful?

The revised system first asks:

> Can another program at this same competitive level use this player?

That matters because every level contains both:

- strong programs carrying unusable surplus; and
- weak programs whose varsity lineup is substantially worse than that surplus.

A reserve player at Rockridge or another 9A power may be completely blocked there while still being useful at a weak 9A or 8A program.

Sending that player directly to 4A improves the individual player's ladder position but removes talent from the top level unnecessarily.

The correct first move is lateral redistribution.

---

## Clearing sequence

The market runs in rounds.

### Level 1: 9A / 8A

1. 9A candidates search 9A.
2. Unmatched 9A candidates search 8A.
3. 8A candidates search 8A.
4. Unmatched 8A candidates search 9A.
5. Apply placements.
6. Recalculate all affected 9A/8A ladders.
7. Re-run the lateral market for newly displaced eligible players.
8. Only unresolved surplus moves to 7A/6A.

### Level 2: 7A / 6A

1. Add unresolved 9A/8A surplus.
2. Clear internally across 7A and 6A.
3. Recalculate affected ladders.
4. Re-run the lateral market.
5. Only unresolved surplus moves to 5A.

### Level 3: 5A

1. Add unresolved 7A/6A surplus.
2. Clear within 5A.
3. Recalculate.
4. Unresolved surplus moves to 4A.

### Level 4: 4A

Clear internally, recalculate, then spill unresolved surplus to 3A.

### Level 5: 3A

Clear internally, recalculate, then spill unresolved surplus to 2A.

### Level 6: 2A

Clear internally, recalculate, then spill unresolved surplus to 1A.

### Level 7: 1A

1A is the final clearing market.

Every remaining player selected for this low-end transfer pool receives a destination.

---

## The cascade

The intended flow is not:

> 9A depth → 1A

It is:

> 9A/8A lateral market  
> → unresolved surplus enters 7A/6A  
> → 7A/6A clears itself  
> → unresolved surplus enters 5A  
> → 5A clears itself  
> → 4A  
> → 3A  
> → 2A  
> → 1A

A player originating in 9A may eventually reach 1A, but only after every higher competitive level has failed to find a useful place for them.

That makes 1A the **final clearing market**, not the default destination.

---

## Second-order displacement

New arrivals can create new transfer candidates.

Example:

1. A buried 9A player cannot find a 9A/8A home.
2. The player enters the 7A/6A market.
3. A 6A program takes the player into its varsity fringe.
4. An incumbent 6A player is pushed below the useful range.
5. That incumbent searches 7A/6A laterally.
6. If still unmatched, the incumbent enters 5A.
7. The process repeats.

This is the point of recalculating after every clearing level.

The market should model a cascade of opportunity rather than one-shot redistribution.

---

## Matching priorities

For this low-end pool, destinations should be ranked approximately by:

1. meaningful playing opportunity;
2. exact same classification;
3. equivalent classification within the same competitive level;
4. smallest real competitive drop necessary;
5. program need;
6. roster/JV value;
7. guaranteed final placement.

The system should not optimize for the largest possible ladder jump.

A player moving from No. 17 at one 7A program to No. 9 at a 6A program has solved the problem without moving down competitively.

---

## Rising seniors

Seniors have one overriding priority:

> **Play varsity now.**

A rising senior should only accept a destination where the player remains inside the projected varsity-playing group after the entire clearing round is complete.

The guarantee must be checked against the **final roster**, not merely at the moment the transfer is assigned.

Later arrivals cannot silently push that senior back out of varsity.

---

## Younger players

Sophomores and juniors can accept a wider range of useful outcomes.

A move can succeed if the player:

- becomes varsity;
- reaches the varsity fringe;
- lands in upper JV with a much shorter varsity path;
- joins a program where their competitive role is materially better than at the source.

JV gives younger players meaningful competition even when the transfer does not immediately produce a varsity position.

---

## JV and the definition of "buried"

JV makes the clearing market more informed.

A No. 15 player receiving a full JV season is not in the same condition as a No. 15 player who remains almost completely unused.

The market should eventually distinguish between:

- competitively active but varsity-blocked;
- genuinely underused;
- low-end surplus whose current level has no meaningful role for them.

JV results inform this classification. They do not cause development. Development rolls remain independent.

---

## Program-side logic

Schools should not receive players only because they have open roster spots.

The market should prefer destinations where a player improves the weak part of the program.

Examples:

- strong top three, weak positions 4–11;
- usable varsity but thin JV;
- small roster that would benefit from several additional players;
- bad program whose existing lower varsity players would be better served by JV.

A program already carrying enormous low-end depth should be a lower-priority destination for more of the same.

---

## Why this fixes the 2039 middle-tier problem

The 2039 transfer wave thickened 1A–3A very effectively, but it let too much talent skip the middle.

The revised market changes that.

### 9A / 8A

Weak top-level programs get first access to surplus from strong top-level programs.

### 7A / 6A

This band absorbs unresolved top-level surplus while also redistributing its own depth laterally.

### 5A

5A gets a dedicated clearing pass rather than being treated as a waypoint.

### 4A

4A gets its own market and can retain/absorb players before anything reaches 3A.

### 3A

3A receives only the surplus unresolved above it, while still redistributing internally.

### 2A / 1A

These remain the final places where the association can guarantee every player a home.

The bottom can still become deep. It simply does not have to absorb players that 5A or 4A could have used.

---

## Everyone gets a home

This is a clearing system, not a selective portal.

Once a player enters this low-end pool, the process continues until a destination is found.

At 1A, the optimizer should place every remaining candidate somewhere rather than return them to the same unusable situation.

The mental model is closer to a tiered sports labor market than to conventional American high-school transfer behavior.

---

## Separation from affiliate / loan cohorts

This is **not** the affiliate-loan system.

Affiliate/loan cohorts solve a different problem:

> A deep source program has an entire overqualified JV / B-team group that can move together to a weak host for one season.

Affiliate/loan characteristics:

- strong blocked players;
- cohort movement;
- temporary presumption;
- source may recall players after one season;
- players not recalled can remain permanently at the host.

Opportunity clearing characteristics:

- bad / marginal / lower-middle players;
- individual matching;
- permanent default;
- same-level-first clearing;
- sequential downward spill;
- no source recall relationship.

These are separate jobs.

---

## Separation from the top-end portal

The high-end transfer market is separate again.

Established good varsity players may move for:

- championships;
- S1 status;
- stronger teammates;
- family;
- a particular program;
- another strategic competitive reason.

Those players are choosing among desirable opportunities.

The clearing market is for players whose basic problem is finding a sufficiently useful competitive role.

---

## Proposed algorithm

```text
LEVELS = [
    [9A, 8A],
    [7A, 6A],
    [5A],
    [4A],
    [3A],
    [2A],
    [1A],
]

carry = []

for level in LEVELS:
    pool = carry + eligible_players_native_to(level)

    run_exact_class_matching(pool)
    run_lateral_matching_within(level)

    apply_placements()
    recalculate_all_affected_rosters()

    recheck_displaced_candidates()
    recheck_senior_final_varisty_guarantees()

    repeat_lateral_clear_until_stable()

    carry = unresolved_players()

place_all_remaining_players_in_1A()
```

The exact optimization method can be greedy, deferred-acceptance, or solver-based.

The important design decision is the **market sequence**.

---

## What to measure

### Player outcomes

- varsity participation before and after;
- JV participation before and after;
- final ladder position;
- classification movement;
- senior varsity success rate;
- number still functionally invisible.

### Program outcomes

- roster size before/after;
- top-11 strength;
- JV depth;
- lateral arrivals;
- arrivals from higher levels;
- players displaced to lower levels.

### Competitive-level outcomes

- median roster size;
- median top-11 strength;
- JV participation;
- inflow/outflow;
- competitive variance;
- whether 4A–7A remain healthy rather than being hollowed out.

---

## Design principle

The low-end transfer market should not ask how far down the pyramid a player can go.

It should ask:

> **What is the highest competitive level at which this player can find a genuinely useful role?**

Search laterally first.

Move down only when the current competitive level has no home.

Recalculate after every move.

Keep cascading until everybody has one.
