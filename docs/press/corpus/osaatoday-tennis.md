# OSAAtoday tennis, 2021-2026 — owner-supplied pages

Twelve tennis pieces from the Oregon association's own news desk, plus one school
publication's interview with a retiring coach. Pasted by the owner (the site cannot
be read from this environment). Full text is not kept here: structure, short
excerpts and what to take. People are NAME.

These are recent pages. Per `VOICE.md` they are used for **what a desk covers, in
what order, and what it notices**. Their wording is not the target: no star
ratings, national ranks, "commit" or "locked in" in generated copy.

What makes this set valuable is that it is **one desk following the same people for
six seasons**. Section 8 is the reason to read this file.

## 2. The notebook (piece 1)

A headline for the lead item, a deck that names all three items, then:

1. Lead item, ~400 words. Opens on a two-line tease ("As far as winning formulas
   go, it's not exactly foolproof."), then the pair, their class, their coach.
   Evidence is two matches told in one sentence each: the deficit, the opponent
   pair with class years, the score.
2. Why they are a pair: both played singles two years ago, did not qualify, asked
   to team up, the coach agreed. A summer on the road together.
3. How they play: "taking turns coming up to the net"; "They're not big people,
   but they're able to generate power."
4. What is left on the schedule, and the one match to circle, against another
   unbeaten pair: "bragging rights for the city".
5. Two more items under two-word bold tags ("Super frosh", "Sprague rookie"),
   250 and 120 words. The short one is record, one three-set scare with its score,
   one coach quote.

The coach's quotes carry character, not tactics: "They don't mind it. They like the
challenge."

## 3. The profile before a tournament (pieces 4, 5, 7, 9)

The desk's main tennis form. 600-1,000 words, one subject, written two to six weeks
before state. Order:

1. Lead on where the player stands in a career ("nears the end of a decorated
   career that includes three ... titles") or on a turn in it ("got a much-needed
   wake-up call").
2. The player's own first quote, early, and about feeling rather than tennis.
3. This season in numbers: "surrendering only eight games in her 10 victories".
4. **The field by name.** Two to four rivals with school and class, and the head
   to head with scores. Career losses are listed in full: "has won all but three
   matches in her high school career", then each one, opponent and score.
5. One earlier match retold as the proof of character (down 5-2 in the first set
   of a final, won 7-5, 6-1).
6. The coach on temperament. A second adult if there is one: club coach, the
   opposing coach, a parent.
7. One odd detail that only a coach would remember (a set won without losing a
   point, and the player upset about two double faults in the next).
8. Where the tennis came from: who taught them, at what age, the family.
9. What comes after high school, including choosing not to play on.
10. A family close, usually on a quote.

A rival's coach praising the subject is the strongest quote available: "I haven't
seen anybody yet that would beat him."

Kickers are short and in the player's mouth: "Four would've been great, but I still
need to get two."

## 4. An in-season tournament told through one match (piece 6)

1. Lead on the score at the low point: "Down 4-1 in the third set, ... was on the
   ropes".
2. Result with seeds and class years.
3. Winner's account of the turn, in plain words: "I was pretty gassed. I didn't
   really focus on the score."
4. Coach on the player's temperament.
5. The team result: points for the top four, how many of last year's top eight
   were entered.
6. Both finalists' paths as strings of scores.
7. The crowd, from both sides: the loser's school cheering gave the winner
   "motivation".
8. History between the two, with scores from other tournaments, including an
   earlier comeback from 6-1, 5-1.
9. Who was absent, by name, and what that means for the district.
10. The doubles title in two paragraphs: seeds, score, and each player's result at
    state a year ago.

## 5. State finals, small schools (piece 8)

Datelined, signed by a contributor, girls then boys under a bold tag.

1. Lead on the history between the finalists: "facing each other Saturday for the
   fourth time in a little over a year".
2. Result, score, site.
3. Winner, underplaying it: "every match is different for me".
4. **The loser quoted at length and with grace**: "I think it was a pretty good
   day, but ... just had a better day." Then asked for the winner's strengths:
   "I think everything."
5. Third place, the team title with points, a four-way tie for second listed.
6. Doubles final and third place, one sentence each, with what a player did in
   earlier years.
7. Boys under a tag, same order, shorter. An injury mentioned in passing ("limited
   by a back injury").

The headline's second line is the loss: a senior's "run at fourth state
championship denied". The beaten champion is the story as much as the winner.

## 6. The state preview (piece 11)

The longest piece of the year. Two halves.

**Top: two team features of ~600 words each**, one per contending boys team. Each:
a hook about the coach (an acronym printed on the jerseys), how many places the team
has in the draws against its rival, then the players one by one with a coach quote
each, then the coach's philosophy to close. Players are sketched by how they play:
one "hits harder than anybody I've witnessed in high school tennis", the partner
"is just steady"; a second-singles player with "a drop shot and ... very unorthodox
play", "He'll bring you in with the short serve and then just lob them."

**Bottom: one block per class and gender**, six blocks, all the same shape:

    Girls
    6A
    Site, days
    Top singles seeds: 1. NAME, School, class; 2. ...; 3. ...; 4. ...
    Top doubles seeds: 1. NAME, class/NAME, class, School; ...
    2025 team scores: School 19, School 16, School 14, ...
    Notes: one paragraph, 100-150 words.

The Notes paragraph is a fixed recipe: for each seed, what they did at state last
year and to whom; who beat whom in the league tournament; who has changed partner
or moved from doubles to singles; who is new to a school; which champion is not
back and why (graduated, or "decided not to play high school tennis this season");
the defending team champion last.

This block is exactly what a simulated season can fill from its own archive.

## 7. Team feature, mid-season (piece 10)

Coach is the only voice. The angle is a lineup that is never the same twice, because
eight players also play club sports in the spring. Order: record and the only
losses (to bigger schools); the coach on depth ("what 1-12 would look like"); the
No. 1 player's unfinished business (lost to the same opponent two years running;
that opponent is not playing this year); the coach on her as a teammate ("she'll
stay and watch No. 4 doubles"); the doubles pairs with their state history; a
transfer; a star from another sport; last year's margin (one point short); the
recent dual against the team that beat them; dates and sites for district and
state; what the title would be in the program's count.

## 8. Threads: the same people over six seasons

Read in date order, these pieces are a serial. Nobody planned it; one desk kept
writing and the earlier pieces became the later ones' background.

**Thread A, the prodigy.** 2021 notebook item: a freshman, unbeaten in four, the
state tournament cancelled, "I really want to play state, and hopefully I can win."
2023 profile: she won it as a sophomore; the cancelled freshman year is now a lost
chance at four titles; her coach compares her to a four-time champion he also
taught. The 2023 piece re-uses the 2021 fact as regret.

**Thread B, the rivalry.** 2021 tournament story: a freshman wins an unofficial
title 6-0, 6-1 over another freshman; her coach says "This one's going to be really
good." 2022: that coach dies (told in 2024). 2024 profile: now a senior with three
titles; a younger rival beat her at district last year and lost to her at state;
"we're 1-1"; her father is coaching the team in the late coach's honour and will
step aside after the season; she will not play in college. 2024 finals: the rival
wins, 6-3, 6-1; the senior is gracious. 2026 team feature and preview: the rival
won twice and is now herself "not playing high school tennis this year", which
opens the door for the player she beat in a semifinal and a final.

Each piece states the earlier results as plain record: year, round, score.

**Thread C, the family.** 2025 profile: a senior with three titles; her younger
brother won the boys title the same day "on adjacent courts". 2026 preview: the
brother is a junior, lost last year's final, has lost twice this year to a
newcomer, and the two are "on a collision course".

**Thread D, the program.** 2021: a boys team wins the unofficial title; a doubles
final between teammates. 2024: the same school sweeps both team titles; a 2021
freshman is now a senior doubles champion. 2026: the girls were one point short
last year and want it back; younger siblings of earlier players are in the lineup.

**Thread E, the coach.** 2022: a girls coach steps down after 35 seasons. 2025: at
a rival school, the boys coach retires after 29 years, in a Q&A. Both say the same
thing unprompted: people assume private-school players arrive from clubs, and most
do not.

What a thread needs from the desk's memory:

- a first mention that makes a promise ("has 5A on notice", "going to be really
  good");
- the head to head kept as a list of dated scores;
- what each person said they wanted, so a later story can say whether they got it;
- off-court facts that were printed (who coaches whom, who is whose sibling, what
  they plan to study), repeated the same way every time;
- an ending that is allowed to be a loss.

## 9. A coach steps down (pieces 3 and 12)

This fills a gap `VOICE.md` named: the retirement tribute.

Piece 3, written before the coach's last district tournament:

1. Lead on the coach looking back, then a quote about former players stopping by.
2. The record as counts: seasons, team titles of the program's total, singles and
   doubles champions, and the superlative ("No girls coach has won more").
3. Credit passed to the players.
4. Why now, in plain personal terms: a long commute, a parent's death, "35 seemed
   like a nice, round number".
5. **The coach's method, which is the heart of it**: she taught PE and recruited
   athletes from other sports ("If you're an athlete, I can make you a tennis
   player"), with one pair named who had never played and won state as seniors.
6. An administrator on what she built.
7. What she is proudest of (doubles, "I just feel like I understand the strategy
   better").
8. Her own start: hitting against a garage "for hours", self-taught, her school
   and class, her college team.
9. How she got the job: an ad in the Sunday paper, three sports, one of which she
   knew nothing about.
10. The place: banners with champions' names in an indoor facility the school
    took over when a club next door went bankrupt.
11. The streak still alive: at least one state qualifier in all 34 district
    tournaments so far.
12. Close on her, then the administrator: "you can't replace with one person".

Piece 12 is a school magazine Q&A: bold questions that carry the facts, short
answers, then two lists (awards as an athlete, coaching record). Useful as a
second format for a tribute, and as proof that a coach's playing past is a story.

## 10. What to take for the generator

Colour that recurs here and can be invented and kept:

- how a pair came to be a pair, and who asked;
- a style sketch in the coach's words, one line a player;
- a habit of falling behind and coming back;
- siblings in the lineup, in the other team, or winning on the same day;
- a parent coaching, a late coach remembered;
- other sports: the soccer player of the year at fourth singles, a lineup that
  changes because of club volleyball;
- a champion who does not come out for the team this year;
- a transfer who becomes a school's first champion;
- a slogan on a shirt, banners in a gym, an indoor court with a history;
- what a senior will study, and whether they will play on;
- a streak the program is protecting.

Facts the game already has that these stories lean on: head to head with scores,
seeds, class years, last year's finish for every seeded player, team points, who
changed flight or partner, siblings, transfers, coaches' tenure and titles.
