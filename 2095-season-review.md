# JHSAA 2095 Season Retrospective

The 2095 season was less about a dramatic shift in who wins championships than about a major change in what a JHSAA roster means.

The association added 12,594 players in one offseason, a 35 percent increase in the active player population. JV became a much larger part of the competitive ecosystem. 5A introduced the 6-singles/5-doubles State lineup and immediately turned players who previously would have been postseason spectators into championship participants. At the same time, the 8,550-player opportunity-clearing transfer class ran directly into the consequences of those larger rosters: thousands of players who had been assigned to projected varsity openings instead arrived at schools whose depth had changed underneath them.

That makes 2095 unusually useful. Several experiments happened simultaneously, and their effects are visible.

Larchmont Ridge boys and Pacific Friends girls were the only undefeated teams and then won the Tournament of Champions as well. Larchmont finished 31-0; Pacific Friends finished 33-0.

There were no first-time State champions this year. Ten of the 24 champions repeated from 2094. Pacific Gate, Larchmont Ridge, Pacific Friends, Westfield Friends, Cherry Hill North and Ashbrook repeated on the boys side; Seawall, Pacific Friends, Ashbrook and Singleton repeated on the girls side.

There were still meaningful droughts broken. Marigny boys won their first title since 2042. Virginia City boys had not won since 2045. Chicago Island had not won since 2068. Salmon Bay girls ended a drought dating to 2056, and Fletcher-Garrison Hall girls won for the first time since 2071.

## Chicago Island was the postseason story

The #16 seed winning Group 1 is the clearest bracket story of 2095.

Chicago Island opened 8-1 over Pierre and then won four consecutive duals by exactly 5-4: Grant, Jackson Hole, Brown, and Lodestone County in the final.

That was not a low seed getting a favorable couple of rounds. They survived four straight one-point elimination duals and won the program's first championship in 27 years.

Marigny was the other major low-seed run, winning 7A from the #6 position and ending a 53-year championship drought.

Across the entire association, though, 2095 was not substantially more upset-driven than 2094. Nine of 24 champions were #1 seeds in both seasons. Lower seeds won 21.6 percent of State duals, compared with 22.9 percent in 2094.

## The 5A 6S/5D experiment worked as a depth experiment

This is probably the cleanest result of the season.

In 2094, a 5A State team used a median of nine distinct players in a dual.

In 2095, every single 5A State team used exactly **16 distinct players per dual**.

That is the 6S/5D format doing exactly what it was supposed to do.

The effect below the traditional varsity line was enormous. In 2094, only seven boys and seven girls whose end-of-season roster position was #12 through #16 appeared in the 5A State tournament.

In 2095:

**169 boys and 165 girls ranked #12 through #16 appeared in 5A State.**

And they were not ceremonial participants. On State lines involving one of those #12-16 players, the side containing the depth player won 50.9 percent of those lines for boys and 49.5 percent for girls.

That is essentially a coin flip.

So the players being reached by the expanded lineup were not just bodies required to complete a card. They were deciding championship points at approximately the same rate they were losing them.

The format did not make 5A more upset-prone in its first season. Boys lower-seed wins remained 17.1 percent, identical to 2094; girls fell from 14.3 to 11.4 percent. One-point finals also naturally became less common on an 11-point card.

The important result is different: **5A successfully converted roster depth into competitive value.**

That gives the class a real identity. A player can be #13 or #15 at a 5A school and still have a meaningful championship role.

## Raising the roster floors fundamentally changed the association

The player population grew from 35,696 in 2094 to 48,290 in 2095.

Median boys rosters moved from roughly 16-22 players depending on class to 20-32. Girls moved similarly.

5A went from median rosters of 19 boys and 20 girls to 27 and 28.

The most extreme case was 1A. Its median boys roster went from 16 to 31.5; girls went from 16 to 33.

This is important because the roster-floor change and the transfer market interacted. The clearing class had been designed around the old roster environment. Then thousands of additional players entered the system.

The destination that looked like it had an open No. 8 or No. 10 position no longer necessarily had that position when 2095 actually began.

## JV became substantially more real

The number of JV duals barely changed:

2094 had 17,317 combined boys and girls JV duals.
2095 had 17,495.

But the number of players actually using that schedule exploded.

JV participants increased from **15,711 to 28,449**, an 81 percent increase.

Players who appeared in JV but never appeared in a varsity dual increased from **2,706 to 9,626**.

So the roster-floor expansion did not simply add names to the bottom of rosters. The existing JV calendar absorbed them.

Baptist won boys JV State as the #14 seed. Irvington won girls JV State as the #7 seed.

This changes the interpretation of roster depth going forward. Being #17 is no longer synonymous with being competitively invisible. There is now a real second team operating across the association.

That does not eliminate the transfer problem, but it changes which players actually have one.

## The 2095 clearing class did not survive the roster-floor change

There were 8,311 actual returning-player school changes between the two seasons. Of the 8,550-player class we constructed, 8,287 players appear at the exact intended destination in the 2095 export.

So this was a real test of the slate rather than a failed application.

As a varsity-placement system, however, the overall result was poor.

Among those 8,287 intended arrivals:

**28.9 percent finished in the top 11.**
**29.7 percent played at least 10 varsity duals.**
**27.2 percent played zero varsity duals.**
The median final roster position was **#18**.
The median varsity workload was **two duals**.

The result changes substantially by age.

The 1,667 players who had been juniors when moved—the rising-senior cohort—did considerably better. 56.2 percent finished top 11, 58.7 percent made at least 10 varsity appearances, and their median final rank was #11 with 15 varsity duals.

The 6,620 younger movers did not. Only 22.1 percent finished top 11. Their median final rank was #20, their median varsity workload was one dual, and 31.5 percent never appeared in varsity at all.

Against the repo's earlier opportunity-clearing standard, that is a failed placement result.

The reason is also visible. We increased association roster capacity by **12,594 players** after constructing a transfer class around a previous roster environment. The new freshman/depth layer was larger than the transfer class itself.

The destination projections had effectively expired before the season started.

## The cascade's biggest problem is now 1A

The old clearing doctrine treats 1A as the terminal market: if every higher level fails, everybody can eventually find a home there.

That assumption no longer works with the new roster floors.

1A absorbed a net 503 boys and 569 girls through school changes. Its median rosters subsequently became the largest in the numbered classification system.

Among the 1,585 intended transfer arrivals who landed in 1A:

only **14.3 percent** finished top 11;
only **14.1 percent** played 10 or more varsity duals;
**43.2 percent played zero varsity duals**;
the median final position was **#24**.

The terminal market became another reserve warehouse.

That is the clearest transfer-policy correction coming out of 2095. Future clearing cannot use “eventually place them in 1A” as the guarantee. A placement has to terminate when the player has an actual role, not when the algorithm reaches its last classification.

## 5A was the best transfer destination

There is an interesting counterpoint.

Among transferred players who landed in 5A:

42.8 percent finished top 11;
45.1 percent played at least 10 varsity duals;
60.9 percent finished inside the top 16;
the median rank was #14;
the median varsity workload was eight duals.

Those were the best results of any numbered classification.

That becomes even more important under the new postseason format because #12-16 are not necessarily failed placements anymore. Those are potentially State lineup positions.

This is the first evidence that the 5A depth experiment and the transfer market can reinforce each other. The class can absorb players who are too good to disappear into somebody else's JV but do not need to become S1 or S2 to have a meaningful season.

## Transfers changed rosters far more than they changed competitive hierarchy

There is almost no association-wide relationship between a program's net transfer count and its year-over-year change in winning percentage.

Some of the biggest boys improvements happened at programs that actually lost more transfers than they received: Lake Esperanza went from 8-19 to 21-9 with a net transfer flow of -3; Narragansett improved from 5-21 to 16-13 while going -5; Montpelier went 15-11 to 26-3 while going -4.

The same thing occurred on the other side. Programs could receive net transfers and still collapse.

So the 8,000-player market did not simply manufacture a new competitive order. Its primary effect was on individual placement and roster structure—which is where it should be evaluated.

## Program arcs

The largest boys improvement was Lake Esperanza, from 8-19 to 21-9. Narragansett went 5-21 to 16-13; Mesa Verde 4-21 to 14-13; Aspen Hollow 6-16 to 18-11; Halbrook 7-15 to 21-11. Montpelier jumped from 15-11 to 26-3.

The largest boys declines included Fort Paynes, 25-4 to 6-10; Dry Fork, 17-10 to 4-21; Haddon, 16-8 to 3-12; and Grizzly Gulch, which fell from 35-2 to 19-12.

For girls, Granite improved from 5-19 to 16-13, Blue Valley from 11-16 to 24-8, Mar Vista from 7-18 to 18-11 and W.H. Harrison from 4-22 to 14-15.

Barley Gap fell from 23-10 to 4-25. Monongahela dropped from 22-9 to 6-22, the only program in either gender to move from at least a .700 winning percentage in 2094 to below .300 in 2095.
