Play to Clinch — The Game Guide

A complete, sectioned reference to how the game actually works: the rules, the economy, the ladder of systems, and the numbers behind them. This is the in-app rendering of docs/GUIDE.md in the repository — that file is the source of truth (and the place to point an LLM if you want help reasoning about the game); this page exists for reading it without leaving the game.

How this differs from the engineering docs: the repo's CLAUDE.md is guardrails for an editing agent ("don't casually change this number, here's why"), and the docs/AAR-*.md files are the changelog — one report per change, in the order it happened. This guide is the rulebook, organized by system rather than by when it was built. Appendix B indexes every AAR by topic if you want the origin story behind a rule.

Part I
Getting Started

1. What Is Play to Clinch

Play to Clinch is a single-player, god-mode-friendly tennis dynasty sim. You run a world: every NCAA-style division (D1, D2, D3, D4) across men's and women's tennis, all evolving together on one shared clock. You can coach one program's roster and lineup directly ("career mode") or simply watch the whole universe unfold as a spectator — every game still simulates in full either way.

Matches are simulated point by point by a real engine (serve/rally probabilities, box scores, playstyles), not dice-rolled at the final-score level. Seasons run on real schedules with conferences, postseason brackets, recruiting classes, a transfer portal, injuries, coaching carousels, a pro league, and a whole second sport (high-school tennis) feeding into the college one. Almost everything is seed-deterministic — the same seed replays the same world — with exactly one deliberate exception: injuries roll on real entropy, so a roster's health can never be save-scummed away by rerolling. Everything else can be, and that's fine — it's a single-player sandbox, and reproducibility is a feature, not a purity test.

2. Starting a League

The onboarding screen asks four questions:

  • Which divisions and genders are active — run the whole NCAA (D1–D4, men's and women's) or a subset; a smaller active set advances faster.
  • A name/region preset and international share — where players are generated from, cosmetic flavor rather than a competitive lever. Open Fine-tune by nation/region to author the international mix directly, region by region, and use Download to keep that mix as a small .json file. Load file… reads it back into a brand-new save, so a geography you tuned once never has to be retyped. (Save to this world keeps a named copy inside the current save only — the downloaded file is the one that outlives it.)
  • A program to coach, or spectator mode — pick a school to control its lineup, recruiting priorities, and job decisions ("Your Team" appears in the sidebar), or leave it blank to watch the whole world with no team of your own.
  • A world seed — optional. Blank gives a fresh random world every time; a typed seed gives a reproducible universe (same rosters, same recruits, same everything except injuries).

Starting a new league always replaces the previous save — there is exactly one world per save file (see Appendix A).

3. The World Clock

Every active division × gender universe advances together, on one clock, behind one button — the "Advance week" control in the top bar. This is deliberate: if D1 men's and D1 women's could advance independently, they'd drift to different weeks with different conference races and nobody would notice until the numbers stopped lining up. There is exactly one advance surface in the whole app.

Clicking it does one of a few things depending on where the world is in its cycle: most of the time it plays one week of duals everywhere, runs the cross-division slate, and drips recruiting; at a fall-portal hold it takes you to review and commit portal proposals (§13); at season's end it steps through the offseason ladder, one event per click (§6).

Part II
The Competitive Structure

4. Divisions, Conferences & Prestige

Four divisions, each its own self-contained tournament ladder, run for both men's and women's tennis:

  • D1 — the top tier. Conferences are tiered (Blue Blood / Major / High-major / Mid-major / Low-major) by prestige percentile, not a fixed list, so realignment never needs special-casing.
  • D2 — a wide middle tier that recruits aggressively and takes players D3/D4 can't reach.
  • D3 — the widest-variety, lowest-floor tier, with a thin "gem" allocation for its top-20 programs by prestige.
  • D4 — an academic-first tier carved out of D3's strongest LACs (NESCAC/UAA/SCIAC/NCAC-style programs): real academic admission gates, but a scholarship-budget economy that plays more like D1/D2 than like D3.

Prestige is dynamic — it drifts every year by how a program over- or under-performs its own expectation. A low-major that keeps winning above its station climbs the prestige ladder (and its recruiting budget with it); a sliding blue-blood drifts down. The drift is bounded and self-correcting, and shows up on the Data Portal as a program's history across seasons. Conference realignment happens periodically as a curated content change (new conferences, programs switching leagues) — never something that happens automatically mid-save.

5. Dual Match Formats

Real college tennis plays 6 singles + 3 doubles (consolidated to one doubles point) everywhere, for court-availability and Title IX reasons this game doesn't have to honor. Play to Clinch instead gives each division its own shape — a bigger singles card is a much better lever for expressing how deep a division's talent runs:

DivisionFormatPointsClinch at
D110 singles + 5 doubles, doubles consolidated to one point116
D2 / D38 singles + 3 doubles, every doubles line its own point116
D410 singles + 3 doubles, every doubles line its own point137

D1 is the only division that keeps doubles consolidated into a single point — a deliberate cap on how much a team can win purely by stacking doubles depth. The engine's own bare-call default (the exhibition dual simulator, cups, tests) is the classic 6-singles/3-doubles, clinch-at-4 shape; no division actually plays a real season under it.

Because the singles card is so much deeper than real college tennis, the recruiting/scholarship economy was deliberately not resized to match — a D1 program's paid recruiting core still only covers six scholarship-caliber starters (§8), so courts 7–10 are walk-on and portal depth by design. A full lineup that thins out toward the bottom is the intended shape of a D1 roster, not a bug.

Play-play in D3/D4: in the regular season and the Preseason NIT, D3/D4 duals play every singles match to completion instead of abandoning dead rubbers once the dual is clinched — real ITA D3 format, purely to generate fuller player stats for portal evaluation. It never changes who won the dual (the clinching point still locks the result) — only fills in the margin, so a D3 final line of 6–2 or 7–0 is normal. D1/D2 and every division's postseason duals stop at the clinch.

Postseason lineups are strict best-six: in conference tournaments and the NCAA Championship, the healthy top six players by results-based rating start — no rotation, no resting. The regular season and the Preseason NIT deliberately keep rotation (everybody plays at some point); elimination duals do not.

6. The Season Calendar & the Offseason Ladder

A season plays out on a real calendar shape: non-conference play front-loads the early schedule (a team's conference duals don't start until it clears its own non-conference slate, typically weeks 4–5), D1 layers six preseason ITA weeks on top of that, and conference races heat up in the back half. It's completely normal to see a big overall record next to an 0–0 or 2–1 conference record early in a season — that's front-loading, not a bug.

Once every universe finishes its postseason, the world enters the offseason ladder — a sequence of distinct, visible events, each its own click, never silently bundled:

  1. Awards — individual and team honors are stamped for the year (§19).
  2. Davis Cup / Billie Jean King Cup — national-team cups play over the year's rosters, before anyone graduates (§18).
  3. Year rollover — graduation, development, recruiting classes signed, prestige momentum applied, the calendar year advances.
  4. Jefferson's high-school season (JHSAA) — Jefferson's high schools play their entire season in one step, before the next recruiting cycle needs their seniors (§21).
  5. Pro-league offseason — GTT drafts the class that just graduated (§22).
  6. Preseason — the new season begins: ITA/Preseason NIT, then a fall transfer portal hold (§13), then the regular season.
Part III
Building a Program

7. Rosters & Walk-Ons

Rosters are not a flat number — capacity and sourcing both vary by division:

DivisionRoster capWalk-on sourcing
D112None — the class tops out at the 6-seat scholarship core and stops; depth backfills from the portal or runs short.
D210From the recruit pool only, never auto-generated.
D316Leftover pool recruits first, then auto-generated fill.
D416Leftover pool recruits first, then auto-generated fill.

Every division carries a hard floor: it must field its own full lineup card (D1/D4 ten players, D2/D3 eight) at minimum. "D1 carries no walk-on depth" is about keeping D1 rosters smaller than everyone else's, never a license to field an unplayable lineup — a short side degrades gracefully rather than crashing, and only a fully empty side stops the world outright.

8. The Recruiting & Scholarship Economy

This is the game's central resource-management system. The short version: a program does not have a flat number of scholarships. It has a budget (set by conference tier) and it spends that budget on recruits, who cost scholarships by star rating. The "8" you'll see on a roster's aid page is a separate, downstream display layer — see §9 — not the actual economy.

Conference tier sets the starting band; a program's own dynamic prestige sets where inside that band it lands. D4 funds a 3-floor / 6–8-top budget but only admits recruits above a per-program academic gate (~SAT 960 floor up to ~1400 for MIT-tier programs) — D2 still out-recruits D4 on average because D2 will take anybody and D4 can't admit everyone it can afford.

Attainment is gated, not just afforded — a program also needs to clear a floor to attract a tier: blue chips need ≥16.5 budget (blue-bloods only), 5★ needs ≥10.5 (Major and up), 4★ needs ≥5.0 (any funded D1, or a top D2 — this floor cascades so 4★ talent always finds a home), 3★-and-below can go anywhere. Clustering at the top is earned: only blue-bloods land blue chips, majors top out at a 5★/4★ core, mid/low-majors build on 4★/3★, and a low-major will never land a 5★.

A program's recruiting radar only reaches its own level, mid-cycle — a D1 program never sees a sub-45-strength recruit on its in-season board (the recruit still dreams of D1 — the gate is about visibility, not aspiration). D1's class tops up its 6-seat scholarship core and stops; it never signs a recruit into a walk-on seat. D2 is the one deliberate exception with a wider reach, so it absorbs mid-tier talent aggressively rather than letting it leak to D3/D4.

Other realism levers, each marginal by design: playing time matters to a recruit's decision (weight 0.35, below prestige's own pull) — recruits lean toward programs where they'd crack the current top six; warm weather / big-city appeal are small tiebreaks (weight 0.06 each) that can nudge a recruit against their home-state pull, never override it.

10. Coaching & Player Development

Coaches are persistent entities with careers of their own — they take jobs, get poached, retire, and build a lineage (former players can become coaches). A coach's development score drives how fast their roster's players grow each year, and the effect is strong: ±30% on growth, anchored to the observed range of generated development scores (roughly 40–65) rather than the full theoretical scale, so the swing is felt rather than compressed away. Recruiting is coach-flavored too: a coach's localism and home-country ties tilt which recruits their program actually lands. Development only applies to active college rosters — juniors and pro-tour decline are coach-free.

11. The Player Model — STR, OVR & Playstyles

Every player carries a rich set of attributes on a 20–80 scouting scale (serve power, groundstrokes, movement, mental game, doubles skills, hardcourt comfort — the game is hardcourt-only). Two different numbers summarize a player:

  • OVR (Overall) — a static, card-based evaluation of raw talent. What a scout would write down looking at the attribute sheet. Doesn't move based on results.
  • STR — a UTR-style rating on a distinctive 31–57 scale, solved from actual results. Drives seeding, rankings, and the Power Index. Two players with identical OVR can carry very different STR if one's been winning and the other hasn't.

Pros (the one tier generated above the normal 80-grade ceiling, into a 100-grade headroom) read measurably better than any college player on both scales.

STR ↔ UTR ↔ WTN conversion. UTR (1.00–16.50) is the upward-facing real-world comparison; WTN runs the other way (40 beginner → 1 elite pro). UTR = 1 + (STR − 31)/26 × 15.5 · WTN = 40 − (STR − 31)/26 × 39. Endpoints are exact; off-anchor values are approximate. Also on Analytics Bureau → Lineup Lab as a collapsed fold. OVR has no conversion — it's the separate static 20–80 scouting scale, not a rating.

Playstyles are weighted archetype profiles, so a serve-and-volleyer's volley moves further than their overhead, and the league's prevailing "meta" rotates by era — the game's texture shifts across decades the way real tennis has moved through serve-and-volley, power baseline, and athletic-defense periods.

Part IV
Living Seasons

12. Injuries

Injuries are the one deliberately non-deterministic system in the game — they roll on real entropy, not the world's seed. Every other system replays identically from the same seed; injuries will not, on purpose ("I never wanted a deterministic sim… save-scumming is fine, I'm the only player," to quote the design rule behind it).

Calibration: a base injury rate of 2.5% per dual, scaled by a player's durability, tuned so roughly half a starter is hurt at any given moment across a program. About 1 in 100 injuries is season-ending; otherwise a player is out for 1–6 duals. A season-ending injury triggers a medical redshirt — the player repeats their class year with an RS- tag (RS-Jr → RS-Sr → a fifth year) that persists until they finally graduate.

13. The Transfer Portal

The fall transfer portal is the only in-season player movement, and it runs once, right after the ITA opener, as a deliberately curated reshuffle rather than a free-for-all migration:

  • Movers ("risers") are picked on ability, not early-season results. A riser has to be a top-2 starter on their current team and clear the median strength of a higher division to be eligible.
  • Capped at 30 risers per gender on a fresh world — roughly 60 total moves. A receiving program takes at most one riser, so movement spreads out; displaced players cascade down to fill the seats that opens up.
  • A mover's season splits into two stints — their record at the old school (frozen at the portal) and at the new school for the rest of the year — both preserved in career history.
  • The slate is editable: redirect a proposed mover, add one the simulation missed, or drop one entirely before committing.

Outside the fall portal window, the Recruiting Board, Portal Rankings, and My Transfer Targets pages surface the broader recruiting pipeline — signings drip across the season, skewed so higher-ranked recruits commit later, the way real recruiting cycles play out.

14. Rankings — the Power Index (TOSS)

Every team is rated by the Power Index, a composite of three weighted components — full formula and methodology at Methodology in the nav, summarized here:

Power Index = (APR × 40%) + (FQI × 40%) + (oGS × 20%)

APR (Adjusted Power Rating) is an RPI-style blend of a team's own win% and its opponents' (and their opponents') win%. FQI (Flight Quality Index) measures how well a team performs at each individual flight, weighted by that flight's competitive significance and the specific opponent's strength. oGS (opponent-weighted Game Share) is the share of actual games — not just flights — a team won, scaled the same way, so a lopsided flight loss reads as more competitive than a blowout one.

Flight weights are per division — because each division plays its own dual shape (§5), the weight table differs by division and there is deliberately no fallback: an unweighted flight is a missing decision, not a free default. Head-to-head results break close ties after Power Index is computed.

Part V
Championships & Cups

15. The Preseason NIT (ITA Kickoff & National Team Indoor)

D1's season opens with a six-week preseason event (three weeks for non-D1) before the regular season begins: the top 60 D1 teams by prior-year ranking are snake-distributed into 15 cosmetic four-team Kickoff Weekend sites (seeded 1v4/2v3, single elimination); the 15 site winners plus a top-ranked auto-bid host form a 16-team National Team Indoor bracket. D2/D3 skip Kickoff Weekend entirely and run their own 8-team Indoor straight from the prior year's ranking.

Results here count toward a team's live Power Index and its NCAA at-large/seeding résumé (never conference record). It shares the exact same bracket-drawing machinery as the NCAA Championship page, with seeds read back from the persisted draw rather than recomputed live.

16. The NCAA Championship

Selection and seeding are one score, computed once and locked:

Committee Seed Score = (Power Index rank × 45%) + (ITA points rank × 30%) + (auto-bid tier bonus × 15%) + (last-5 form × 10%)

The auto-bid bonus is tiered by conference strength (top conferences ~100 points, mid ~40, low ~12), so a champion from a weak conference still gets a bid but merit still dominates overall.

Field size: 96 teams in D1 (top 32 seeds bye, 33–96 play a within-region play-in), 64 teams in D2/D3/D4 (no play-in). The field splits into four S-curve regions, dealt serpentine so every region carries balanced overall strength; region champions meet at the national semifinal stage. Region names are purely cosmetic and carry no geography.

True seeding — no conference separation. The bracket is never rearranged to keep teams from the same conference apart, in any division or gender. The only anti-collision rules are avoiding an early regular-season rematch and avoiding two conference auto-bid teams meeting in round one. Once locked, the field/seeds/regions are always read back from that lock, never recomputed.

17. Singles & Doubles Individual Championships

Run once a division's team bracket finishes. Singles pools every program's top 2 players and takes the best 128 by ability. Doubles takes each program's #1 pair plus the best 64 pairs overall, played through the real four-player doubles engine. Format is best-of-3, no-ad, with a 10-point match tiebreak deciding the third set. Only the top quarter of the draw is seeded (32 of 128 in singles, 16 of 64 in doubles) — seeds 1 and 2 anchor opposite ends, byes go to the top seeds, everyone else draws at random.

18. Davis Cup / Billie Jean King Cup

National-team knockout cups — Davis Cup (men) and Billie Jean King Cup (women) — run every offseason, before the year rolls over, so graduating seniors get to represent their country one last time. Squads regroup the entire current player pool by country, pulling from every division at once. Any nation with at least 4 eligible players fields a squad, trimmed to the largest power of two (at most 32 nations) by squad strength. A tie is 4 singles plus one doubles rubber, first to 3 rubbers. Titles are stamped as real honors onto a player's career record, alongside their college and pro-tour honors.

Part VI
The Wider World

20. Jefferson — the 55th State

Jefferson (JF) is a fictional alternate-history state (~17.6M people) standing on real ground across southern Oregon, northern California, northern Nevada, and western Idaho. It's the 55th entry alongside the real 50 states, DC, and the territories, treated as an entirely ordinary state in every system — its own region, its own recruit generation, its own D1–D4 programs.

Four D1 programs, in existing conferences: the flagship University of Jefferson (Pac-16), Jefferson State (WAC), Southern Jefferson (Big West), and Jefferson A&M (CUSA). The Jefferson Valley Conference is D2 (8 programs). In total, Jefferson hosts 39 colleges across D1–D4, deliberately kept to about 2.2 programs per million people (matching California) rather than the dozen-plus D1 programs a state that size might otherwise "deserve."

Jefferson develops talent; it doesn't hoard it. Jefferson produces recruits at a rate matched by very few states — second only to California — and most of that talent leaves to play elsewhere, exactly the way California, Texas, and Florida's talent does. A big in-state D1 footprint was never the intended signal of "this is a good tennis state" — production, not retention, is the measure.

21. The JHSAA — Jefferson's High School Season

Jefferson's own high-school athletic association — roughly 335 girls' and 292 boys' programs — plays a complete season inside this engine, browsable at /jhsaa, and its graduating seniors are Jefferson's entries on the college recruiting board. It's simulated once per year, in one step, at the very start of the offseason ladder.

  • Two format axes. Dual shape: regular season is 5 singles + 2 doubles, the state tournament is 1 singles + 4 doubles — both odd, so a dual can never tie. Scoring: every JHSAA match is no-ad, and doubles is a full best-of-3 (not the college 8-game pro set).
  • A real double round-robin schedule — every district opponent is played home and away, so district size sets season length. Non-district opponents are matched by geography then talent, gated to the same classification or one apart.
  • Seeding runs on TOSS — the same APR/FQI/oGS composite the college game uses, computed over the whole gender at once and over the regular season only.
  • State qualification is a ladder, the same shape for every classification and both genders: Sectionals (every non-protected team; byes/play-ins as needed; cuts to 32; a multi-round Sectionals opens with Areas) → Wards (32→16) → Regionals (16 Ward champions + 16 protected — district champions first, then best cutoff TOSS) → Zonals (16→8). Three ways into State: the eight Zonal champions qualify automatically and are the State draw's top seeds (in a 24-team field the eight first-round byes are theirs); a district champion is guaranteed access even if it loses in the ladder (access, not a bye — a geographic safeguard so no region is shut out); and every remaining berth goes through the recovery rounds. State is 32 teams in 7A and 24 everywhere else; nobody sits at home and qualifies on a rating — the old TOSS wild cards are gone.
  • How the recovery rounds actually work. The berth arithmetic is dynamic: State field − Zonal champions − district-guarantee teams = berths still open. The candidate pool is the Regional losers (entering at Super Regionals), the Zonal losers (joining at Semi-State), and — where the numbers need bodies, mainly 7A — the best-TOSS Ward losers, who get another chance to play, never a berth. The pool is usually bigger than the open berths by only a handful, so each recovery round is a cut, not a full bracket: only enough duals are played to eliminate the difference, the pairings drawing from the bottom of the round's TOSS order, and the teams above the line take a bye straight through. A strong Regional loser can therefore reach State as a lucky loser — beaten in the ladder, protected by rating through a recovery round — which is the same privilege a district champion's guarantee expresses geographically. Byes are listed under each round on the bracket page's Road to State.
  • No double byes. A team that sits out Super Regionals must play at Semi-State; the Semi-State bye can only be earned by playing — and winning — a Super Regional. One bye buys a round of rest, never a ride to State without a recovery dual: every recovery qualifier either won its way through or won the dual that followed its one bye.
  • Postseason awards are résumé selections, shown on the Honors tab and archived season by season. Per classification and gender, in one pass off the completed season — every dual, including State and the TOC: State Player of the Year; All-State First / Second / Third Team — plus a Fourth Team in 7A — then Honorable Mention; and for every district a District Player of the Year and one All-District team. Separately, once for the whole association, one All-Region team per geographic region. Every team is the same size: 10 singles + 8 doubles teams — 26 athletes. Selectors weigh the player's record and how much they played; the flight it was earned at; the quality of the individual opponents faced; quality wins over other candidates; good losses, which cost little against elite opposition; head-to-head between otherwise level résumés; and postseason matches, weighted up. Ability ratings are deliberately not an input — no OVR, talent, class year or recruiting rank — so a great player on a poor team can win Player of the Year, and an unbeaten record against a weak slate does not automatically beat a strong one against the best in the class. Honorable Mention is a threshold, not a team: no slot count, so a deep classification honours a couple of dozen extra players and a thin one only a handful, and a good season can miss it. At most two HM selections per school; the numbered teams have no such cap.
  • Doubles honours go to pairings, not to individual doubles players. "8 doubles" means eight doubles teams — sixteen athletes. A doubles selection is two players who actually competed together, judged only on the matches they played as a team against other teams, so a player who partnered around is a separate candidate with each partner and the record beside a pairing is the partnership's. Which category an athlete is considered in follows where they actually played, so nobody holds a singles slot and a doubles slot on the same team. A team is only as large as the candidates who cleared the bar, so a thin region occasionally crowns one pairing fewer rather than honouring one that did not earn it.
  • Flight is structural, not a bonus. A 19–7 season at #1 singles is bigger than 25–1 at #5, because the #1 spent the year playing every other program's best player. All-State is a #1/#2 honour and All-Region reaches #3; below that a player is selected only on extraordinary evidence — a near-perfect record and a win over somebody who played higher up the card. All-District broadens further down, because a district is a smaller pond, but the hierarchy holds. Every season archives a Flight check, shown on the Honors page: the count by flight at each level, with every below-band pick named, positioned and recorded.
  • All-Region is the whole region, every classification. There is no 7A All-Region team — there is a Gold Valley All-Region team, drawn from every program in that region whatever its enrollment, which is how it works in real life. All-State is the classification and a district is (classification, name), so those two are per class; a region is neither, and selecting one per classification made it a second All-District — four or five schools per class-region, and every school placing somebody. Region-wide it is one team drawn from ~40 programs, so it is harder to make than All-District and open to a 2A school that produced a regional #1.
  • The regions are nowhere near the same size, so the honour scales with them. Halbrook Basin has 115 boys'/128 girls' programs; North Range has 17. A region of 45+ programs crowns a First and a Second Team (Halbrook Basin, Gold Valley, Harborline, South Coast, Ashbury Metro); the rest crown a single unnumbered team. Halbrook Basin alone — nearly twice the next region — also crowns an All-Region Honorable Mention, on exactly the All-State terms: a merit threshold rather than a third team, no slot count, same criteria and flight weighting, capped at one selection per school. The thresholds are on the program count, not a list of region names.
  • An athlete is honoured in one category, the one they were better at. Kids cannot play singles and doubles in the same match, so each player is judged where their season stood higher — their standing in the singles field against their strongest partnership's standing in the field of partnerships — and is not a candidate in the other. Within a level they are honoured once: a player with two strong partnerships takes their better one, never a place on both the First and the Second Team.
  • The Honors page is four views of one slate — All-State, All-Region, All-District and the method — with a dropdown for the classification, one for the region and one for the district, so a team can be read and cited without scrolling past four hundred selections. Every season is archived as it was awarded and the year selector reads it back.
  • Program archetypes are a durable, editable school-level trait — blue_blood (talent that clusters at the top of a lineup), development (ordinary freshmen who grow into the best seniors in the state), doubles (a small doubles-only boost), and upstart (a temporary, expiring multi-year hot streak) — representing facilities and program culture, never a school's classification.

22. The Pro Tour (GTT)

Global Team Tennis is a persistent, multi-season, co-ed professional league layered on top of the college game, built around one idea: player continuity. A college graduate keeps their real player identity into the pros, so a college Player of the Year and a later GTT MVP can be the exact same person, visible on one career page.

Format: 8 franchises, a double round-robin regular season, and a top-4 single-elimination playoff. Each tie is 9 lines — 3 men's singles, 3 women's singles, 3 mixed doubles — first to 5, every line a single no-ad Fast4 set. Doubles carries far more relative weight here than in the college game (a third of every tie, versus one point of seven in a dual).

College → pro pipeline: every offseason, that year's real graduating class is drafted in via a reverse-standings snake draft. About 95% of intake is D1 talent. Grad-transfer ("Gr") pros enter through a standalone Pro Round at the top of each draft. In-season movement is a deterministic weekly waiver wire, not a trade market — a club may only replace its weakest reserve, and only with a genuine upgrade, so starters are never at risk.

Locked to the college world's clock: GTT season i runs alongside college calendar year 2026+i, and the pro offseason can't advance until the college world finishes that year.
