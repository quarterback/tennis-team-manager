# Four Regions, No Pods
### Drawing a 96-team bracket that's balanced *and* readable

Brackets are where I lose entire weekends, because a bracket is a design object pretending to be a formality. Everybody thinks the hard part is simulating the games. The hard part is *drawing* them — deciding, before a single ball is hit, who's even allowed to meet whom and when. Get that wrong and the most talented team in the field can be eliminated by geography in the second round, and the sim will report it as a normal Tuesday.

My field is big — 96 teams in the top division, because the league is enormous and a 64-cap would get eaten alive by automatic bids alone. So the first real question was: how do you carve 96 teams into a shape that's fair *and* legible? And there were two honest answers on the table, so I'll show my work.

**Option one: host-site pods.** Very tennis. The top sixteen seeds host six-team sites, everybody funnels through their pod, sixteen survivors advance. It's authentic to how college tennis actually runs early rounds, and I almost shipped it. The reason I didn't is that pods make the whole tournament hostage to host placement. Your draw is decided less by your seed than by which pod you happened to land in, and a strong team can get buried in a brutal local group while a weaker one strolls through a soft one. It optimizes for *venue*. I wanted to optimize for *balance*.

**Option two: regions.** Very basketball. Four regions, each a self-contained bracket, region champions meeting at the end. Less tennis-flavored, but it does the one thing pods can't: it lets me guarantee the field is evenly distributed by *strength* before anyone plays.

I went regions, and the tool that makes regions fair is the **S-curve**. You build one honest national seed list, 1 through 96, then deal it out across the four regions like a snake. Line one goes A, B, C, D. Line two reverses — D, C, B, A. Line three flips back. Down the whole list, serpentine. The effect is that every region ends up with exactly one team from each seed line, and when you add up the seeds, all four regions come out *dead even*. Not approximately. The same total, by construction. No region is the "group of death" because the math refuses to let one exist.

Inside a 24-team region, the top eight seeds get a bye and seeds nine through twenty-four play an opening round — nine versus twenty-four, ten versus twenty-three, on down — and the survivors join the byes for a normal sixteen-team region bracket. Win that, win your region, and you're one of four teams headed to the national semifinals. (The smaller divisions run 64-team fields, which is just the same idea with four regions of sixteen and no play-in.) I arranged the regions so the overall one and two seeds sit in opposite halves and can only meet in the final, which is the oldest rule in bracketing and still the most important one.

Then, and only then, comes the cleanup pass — the part nobody sees and everybody feels. Within each region I nudge the draw to avoid same-conference first-rounders, avoid rematches of games these teams already played (with the penalty climbing each additional time they've met), and keep two automatic qualifiers apart early. Crucially, every one of those swaps happens *within a seed line*. A team only ever trades places with a peer on its own line, so the draw gets cleaner without anybody's earned seed moving. Fairness in the structure, freshness in the matchups, and no horse-trading that costs a team the seed it bled for.

The last touch is pure decoration and I refuse to apologize for it. The four regions get **names** — but rotating, cosmetic ones, pulled at random each season from a big pool of trivia-league names. No East, no West, no geography to argue about, because there's no geography in the bracket at all; placement is strength, full stop. One year you're the one seed in the Bayou region, the next you're the one seed in Cosmos. It means absolutely nothing and I love it, because the labels finally stop pretending to be a map and admit they're just jerseys for the four quarters of a very carefully balanced draw.
