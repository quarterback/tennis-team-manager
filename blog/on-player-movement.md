My decision matrix is:

Start with the player’s current situation.

An elite player becomes movable when one of these is true:

they are carrying a bad team and have clearly outgrown the level around them;
they are on a decent team but the program is going nowhere;
they are buried at D3/D4 despite having S1/D1 ability elsewhere;
they have two or more years left and can reasonably seek a harder environment;
they are a junior whose final season would otherwise be wasted in the same role.
Ask what kind of move it is.

There are four main types:

Challenge move: elite player leaves a weak/small program for a stronger or larger-class program.
Backfill move: strong player joins an established contender losing a major senior class.
Role correction: talented player leaves D3/D4 to become S1, S2, D1, or D2 elsewhere.
Cohort move: two or more players who already know/play with each other move together into a program that can become a contender.
The destination needs an actual need.

I prefer programs that:

just lost S1 or multiple top doubles players;
were already good but one or two courts short of winning;
have a strong returning core but obvious holes;
are historically good and entering a reload year;
are emerging programs where one elite player changes the ceiling.

I avoid sending elite players to a team that already returns a complete championship lineup unless the move itself has a strong reason.

The move should improve the player’s competitive environment.

For an elite player, the destination should usually give them at least one of:

stronger daily practice partners;
harder league competition;
a jump in classification;
a real state-title opportunity;
a more meaningful lineup role;
better teammates around them in doubles;
a chance to stop being the only serious player on the roster.
I care about class progression.

Grade matters a lot.

For 9th graders:

I’ll make aggressive challenge moves because they have three years left.
A jump from 2A/3A to 7A/8A/9A is plausible if they are already dominant.

For 10th graders:

this is the ideal transfer age;
they have proven themselves and still have two full seasons left.

For 11th graders:

the move needs a sharper reason;
usually being stuck, carrying a bad team, or filling a major contender vacancy;
I am less likely to move a junior just for novelty.
I don’t automatically move stars off bad teams.

Sometimes a great player staying put is more interesting.

I move them when the gap is extreme:

player is 65–70 OVR;
team is 3–22, 7–22, 0–27, etc.;
player is already winning 70–90% of matches;
nothing around them suggests the program will catch up.

That is when “I want better competition” becomes obvious.

I distinguish a star transfer from a roster raid.

A contender getting one 65 OVR player to replace a graduated S1 is normal.

A contender getting four 65+ players when it already returns eight starters is usually boring unless we are intentionally building a superteam.

So:

one elite player = targeted backfill;
two = deliberate push;
three or more = explicit roster-construction experiment.
For underused elite players, current role matters more than team record.

A 55–60 OVR player who has spent three years at D3/D4 is movable even if their team is excellent.

The question becomes:
“Could this player be S1 or D1 on 70% of the association?”

If yes, they are a legitimate transfer candidate.

I use program history.

Eight years in, programs have identities.

That changes the logic.

Examples:

Rockridge can plausibly attract elite 9A players because it is an established power.
Baptist can plausibly make an aggressive title push because it already has championship pedigree.
Paddock County can attract players because it is an emerging contender.
a historically irrelevant school needs a stronger reason or a coordinated investment story before elite players start appearing there.
I want the move to create a future question.

The best transfers produce a storyline:

Can this star win at 9A?
Can this contender replace its graduating class?
Can this buried doubles player become a singles ace?
Can two stars turn a 12–17 program into a state contender?
Does this public program become a regional destination?
Was the player carrying the old school, or was the old school holding them back?

That is usually the final test.

Condensed version for the other agent:

ELITE TRANSFER DECISION RULES

Move a player when:
- they are clearly too good for a bad team;
- they are underused relative to ability;
- they have at least 1–2 years left and need a harder challenge;
- an established contender has a real graduating-class hole;
- several local players can plausibly form a cohort.

Prefer destinations that:
- need S1/D1/top-four help;
- lost major seniors;
- are historically strong or clearly emerging;
- offer a meaningful class/competition jump;
- give the player a larger role or stronger practice environment.

Avoid:
- piling stars onto already-complete champions without a reason;
- moving juniors randomly;
- moving stars simply because they are good;
- annual private-school-style roster raids unless intentionally creating a superteam;
- sending players somewhere they would still be buried.

For each move, answer:
1. Why would this player leave?
2. Why this school?
3. What role do they get?
4. What does the receiving team need?
5. What new storyline does the move create?


he better model is closer to: what would make this particular player plausibly choose this particular program at this point in their high-school career?

The biggest correction is that “mismatch” is not itself a problem. A 67 OVR kid playing S1 for a terrible team can be perfectly plausible. Plenty of great players stay at mediocre schools because that is their school, their friends are there, their family is there, they like being the star, or they have no reason to leave. The transfer only becomes interesting when there is an actual pull somewhere else.

For me the order should be more like this:

What is the player’s story right now?
Star carrying a bad team, buried doubles player, young breakout, established junior, member of an unusually strong local age cohort, etc.
Is there a believable reason to leave?
Wanting stronger daily competition. Friends assembling somewhere else. A coach with a reputation. A program making a title push. A huge graduating class opening spots. A public-school magnet emerging locally. Three years stuck at D4. Moving from a tiny class after proving everything there. Those are reasons. “OVR too high for team rank” is not.
Is there a believable reason to choose this specific school?
Existing power. Emerging power. Local peer group. Geographic convenience. Family connection. Coach. Public choice/open enrollment. Familiar junior-tennis network. A team that is obviously one player away. A school where several players are already converging.
Does the move change the player’s experience?
It does not always need to mean more playing time. An S1 at a 2A school can remain S1 at an 8A school and still have made a meaningful move because practices, schedule, teammates and postseason ambitions are completely different.
Does it make sense in the program’s history?
Eight years in, schools have reputations. A perennial contender can attract somebody without much explanation. An emerging program can suddenly become fashionable after a breakthrough. A historically awful program should not randomly land a 70 OVR player unless we have deliberately created a reason for that program to be changing.
What does age do to the decision?
Freshmen and sophomores can make ambitious moves because there is time to build something. Juniors need a stronger trigger: last shot at a title, years buried in doubles, a major challenge jump, following a cohort, or a program losing exactly the players that open their route.
Where is talent already flowing?
This matters more than filling holes. If two good players choose a school, a third choosing it becomes more plausible. Success creates its own gravity. That is how you get Condotti, Baptist, Rockridge, a new public-school magnet, etc. The simulation should sometimes reinforce concentrations rather than constantly redistribute talent toward equilibrium.
Sometimes intentionally create an experiment.
There should be occasional moves whose purpose is basically: “what if these four good players all decided to play together?” That is realistic too. High-school teams sometimes emerge because one age-group cohort converges at the same school and suddenly produces three dominant seasons.
