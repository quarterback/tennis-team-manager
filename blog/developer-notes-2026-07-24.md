# Developer Notes: 7.24.26
### Making the box score read like the player who produced it

I spent this stretch on the match statistics, the aces and double faults and winners and unforced errors that show up after a dual. I had been reading full box scores and kept noticing the same four things. Winners were too scarce. Aces almost never landed. Double faults were rare enough to feel absent. And the unforced error totals looked the same from one player to the next.

Everything on that list comes out of one file, the point resolution tables in `engine/rally.py`. The season runs its scorelines through a fast game level model, and a second pass replays each recorded game through the full point engine to fill in the per player numbers, so a box score is a product of these tables and nothing about them touches who wins. That gave me room to work on the statistics on their own.

Three of the four were a matter of moving numbers. I raised the ace rates, dropped the rate at which second serves land so more of them become faults, and increased the fraction of won rallies that finish as a clean winner.

The unforced errors needed a real change. The choice between a winner and an error was two constants applied to every player alike, so the only thing that moved a player's error total was how long the match ran. I rewrote the choice to read from the two players in the point. A heavier ground game produces more winners. A steadier opponent gives away fewer cheap points, so you have to hit through them. A server with low consistency misses more on his own serve. Once that was in, a player's consistency set his error count, and the spread I had been looking for appeared.

I ran a season and it still came across as random. The cause was that each statistic listened to exactly one attribute. Aces heard serve power against return, faults heard placement, winners heard the two ground strokes, errors heard consistency. Across sixty or so points in a match a single thin signal disappears under the noise, so a big server who happened to draw a good returner would post two aces and look like an accident. I gave each statistic a small group of attributes to read from. Aces lean on raw serve power with the return only partly pulling it back, so a genuine big server keeps producing them against anyone. Faults listen to placement and composure. Winners read the weapons, the court coverage that sets up a put away, and the willingness to go for it. Errors read consistency, shot tolerance, discipline, and movement. The correlation between each statistic and the talent behind it roughly doubled.

While doing that I found the deeper gap. The engine had never seen the detailed attributes at all. A player carries forty nine graded attributes, and at the boundary where a roster player becomes an engine player they were averaged down into nine drivers before a point was ever played. A standout first serve dissolved into a serve average, and whole attributes like court vision and discipline reached nothing. I carried the full attribute table onto the engine player and had the point tables read the specific attributes that belong to each outcome, keeping the old nine driver path as a fallback for the synthetic players the tests build.

That surfaced a calibration mistake I had been living with. I had tuned the talent swings around a midpoint of one half, on the assumption that an average player sat there. Real rosters sit higher. A first division roster centers well above that, so once the engine read true attribute values every strong player got pushed toward winners and away from errors, and the winner to error ratio ran past three to one. I added a reference point set to the real playing population and retuned against actual rosters. A player at the reference gets the baseline rates, a stronger one bends toward winners and aces, a weaker one toward errors, and the divisions separate on their own, the top division hitting through points and the lower ones grinding and missing.

Doubles needed the same work in two places. The four ratings that define a doubles player, the serve, the return, the net game, and the poach, were still reading the nine drivers, so the attributes that only matter in doubles, net play and volleys and poaching and the overhead, entered no doubles point. I pointed those ratings at the specific attributes, which flows into both the point simulation and the seeding. I also found that doubles computed its aces from its own stale constants and never called the shared serve model it imported, so I routed it through the same code with a single factor that keeps doubles aces rarer than singles. The net exchange still divided winners and errors by a fixed number, the same shape the singles engine had before, so I made it move with the finisher's weapons and net game and put the error on the less steady of the two losing partners.

With all of that done, a doubles pair's rating still tracked a player's singles overall almost perfectly, and that one lived in the generator. It built every player by drawing all forty nine attributes as independent noise around a single talent number, so each player was a smooth copy of his own average, with no real shape and no net specialist anywhere. The play style field sat on every player and drove nothing. I made it, together with a separate roll for net specialists, shift whole groups of related attributes at once, more at the net and less off the ground for one kind of player, the reverse for another. The shifts are normalized against the attribute weights so a player's overall grade stays where it was, which let me give players real profiles while leaving the talent and rating distribution the rest of the sim depends on exactly as it was. I checked that directly. Rating over several thousand roster players came out the same with the profiles as without, to the second decimal. What moved is the doubles order. A doubles lineup now pulls from a different ranking than the singles ladder, about a third of programs field a doubles player who sits outside their top six in singles, and a team's best doubles player is its singles number one only two thirds of the time.

None of this reads as a headline feature. The box score after a match now looks like the specific people who were on the court, a server who piles up aces, a grinder who almost never misses, a net player who earns a doubles seat above his singles rank, and the sim keeps its habit of surprising me about who wins.
